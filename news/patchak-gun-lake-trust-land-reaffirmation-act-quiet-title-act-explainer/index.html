Patchak and the Gun Lake Act: How Trust-Land Casino Challenges Work

Two Supreme Court rulings and an act of Congress over one Michigan casino still define the litigation risk on new tribal gaming land.

By James KingfisherPublished November 27, 20265 min read

When the Interior Department takes land into trust for a tribe that plans to build a casino, the decision can be challenged in federal court, sometimes years after the casino opens. The legal rules governing those challenges were reshaped by a single Michigan dispute: the Patchak litigation over the Gun Lake Casino, which reached the U.S. Supreme Court twice and prompted Congress to pass the Gun Lake Trust Land Reaffirmation Act. This explainer walks through what happened, what the courts decided and why the case still matters for tribal gaming projects that depend on newly acquired trust land.

Background: the Gun Lake trust acquisition

The Match-E-Be-Nash-She-Wish Band of Pottawatomi Indians, known as the Gun Lake Tribe, regained federal recognition in 1999. The tribe then asked the Secretary of the Interior to take about 147 acres in Wayland Township, Allegan County, Michigan, into trust under Section 5 of the Indian Reorganization Act of 1934, with the stated purpose of building a casino. Interior announced its decision to acquire the land in 2005.

A local opposition group challenged that decision under environmental and other theories and lost. Separately, in 2008, a nearby resident named David Patchak filed his own suit. Patchak argued that the Secretary lacked authority to take the land into trust because, he said, the tribe had not been "under federal jurisdiction" in 1934, the theory the Supreme Court would soon adopt in Carcieri v. Salazar (2009). Patchak's claimed injury was the effect a casino would have on his rural community. Interior took the land into trust in early 2009, and Gun Lake Casino opened in February 2011 while Patchak's suit was still in court. The casino has since become a major regional employer and a significant source of revenue-sharing payments to Michigan and local governments, as described in our report on Gun Lake Casino's 15 years of revenue sharing.

Patchak I: the Quiet Title Act question

The government and the tribe argued that Patchak's suit was barred by the Quiet Title Act, a federal statute that waives the United States' sovereign immunity for suits to resolve disputed title to land but expressly does not apply to "trust or restricted Indian lands." Once land is in trust, they argued, no one can sue to undo the acquisition.

In Match-E-Be-Nash-She-Wish Band of Pottawatomi Indians v. Patchak (2012), the Supreme Court disagreed by an 8–1 vote. Writing for the majority, Justice Elena Kagan held that the Quiet Title Act covers suits in which the plaintiff claims an interest in the land itself. Patchak claimed no ownership interest. He was challenging the legality of the Secretary's decision under the Administrative Procedure Act, which carries its own waiver of sovereign immunity. The Court also held that Patchak, as a neighbor objecting to the land's use for gaming, fell within the "zone of interests" protected by the Indian Reorganization Act and could bring the claim. Justice Sonia Sotomayor dissented, warning that the ruling would expose trust acquisitions to prolonged uncertainty.

The practical holding of Patchak I was that the transfer of title into trust does not end the litigation. A neighbor can still ask a court to declare the acquisition unlawful.

Congress responds: the Gun Lake Trust Land Reaffirmation Act

With a casino already operating on the land, Congress intervened. The Gun Lake Trust Land Reaffirmation Act, signed in September 2014, did two things. It ratified and confirmed the land's trust status, and it provided that any action relating to the land "shall not be filed or maintained in a Federal court and shall be promptly dismissed." Congress has passed many land-specific statutes for tribal gaming sites, a practice examined in our explainer on congressional land-into-trust acts. The Gun Lake Act was notable because it targeted pending litigation directly.

Patchak II: separation of powers

Patchak then argued that the Act was unconstitutional because Congress had effectively directed the outcome of a specific case, intruding on the judicial power under Article III. In Patchak v. Zinke (2018), the Supreme Court rejected that challenge, 6–3, though without a single majority rationale. A four-justice plurality led by Justice Clarence Thomas concluded that Congress had changed the law governing a category of cases, which it may do, rather than dictating a result under existing law. Justices Ruth Bader Ginsburg and Sotomayor concurred in the judgment on the ground that the Act restored the federal government's sovereign immunity, which Congress may withdraw at any time. Chief Justice John Roberts, joined by Justices Anthony Kennedy and Neil Gorsuch, dissented, arguing that the Act did exactly what the separation of powers forbids. The fractured result left the Gun Lake land secure but provided limited guidance for future cases.

Why Patchak still matters for tribal gaming

The cases changed practice in several ways. After Patchak I, Interior revised its land acquisition regulations in 2013 to drop a 30-day waiting period that had been used to allow challenges before title transferred. Because title transfer no longer ends litigation, Interior now finalizes decisions promptly and leaves challengers to sue under the Administrative Procedure Act, generally within its six-year statute of limitations. For tribes, that means a trust acquisition for gaming can remain open to suit for years, even after construction.

That exposure is now a standard part of financing and planning. Lenders and developers assess litigation risk on new trust land, and several recent off-reservation projects have faced APA challenges from neighbors, competing tribes or local governments, as discussed in our coverage of fee-to-trust reversals and off-reservation risk. The legal questions in those suits, including the Carcieri "under federal jurisdiction" test and IGRA's restrictions on gaming on land acquired after 1988, are often the same ones Patchak raised.

For a broader introduction to the federal framework, see our Legal Guide to IGRA and Class III gaming. The Gun Lake story ended well for the tribe, but it took a decade, two Supreme Court decisions and an act of Congress to get there. Few tribes can count on that kind of legislative backstop, which is why Patchak's lesson about lasting litigation exposure still shapes how tribal casino land is acquired and defended.
