Mille Lacs Band Enterprise Profile: Inside Minnesota's Grand Casinos in 2026

Two flagship casinos, a half-billion-dollar enterprise, and a diversification push define the Mille Lacs Band's place in Minnesota gaming.

By Sarah BegayPublished August 12, 20264 min read

Among Minnesota's eleven federally recognized tribal nations, the Mille Lacs Band of Ojibwe occupies a distinctive position: it built one of the state's earliest and largest gaming enterprises, and it has spent the years since trying to make that enterprise less dependent on gaming alone. The Band's two flagship properties, Grand Casino Mille Lacs and Grand Casino Hinckley, together form a gaming operation approaching half a billion dollars in annual revenue and serve as the financial engine behind a broad slate of tribal government programs. For anyone studying how a mid-sized tribal operator sustains itself in a competitive regional market, the Mille Lacs Band offers a clear case study.

Two casinos, one enterprise

Grand Casino Mille Lacs, on the shore of Lake Mille Lacs near Onamia, and Grand Casino Hinckley, positioned along Interstate 35 roughly midway between the Twin Cities and Duluth, anchor the Band's gaming portfolio. Recent estimates put Grand Casino Mille Lacs' annual revenue around $450 million, a figure that reflects the properties' scale as full-service destinations with hotels, event centers, dining, and golf rather than standalone gaming halls. The Hinckley property in particular benefits from its highway location, drawing traffic moving between two major population centers — a geographic advantage that has made it a durable performer.

Like most tribal operators, the Mille Lacs Band runs its casinos under Class III compacts with the state, and gaming revenue flows into essential government services: health care, education, housing, elder support, and infrastructure across the Band's districts. That connection between the casino floor and tribal governance is the through-line of the entire enterprise, and it is the reason gaming performance is treated less as a profit metric than as a public-finance one. A push beyond the floor

What sets the Mille Lacs Band apart from many peers is the deliberate effort to diversify. Through its corporate ventures arm, the Band has invested in hospitality and non-gaming businesses designed to generate revenue that does not rise and fall with the casino floor. That strategy mirrors a broader movement among established tribal operators to build resilience against gaming saturation, and it reflects a hard-learned lesson from earlier decades: a tribal economy anchored to a single casino is exposed to competition, regulation, and shifting consumer habits in ways a diversified one is not.

The Band has also continued to invest in the guest experience at its existing properties. In August 2026, Grand Casino Mille Lacs and Grand Casino Hinckley moved to upgrade their guest-service operations through a new hospitality-technology partnership aimed at improving service consistency and driving repeat visitation — a modest but telling signal that the enterprise is competing on experience, not just square footage. In a mature market, retention economics increasingly outweigh acquisition.

For the Mille Lacs Band, gaming is the engine, but diversification is the insurance policy — and both are managed with the long horizon of tribal government in mind.

Position in the Minnesota market

Minnesota's tribal gaming sector is large, fragmented across eleven nations, and economically significant statewide, contributing well over a billion dollars in annual economic impact and tens of thousands of jobs — figures detailed in our Minnesota economic impact study coverage. Within that landscape, the Mille Lacs Band sits among the larger operators, though it competes for the Twin Cities market with properties run by the Shakopee Mdewakanton Sioux Community and others. For the wider competitive picture, our Minnesota market deep-dive maps how the state's operators stack up.

The scale of the enterprise is best understood in human terms rather than purely financial ones. The Band's casinos are among the larger employers in their rural regions of central and east-central Minnesota, and the payroll, purchasing, and tourism they generate ripple through surrounding communities that have few comparable economic anchors. That local weight is a defining feature of tribal gaming generally: unlike a commercial operator answerable to shareholders, a tribal enterprise measures success partly by how many jobs it sustains and how many government services it underwrites. For the Mille Lacs Band, the two Grand Casinos are simultaneously businesses, employers, and instruments of self-determination — a combination that shapes every major decision about expansion, reinvestment, and risk.

One structural feature shapes the Band's outlook as much as any business decision: Minnesota has repeatedly debated but not yet authorized mobile sports betting, and the tribes have insisted that any framework preserve their gaming role. For the Mille Lacs Band, the status quo protects an in-person model built over three decades; a future online framework would be both an opportunity and a disruption. Until that debate resolves, the Band's strategy remains what it has been: run two strong destination casinos efficiently, reinvest in the guest experience, and keep building the non-gaming businesses that will matter most whenever the next competitive shock arrives.
