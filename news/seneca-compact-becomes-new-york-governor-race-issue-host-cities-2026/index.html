Seneca Gaming Compact Dispute Enters New York Governor's Race

With Election Day weeks away, Seneca and the governor describe the state of compact talks differently, and Buffalo, Niagara Falls and Salamanca are waiting on the outcome.

By Elena RuizPublished October 17, 20264 min read

The unresolved Seneca Nation gaming compact has moved from the negotiating table into New York's governor's race. In the first week of October 2026, the Seneca Nation said negotiators had reached an agreement in principle on a new Class III compact that contains no revenue-sharing provision, while Gov. Kathy Hochul said that no agreement exists and that talks are ongoing. The competing statements, made less than a month before Election Day, leave one of the largest tribal-state relationships in the Northeast without a settled framework.

The dispute is not new. The original 2002 compact, which expired in 2023, required the Nation to share a portion of slot-machine revenue with the state, and a share of that money flowed to the host cities of Buffalo, Niagara Falls and Salamanca. What changed in early October is that the question of what replaces that arrangement became a campaign issue as well as a legal and fiscal one.

What each side said

According to reporting by Spectrum News, Seneca Nation President J.C. Seneca announced on a Friday that negotiators had reached an agreement in principle with no revenue-sharing component, and he indicated he expects a finished document to go to the Legislature in the spring. Any compact would still require legislative approval before it could take effect, and federal review under the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act would follow. In the Nation's framing, giving up revenue sharing is tied to stronger market protections and broader economic opportunities in exchange.

The governor's office has described the situation differently. Gov. Hochul stated publicly that there is currently no agreement and that negotiations are ongoing. She also said the state would continue to financially support Niagara Falls, Buffalo and Salamanca so that the cities are not harmed by changes to the compact. The two statements are not necessarily irreconcilable, since an agreement in principle between negotiators is not a signed compact, but they point to a real gap in how each party characterizes the state of play.

The campaign overlay

The following Monday, the Seneca Nation's president hosted Republican gubernatorial candidate Bruce Blakeman for a fundraiser at the Nation's casino in Niagara Falls. Blakeman had criticized the governor earlier that day, saying the state had not been a willing negotiating partner. Seneca citizens protested outside the event. One protester objected to Blakeman's support for prediction markets, which she argued could erode both tribal and state gaming revenue, and former Seneca Nation President Mike Schindler, who joined the protest, said the state and the Nation should return to negotiations.

It is worth keeping the facts of the fundraiser separate from the substance of the compact. Public reporting does not indicate that the candidate has taken a position on a compact without revenue sharing, and a campaign event at a tribal enterprise is not an endorsement of any particular term. What the episode does show is that the compact has become politically salient in a statewide race, which can both accelerate and complicate a negotiation.

Why the host cities are watching

For the three host cities, the stakes are concrete. Salamanca's mayor has said casino revenue accounts for about 30 percent of the city's operating budget and warned that losing it could mean layoffs. State legislation directs the three cities to allocate funding to community stakeholders, including roughly $750,000 for the Niagara Falls City School District. The state's pledge to keep supporting the cities is meant to address precisely this exposure, though its form and duration have not been specified publicly.

Revenue sharing in tribal-state compacts is generally a negotiated exchange: a state may receive a percentage of net slot revenue in return for meaningful exclusivity or other benefits. Our explainer on revenue sharing in tribal-state compacts walks through how those payments are structured, and our earlier report on the Seneca agreement in principle and the host cities covers the initial announcement. Because IGRA limits what states may demand, any payment must be tied to a substantial economic benefit to the tribe, which is part of why the Nation is asking what it would receive in return.

What to watch next

Several milestones will indicate whether the agreement in principle becomes a compact. The first is the release of actual text; until a document is public, descriptions from either side remain characterizations. The second is whether the state and the Nation align on the host-city question, since a compact that eliminates revenue sharing will force the Legislature to decide how, or whether, to replace the funds. The third is the election itself, because the next governor will inherit whatever process is under way. For broader context on how the compact fits into the state's market, see our analysis of what is at stake in New York and the Legal Guide to IGRA and Class III gaming.

For now, the accurate summary is narrow: the Seneca Nation says it has an agreement in principle without revenue sharing, the governor says there is no agreement, the Legislature has not seen a compact, and three upstate cities are waiting to learn what happens to a meaningful portion of their budgets. TribalGaming.com will report on the published text and any legislative action as they occur.

It is also worth noting what the dispute does not yet involve. No compact text has been published, no bill has been introduced, and no federal review has begun. Until those steps occur, projections about host-city budgets or the Nation's payments are conditional. Readers should treat claims from any quarter, including campaigns, advocates and officials, as positions in an ongoing negotiation rather than as final terms.
