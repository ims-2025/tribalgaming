Banked vs. Non-Banked Card Games: Where IGRA Draws the Class III Line

A single question, whether players compete against the house or against each other, decides which regulatory track a tribal card game follows.

By Daniel YazziePublished November 11, 20264 min read

News coverage of tribal gaming often refers to "house-banked" table games as shorthand for Class III gaming. The phrase carries real legal weight. Under the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act, the difference between banked vs. non-banked card games determines whether a game can be offered under a tribe's own regulatory authority with federal oversight, or only under a tribal-state compact. This explainer sets out where the line falls, why it exists and how state law shapes the outcome.

What makes a card game "banked"

In a banked game, the house, or a designated bank, takes a position against every player. The player wins or loses against the bank, and the bank pays winning wagers from its own funds. Blackjack and baccarat are the standard examples: each hand pits the player against the dealer, who represents the house.

In a non-banked game, players compete against one another. The house provides the table, the dealer and the security, and earns money through a fee, typically a rake taken from each pot or a time charge for the seat. Poker in its common forms, such as Texas Hold'em and Omaha, is the clearest example. The house has no stake in which player wins the hand.

The distinction matters economically as well as legally. A banked game gives the house a mathematical edge on every wager, which produces revenue that scales with volume. A non-banked game yields a more modest service fee, which is one reason poker rooms are often seen as amenities rather than primary profit centers.

How IGRA classifies card games

IGRA divides gaming into three classes, an overview of which appears in our Class II vs. Class III explainer. Class II includes bingo and related games, and also certain card games. To qualify, a card game must either be explicitly authorized by state law or not explicitly prohibited by state law and played legally somewhere in the state. It must also be played in conformity with any state rules on hours of operation and limits on wagers or pot sizes.

The statute then carves out an exclusion. Class II does not include banking card games, and IGRA names baccarat, chemin de fer and blackjack as examples. Those games fall into Class III, alongside slot machines and other casino games, which means a tribe can offer them only under a compact with the state that has been approved by the Interior Department. The full compacting framework is summarized in the Legal Guide to IGRA and Class III.

In practice: blackjack requires a compact. Poker can be Class II, but only if state law allows it and only within the state's limits on hours and stakes.

Congress also included a narrow grandfather provision for card games that certain tribes in Michigan, North Dakota, South Dakota and Washington were operating on or before May 1, 1988. That provision is rarely relevant today because most of the affected tribes now operate under compacts, but it explains some historical anomalies in those states.

Why state law still matters for Class II card games

Because Class II card games must conform to state law, a tribe's poker room in one state may operate under different conditions than one across the border. If a state permits charitable poker with low betting caps, those caps may limit what a tribe can offer as Class II. If a state prohibits poker entirely, a tribe generally cannot offer it under Class II, though it may negotiate for it in a compact. Our explainer on tribal poker rooms and Class II classification works through several examples.

Class II status still involves regulation. Tribes regulate Class II gaming through their gaming commissions, with oversight from the National Indian Gaming Commission, and the NIGC's minimum internal control standards apply. What Class II status removes is the need for state consent through a compact.

Player-banked and hybrid formats

Some games blur the line. In player-banked formats, the role of the bank rotates among players at the table rather than resting with the house. In some jurisdictions, commercial card rooms have used these structures, sometimes with third-party companies acting as the player-dealer, to offer games resembling blackjack or baccarat while arguing they are not house-banked. Tribes in California, where the state constitution grants tribes the right to operate banking and percentage card games under compacts, have challenged those arrangements as infringing on that exclusivity. The dispute illustrates how much commercial value rides on the banked/non-banked definition. Our California state hub tracks the tribal properties involved.

Electronic formats raise related questions. Electronic table games that replicate blackjack or roulette are generally treated as Class III when they are banked against the house, a point covered in our electronic table games explainer.

Why the distinction comes up in current policy debates

The banked/non-banked line appears whenever a state considers expanding gaming. A state that authorizes casino games for commercial operators may change what tribes can negotiate in compacts. A state that has never allowed house-banked games, such as Texas, offers tribes no Class III option at all until that changes. Understanding which side of the line a game falls on is often the first step in understanding who can offer it, under whose rules and with whose consent.
