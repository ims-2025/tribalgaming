Idaho Casino History: Why the I-84 Tribal Proposals Face a Long Road

Two tribal casino proposals near Boise arrive in a state whose gambling law was set decades ago and whose approval path has three distinct steps.

By Linda CardinalPublished October 24, 20266 min read

Two tribal casino proposals along Interstate 84 in southwestern Idaho have drawn attention this fall, and the Idaho tribal casino debate is easier to follow with a little history. According to reporting by BoiseDev published in October 2026, one project is backed by the Shoshone-Paiute and Coeur d'Alene Tribes near Simco Road, and the other by the Shoshone-Bannock Tribes near Mountain Home. Both would depend on federal approvals and on a state process that is unusual in its detail. The state's gambling past explains why.

How Idaho arrived at its current gaming map

BoiseDev's background reporting notes that Boise banned gambling in 1949, after which gaming activity moved to Garden City. A 1953 Idaho Supreme Court decision then closed loopholes that had allowed non-tribal casinos to operate. Casino gaming did not disappear from the region; Cactus Pete's opened across the border in Jackpot, Nevada, in 1954. In the decades that followed, the state's casino activity was concentrated on tribal land. The Coeur d'Alene Bingo Hall opened in 1993 and has since expanded into a resort casino, and the Shoshone-Bannock Casino Hotel operates in Fort Hall.

The result is a state with no commercial casinos and a tribal gaming sector that exists within a narrow legal channel. Our earlier analysis of Idaho's lottery-machine model and constitutional limits describes how that channel works for the machines tribes currently operate, and why the state's constitution is central to it.

The two proposals

The Shoshone-Paiute and Coeur d'Alene project involves about 557 acres along I-84 at Simco Road, less than 20 minutes from Boise by car. A fee-to-trust application was submitted to the U.S. Department of the Interior in September 2025. BoiseDev reports that a National Environmental Policy Act review is expected to open for public comment in the coming months, and that a spokesperson said gaming fee-to-trust reviews have historically taken 18 to 36 months or longer. New renderings show a six-story glass-clad hotel. A developer, Ahlquist, is working with the Coeur d'Alene Tribe on a mixed-use project next to the planned casino resort. We covered the review stage in our report on the Shoshone-Paiute and Coeur d'Alene fee-to-trust review.

The Shoshone-Bannock Tribes, based in Fort Hall, propose a casino on about 157 acres near Mountain Home that they bought in 2020. It would be the tribe's fourth casino and its first off tribal lands. The concept described includes a $311 million complex of roughly 500,000 square feet, with about 2,000 electronic gaming machines, a 250-room hotel, six restaurants, an event center, two movie theaters, an arcade and a horse track with a grandstand. The application was submitted to Interior in 2023, and the tribe says work continues behind the scenes. More detail is in our report on the Shoshone-Bannock Mountain Home project. BoiseDev also reports that Shoshone-Paiute Chairman Brian Mason said his tribe rejected an offer from the Shoshone-Bannock to collaborate.

A three-step approval path

Because both sites lie off existing reservations, the process runs through the federal two-part determination that IGRA reserves for such cases, followed by state concurrence and compacting. A spokesperson for the governor's office, identified in the BoiseDev report as Emily Callihan, outlined the sequence: the Secretary of the Interior must determine that the casino is in the tribe's best interest and not detrimental to the surrounding community; the governor must concur in that determination; and the tribe and state then negotiate a compact, which the Secretary must approve before construction begins.

That sequence matches the framework described in our explainer on governor concurrence and the two-part determination. The governor's role is the distinctive feature: unlike most steps in the federal trust process, concurrence is a discretionary state decision that can end a project. That is one reason the Idaho history matters. A state that has kept commercial casinos out for roughly 70 years may weigh any new off-reservation facility, tribal or not, against a long-standing public policy.

What can be said, and what cannot

Several things remain uncertain. Neither project has completed the federal environmental review, so the Interior determination is not imminent. The governor's position on concurrence has not been reported beyond the description of the process. And the existence of two competing proposals in the same corridor raises a question that law does not answer directly: whether the market, the state or both would support more than one. Our analysis of the I-84 corridor and governor concurrence addresses the process side; this article adds only the historical frame.

The timeline also deserves emphasis. If a gaming fee-to-trust review takes 18 to 36 months or longer, as described, then even a smooth process would leave any opening years away, and the Shoshone-Bannock application, filed in 2023, has already been pending for some time. Planning assumptions about jobs, revenue and competition should therefore be treated as projections that depend on decisions not yet made.
