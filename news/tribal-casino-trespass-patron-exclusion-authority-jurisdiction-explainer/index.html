Tribal Casino Trespass and Patron Exclusion: How It Works

How tribes ban patrons, who enforces the ban, and why Oliphant and Public Law 280 shape what happens when someone returns.

By Sarah BegayPublished October 26, 20264 min read

Every tribal casino has to answer a practical question: what happens when a patron must be removed or barred from the property? The answer draws on tribal sovereignty, the gaming ordinance, the tribe's regulatory and security structure, and the limits federal law places on tribal jurisdiction over non-Indians. This explainer describes how tribal casino trespass and exclusion authority generally works. Specific rules differ by tribe, so readers should treat it as a general framework rather than legal advice.

The source of authority: the power to exclude

A tribe's ability to decide who may enter and remain on tribal land is among the oldest attributes of tribal sovereignty. The Supreme Court described the power to exclude as an inherent attribute of tribal sovereignty in Merrion v. Jicarilla Apache Tribe (1982), and it underlies a casino's right to refuse service and to bar individuals. In practice, casinos are private-facing businesses owned by tribal governments, and like any gaming operator they can decline to serve patrons, but the tribal government's authority gives that decision a legal footing in tribal law.

That authority is typically exercised through tribal codes. Many tribes have adopted trespass or exclusion ordinances, and gaming ordinances commonly authorize the casino or the tribal gaming regulatory authority to remove or bar persons for cheating, disorderly conduct, violations of casino rules, or other reasons. Our explainer on the tribal gaming ordinance and its NIGC approval describes the document in which these provisions often appear.

Who does what inside the casino

Day-to-day exclusion decisions usually involve three layers. First, casino security and surveillance identify the problem, whether it is suspected cheating, intoxication, or a safety incident. Surveillance systems are a core compliance tool, as explained in our piece on surveillance and minimum internal control standards. Second, management issues the trespass or exclusion notice, often in writing, which tells the person they are no longer welcome and that returning may result in enforcement. Third, the tribal gaming regulatory authority may review or impose exclusions where the conduct implicates gaming integrity. The authority's functions are covered in our TGRA explainer.

Exclusion also overlaps with player protection. Voluntary self-exclusion programs let a patron ask to be barred from a property, usually for a defined period, and operators are expected to honor and enforce those requests. This is a distinct mechanism from an operator-initiated ban, but it relies on the same ability to identify and remove a person. Details are in our explainer on self-exclusion programs and responsible gaming.

The jurisdiction problem: non-Indians and enforcement

Removing a person is one thing; enforcing a ban against someone who returns is another. In Oliphant v. Suquamish Indian Tribe (1978), the Supreme Court held that tribes generally lack inherent criminal jurisdiction over non-Indians. Congress has since restored limited tribal authority in specific areas, notably certain domestic violence-related crimes through the Violence Against Women Act reauthorizations, but that authority is narrow and does not cover ordinary trespass by a non-Indian visitor.

As a result, enforcement frequently depends on who has jurisdiction where the casino sits. In states covered by Public Law 280, state authorities may have criminal jurisdiction over offenses on tribal land, and a returning trespasser may be arrested by state or county officers. Elsewhere, enforcement may involve federal authorities or cross-deputization and cooperation agreements between the tribe and local law enforcement. Our explainers on Public Law 280 and who polices crime at tribal casinos and on criminal jurisdiction and law enforcement go deeper. For intergovernmental arrangements that support these functions, see intergovernmental agreements with host communities.

Civil remedies are also possible. A tribe may pursue a civil trespass matter in tribal court, although civil jurisdiction over non-Indians has its own limits, and the framework for patron claims against the casino is a separate question governed by sovereign immunity, which we address in patron disputes and sovereign immunity.

What patrons should know

For visitors, the practical points are straightforward. A casino can ask a patron to leave and can bar a patron from returning. A written notice typically describes the scope and duration of the ban. Patrons who believe an exclusion was wrongful generally must use the tribe's internal processes or tribal court rather than state courts, a consequence of sovereign immunity and the tribe's authority over its own land. Appeals processes vary, and some tribes publish patron dispute procedures.

It is also worth noting that exclusion is distinct from the age and conduct rules that apply to everyone. Who sets the minimum gambling age is addressed in our explainer on the minimum gambling age under IGRA, and the broader regulatory structure appears in who regulates tribal casinos.

The central point is that tribal casino exclusion rests on strong sovereign authority over land and operations but runs into real limits when enforcement requires criminal jurisdiction over non-Indians. Most casinos manage that tension through clear written notices, coordination with local law enforcement, and consistent internal procedures.
