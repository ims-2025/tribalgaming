Graton Resort Expansion Adds About 220 Hotel Rooms and Pool in 2027

After a 144,000-square-foot gaming expansion in May, Graton Rancheria turns to overnight capacity in a crowded Sonoma County market.

By Elena RuizPublished November 18, 20264 min read

The Federated Indians of Graton Rancheria are moving into the next stage of the Graton Resort expansion, with roughly 220 additional hotel rooms and a new adults-only pool complex scheduled for 2027 at the tribe's property in Rohnert Park, Sonoma County. The hotel phase follows the May 2026 opening of a major gaming expansion and keeps the resort on track with a multi-year build-out that the tribe has described as a roughly $1 billion investment.

The 2027 phase is less dramatic than this spring's gaming floor opening, but it addresses the constraint that most limits a destination casino in a drive-in market: the number of guests who can stay overnight. It also arrives while the tribe remains a party to litigation over a competing casino proposal in the same county, a dispute that is still awaiting a federal appellate ruling.

What the Graton Resort expansion adds in 2027

According to published expansion materials, the next phase centers on approximately 220 new hotel rooms. Alongside them, the resort plans an adults-only pool with a pool bar, cabanas and dedicated lounge space, an amenity aimed at extending length of stay and attracting non-gaming spend from weekend and leisure visitors. Construction schedules, as with any project of this size, remain subject to change, and the tribe has not published a precise opening date for the rooms.

Longer-term plans go further. The project's environmental review covers a proposed 3,500-seat theater, which would give the resort a dedicated live entertainment venue capable of hosting touring acts. No construction start or opening date has been announced for the theater, and it should be read as an approved option rather than a scheduled phase.

The hotel work builds on the expansion that opened on May 4, 2026, which added about 144,000 square feet of gaming space, roughly 2,000 slot machines and four new dining venues. We covered that opening in detail in our report on phase one of the Graton build-out. Taken together, the sequence follows a familiar pattern in California tribal gaming: expand the gaming floor first, where returns are fastest, then add the rooms and amenities that convert a regional casino into a resort.

Why hotel capacity matters in Sonoma County

Graton opened in November 2013 as the Bay Area's closest large tribal casino to San Francisco and Oakland, about an hour's drive north. Its customer base has always been heavily local and regional, which makes day-trip visitation the core of the business. Hotel rooms change that equation. Overnight guests tend to spend more on dining and entertainment, generate midweek occupancy that smooths revenue, and make it easier to book group and convention business.

The timing also reflects competitive pressure. Sonoma County now hosts several tribal gaming projects within a short drive of one another, a concentration we examined in our analysis of the Sonoma County tribal casino cluster. In a market where multiple tribal operators are investing at the same time, incumbents that add amenities ahead of new entrants can lock in loyalty program members and convention calendars before alternatives arrive.

California's compact framework shapes how that competition plays out. Tribes there operate Class III gaming under individual tribal-state compacts, and newer compacts have shifted away from the large revenue-sharing payments that characterized earlier agreements. The California state hub tracks compacts, properties and operators across the state, including the North Bay.

The Koi Nation appeal in the background

The expansion is proceeding while the future of a nearby competitor remains unresolved. The Koi Nation of Northern California has sought to build the Shiloh Resort and Casino on land near Windsor. A federal district court set aside the Interior Department's decision to take that land into trust for gaming, and the property was removed from trust in March 2026. The Koi Nation appealed, and the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit heard oral argument on September 1, 2026. Graton Rancheria is among the parties that challenged the original federal approval.

As of late October, the Ninth Circuit had not issued a decision. Our coverage of the Ninth Circuit oral argument in the Graton and Koi Nation case outlines the arguments the panel heard and the paths the case could take from here.

For Graton, the practical point is that its 2027 investments do not depend on the appeal's outcome. The rooms, pool and long-term theater option are being built for a market the tribe already serves.

An appellate ruling in either direction would still matter. If the district court decision stands, the Shiloh project would face a long path back through federal review. If it is reversed, a new resort could eventually open a short drive from Rohnert Park, and Graton's earlier investment in rooms and amenities would become its principal competitive response.

What to watch

Three markers will indicate how the next phase is progressing. The first is a firm opening date for the new hotel rooms, which would allow the resort to begin selling group business for late 2027 and 2028. The second is any move to schedule the 3,500-seat theater, which would signal confidence in sustained demand for live entertainment in the North Bay. The third is the Ninth Circuit's decision in the Koi Nation appeal, which will determine whether Sonoma County's tribal gaming map is close to settled or still in motion.

For now, Graton is following the most conventional strategy available to an established tribal operator in a contested market: keep building on land it already has, and make the property harder to replace before anyone else can build.
