Tribal Enrollment and Per Capita Payments: Why Gaming Revenue Raises the Stakes

Membership is a sovereign decision, but distributions are governed by IGRA. How the two meet in practice.

By Elena RuizPublished October 25, 20264 min read

Tribal gaming revenue is, in most cases, the single largest source of funding for the governments that own the casinos. That fact is why tribal enrollment and per capita payments sit at the center of some of the most sensitive governance questions in Indian Country. Who counts as a citizen of a tribal nation, and who decides, is a question of sovereignty long before it is a question of money. But once a gaming operation produces significant distributions, the stakes of those decisions can rise considerably. This analysis looks at why tribal enrollment and gaming revenue are linked, what the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act does and does not say about the subject, and why evenhanded observers treat the topic with care.

Enrollment is a sovereign decision

Federally recognized tribes determine their own membership. That principle is long established in federal law and in the practice of tribal governments, which set criteria in constitutions, enrollment ordinances or other governing documents. Common criteria include descent from persons on a historical base roll, a minimum blood quantum, lineal descent, residency or community ties, or some combination. These rules differ widely from nation to nation, and there is no uniform national standard.

Because enrollment criteria are an exercise of self-government, courts have generally been reluctant to intervene in membership disputes, and the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act does not regulate who a tribe enrolls. The National Indian Gaming Commission oversees gaming operations, not tribal citizenship. For a broader view of the regulatory architecture, see our explainer on who regulates tribal casinos.

Where IGRA does touch distributions

IGRA addresses the use of gaming revenue rather than membership. Net revenues from Class II and Class III gaming may be used only for specified purposes: to fund tribal government operations or programs, to provide for the general welfare of the tribe and its members, to promote tribal economic development, to donate to charitable organizations, and to help fund local government agencies. We discuss each category in our explainer on the five permitted uses of net gaming revenue.

A tribe may distribute a share of net revenue directly to members as per capita payments only if it has a revenue allocation plan approved by the Secretary of the Interior, and the plan must provide for reserved funds for the other permitted purposes before distributions are made. Per capita payments are also subject to federal tax obligations for recipients, and special protections apply to payments made to minors and legally incompetent adults, a topic covered in our guide to minors' trusts and per capita taxation. Our broader introduction is in the revenue allocation plan explainer.

Why the link raises the stakes

When a casino generates meaningful distributions, membership status can carry material value in addition to its cultural and political meaning. Citizens receive voting rights, access to services, and in many nations a share of distributions. That combination can intensify disputes over eligibility and, in some communities, produce contested decisions about who belongs. Analysts, tribal leaders and scholars debate how large a role money plays in such disputes and how often it is the primary driver. The evidence varies by community, and generalizations tend to be unreliable. What can be said is that financial stakes are one factor among several, alongside historical, familial and political ones.

Tribes also differ sharply in how they use gaming revenue. Some distribute substantial per capita payments; others direct nearly all net revenue to government services, health care, education, housing and infrastructure. Our analysis of reinvestment versus per capita allocation examines that tradeoff, and our review of how gaming revenue funds tribal health care shows how large the non-distribution uses can be.

Governance safeguards and transparency

Because enrollment is a sovereign function, safeguards come primarily from within tribal governance. Written enrollment ordinances, defined appeals processes, independent enrollment committees and tribal courts can add predictability and fairness. Clear separation between the body that sets enrollment criteria and the body that manages gaming distributions can reduce perceived conflicts of interest. Tribal-state compacts and tribal ordinances may also address specific topics, though they vary. Our explainer on gaming enterprise structure describes one way tribes separate business operations from government.

Financial transparency matters as well. Annual independent audits are required of Class II and Class III operations under federal regulations, as described in our piece on the annual audit requirement, though the audits go to regulators and tribal leadership rather than the public. We have previously examined the limits of public disclosure in our analysis of the transparency gap in tribal gaming finance.

What this means for readers

For industry observers, journalists and policymakers, the key point is that enrollment and distributions are matters for tribal self-government and that federal law constrains the use of gaming revenue more than it constrains membership. For members, the practical protections are those their own constitutions, ordinances and courts provide. And for anyone evaluating a tribal enterprise, it is useful to remember that the same casino may fund very different mixes of services, reserves and distributions depending on the nation that owns it.
