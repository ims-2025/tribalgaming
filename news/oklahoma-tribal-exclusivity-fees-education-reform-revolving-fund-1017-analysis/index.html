Where Oklahoma Tribal Exclusivity Fees Go: The 1017 Education Fund Share

The statute is clear about the 88 percent. What that money does inside a $1.26 billion education fund is a harder, and more interesting, question.

By James KingfisherPublished November 11, 20264 min read

A recurring claim in Oklahoma politics is that tribal casinos fund the state's schools. The question resurfaced this week when Oklahoma Watch examined whether millions of dollars in education funding really come from tribal gaming. The underlying mechanics are set out in state law and in the state's own compliance reports, and they show that Oklahoma tribal exclusivity fees are, by statute, mostly an education revenue stream. What they show less clearly is how much that stream changes total school funding, which depends on budget decisions the statute does not control.

How the exclusivity fee is calculated

Under the Model Tribal Gaming Compact, the template that Oklahoma's compacted tribes signed under the 2004 State-Tribal Gaming Act, tribes pay the state a fee in exchange for exclusive rights to offer covered Class III games. According to the Office of Management and Enterprise Services (OMES), which administers the payments, the rate on electronic covered games is 4 percent of the first $10 million of annual adjusted gross revenue, 5 percent of the next $10 million, and 6 percent of revenue above $20 million. Table games carry a fee of 10 percent of monthly net win. Our model compact explainer covers the structure and its renewal terms in detail.

The totals have grown steadily. The OMES gaming compliance annual report for fiscal year 2024 recorded $210.2 million in exclusivity fees, up about 3.9 percent from $202.3 million in FY2023 and well above the $123.6 million collected in pandemic-affected FY2020. The report says fees have risen every year since 2005 except FY2014 and FY2020. For FY2024, electronic games produced $181.8 million in fees and table games $28.4 million, from 33 compacted tribes operating 137 facilities with an average of about 46,525 Class III machines a month. The Oklahoma Indian Gaming Association has cited a figure of more than $221 million for the 2025 budget year, a number we examined in our report on exclusivity fees and prediction markets.

What the statute says about the 1017 fund

The distribution rule appears in Title 3A, Section 280 of the Oklahoma Statutes. Since July 1, 2008, 12 percent of fees received under the compact's fee provision go to the General Revenue Fund and 88 percent to the Education Reform Revolving Fund, widely known as the 1017 Fund after House Bill 1017, the Education Reform Act of 1990. Before that split applies, the first $20,833.33 received each month, or $250,000 a year, is transferred to the Department of Mental Health and Substance Abuse Services for compulsive gambling treatment and related education.

FY2024 distribution: $184.8 million to the 1017 Fund, $25.2 million to the General Revenue Fund and $250,000 to the Department of Mental Health and Substance Abuse Services.

On the narrow factual question, then, the answer is yes: well over $150 million a year in tribal exclusivity fees is deposited into a fund dedicated to education, and that figure has risen in most years since the compacts took effect.

How large is the tribal share?

Context comes from the size of the receiving fund. According to the Oklahoma Policy Institute, $1.263 billion was appropriated from the 1017 Fund in FY2026, about 31.7 percent of total state appropriations to the Department of Education. The fund's original sources were income, sales and use taxes tied to House Bill 1017; tobacco taxes and a portion of tribal and horse-track gaming revenue were added later.

Comparing adjacent years carries some imprecision, but the order of magnitude is clear. If 88 percent of the reported FY2025 total of roughly $221 million went to the fund, the deposit would be about $195 million, or roughly 15 percent of the FY2026 appropriation. Tribal exclusivity fees are therefore a meaningful contributor to the 1017 Fund, but not its largest source, and a much smaller share of total state spending on schools.

Dedicated revenue and fungibility

The harder question is whether those deposits increase education funding or replace dollars that would otherwise come from general revenue. Dedicated funds are a budget-labeling tool. When lawmakers write the annual education appropriation, they can draw on the 1017 Fund and the General Revenue Fund in different proportions, so a rise in exclusivity fees does not automatically translate into an equal rise in total school funding. Budget analysts across states describe this as supplanting, and it is a standard caveat for lottery and gaming revenue earmarked for schools, not a claim specific to Oklahoma.

That does not diminish the role of the payments. It means the most accurate statement is that tribal exclusivity fees are a legally dedicated and growing source within the education budget, and that their net effect depends on legislative choices about the rest of the budget. Both tribal advocates, who point to the deposits, and skeptics, who point to fungibility, are describing real features of the same system.

Why the figures matter in 2027

The figures carry policy weight because Oklahoma's fee stream depends on exclusivity. Proposals to legalize sports betting, debates over prediction market apps and any future renegotiation of the model compact all affect what tribes pay and why. Monthly fee receipts in OMES records for 2025 through 2027 have ranged from about $15.5 million to $23.0 million, levels that state budget writers have come to rely on. The broader economic picture is set out in our tribal gaming economic impact report, and property-level context is available in the Oklahoma state hub.

The statute answers where the money goes. The next legislative session is likely to test how much policymakers value that revenue stream when weighed against new forms of competition that do not pay into it.
