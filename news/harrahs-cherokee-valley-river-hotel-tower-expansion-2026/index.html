Eastern Band Nears Finish on $275M Harrah's Cherokee Valley River Tower

A new 12-story tower turns the Eastern Band's second casino into a full destination resort in western North Carolina.

By Marcus WhitehorsePublished August 01, 20265 min read

The Eastern Band of Cherokee Indians is nearing completion of a roughly $275 million expansion of Harrah's Cherokee Valley River Casino & Hotel in Murphy, North Carolina, adding a 12-story hotel tower and new dining venues to the tribe's second gaming property in the western part of the state. The Harrah's Cherokee Valley River expansion transforms what opened in 2015 as a compact regional casino into a full-scale destination resort, and it reflects the Eastern Band's strategy of reinvesting gaming proceeds into hospitality capacity that keeps visitors — and their spending — on tribal land longer.

A destination resort takes shape in Murphy

Valley River sits in Cherokee County near the point where North Carolina, Georgia, and Tennessee meet, positioning it to draw from metropolitan Atlanta and Chattanooga as well as the surrounding region. The new tower substantially increases the property's room count, the constraint that has historically capped how much overnight business a casino can retain. Additional food-and-beverage outlets round out the build, part of a broader industry move to lean on non-gaming amenities as a driver of visitation and margin — a trend examined in our analysis of the tribal casino branded-hotel flag strategy.

The expansion complements the Eastern Band's flagship, Harrah's Cherokee Casino Resort in Cherokee, roughly two hours to the east. Together the two properties anchor the tribe's gaming enterprise and its wider economic footprint in a mountainous region with few comparably scaled employers. Both operate under a long-running management relationship with Caesars Entertainment, which lends its Harrah's brand and operating expertise while the tribe retains ownership and the underlying gaming rights. The economics of that arrangement are the subject of our look at Caesars management contracts and tribal operators.

Reinvestment as a growth engine

Capital projects of this size illustrate how mature tribal operators compound gaming revenue into diversified, higher-margin assets. Rooms, restaurants, and event space convert a day-trip casino into a multi-night resort, raising per-visitor spend and smoothing the seasonality that affects mountain-tourism markets. The Eastern Band has pursued this reinvestment model steadily, and the Valley River tower is its most visible recent commitment.

Adding hotel rooms is the clearest lever a regional casino has to grow: every additional night a guest stays on property is revenue that would otherwise leak to off-reservation lodging.

The move also lands in a competitive Southeast. The Southeast has become a contested frontier for tribal gaming, with the Catawba Nation building its Two Kings resort near the South Carolina line and pressing plans for additional North Carolina venues, as covered in our report on the Catawba Two Kings permanent resort groundbreaking. For the Eastern Band, upgrading Valley River is partly a defensive investment: strengthening its western North Carolina position before newer entrants can erode it.

Sports betting and the broader enterprise

The expansion arrives against the backdrop of North Carolina's now-established sports-betting market, which the Eastern Band entered when the state authorized wagering. The tribe's experience during that rollout, reviewed in our two-year retrospective on North Carolina sports betting, showed both the appeal of digital wagering and the continued centrality of brick-and-mortar amenities to a tribe's revenue base. A larger, more capable resort at Valley River reinforces that base even as digital channels expand.

For the surrounding community, the payoff is measured in employment and in the tribal-government services that gaming revenue funds. Construction generates work in the near term, and a larger resort sustains more permanent hospitality jobs once the tower opens. As the Eastern Band completes the project, Harrah's Cherokee Valley River moves from a well-located regional casino toward a resort capable of competing for the multi-night, multi-state visitor — the segment where the Southeast's tribal operators increasingly stake their growth.

Geography sharpens the opportunity. Valley River draws from a corner of the country where full-scale casino gaming is scarce: Georgia has no commercial casinos, and much of the surrounding region lacks a comparable destination resort, leaving the Eastern Band's western North Carolina properties as the nearest option for a large swath of the Southeast. A bigger hotel lets the tribe convert that latent demand into overnight stays rather than day trips, capturing dining, entertainment, and repeat-visit revenue that a room-constrained property forfeits. It is the same logic that has driven the Eastern Band's investment at its flagship in Cherokee, applied to a market with room to grow.

The expansion is also a statement about pace. Rather than waiting for competition to mature, the tribe is enlarging Valley River now, while its regional position is strong. That timing gives the property a head start in building the loyalty base and brand recognition that are hard for later entrants to dislodge, and it signals to prospective competitors that the western North Carolina market already has an entrenched, well-capitalized incumbent prepared to defend its share.
