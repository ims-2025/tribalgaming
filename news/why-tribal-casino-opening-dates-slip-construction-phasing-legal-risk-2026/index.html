Why Tribal Casino Opening Dates Slip: Construction, Phasing and Law

A month's slip in Wisconsin, an unfinished resort in the Carolinas and a California casino a decade in the making reveal three distinct kinds of schedule risk.

By Daniel YazziePublished October 21, 20264 min read

Announced opening dates are among the most closely watched numbers in tribal gaming, and among the least reliable. In the past several weeks alone, the Ho-Chunk Nation moved its Beloit grand opening from September 26 to October 23, the Catawba Nation’s Two Kings Casino Resort was reported to be still completing construction nearly four months after its first phase opened, and the North Fork Rancheria is preparing to open a casino whose legal path began more than a decade ago. Each case illustrates a different type of delay. Understanding the categories, and why tribal projects face a distinctive version of each, helps readers judge how much weight to give a published opening date.

Construction and commissioning risk

The most familiar source of slippage is physical. Large casino resorts involve gaming floors that must be inspected and tested before patrons are admitted, systems that must be certified, and staff that must be licensed. Tribal gaming commissions license facilities and key employees, and every slot machine and system must be tested and connected to the casino’s accounting systems before play begins. Our explainers on key employee licensing and game certification and testing labs describe the steps that sit between a finished building and a legal opening. When any step slips, the opening date moves with it.

The Beloit change fits this category. The first phase is planned with about 1,300 slot machines, table games and live poker, a substantial installation, and a move of less than a month is a typical adjustment rather than a sign of trouble. We reported the new date and scope in our article on the Beloit grand opening.

Phasing risk

A second category is phasing. Many tribal developments open in stages so that revenue begins before the full resort is complete. That approach reduces financing pressure but extends the period during which a property is partly a construction site. The Catawba Nation’s Two Kings Casino Resort is an example: the first phase opened earlier this year, and construction on remaining components was still under way as of late September, according to Tribal Business News. Our reports on the phase-one opening and the hotel tower milestone follow the project. Phasing is not a failure of planning; it is a financing and risk-management tool. But it does mean that “opened” can describe a property that is far from finished, and readers should check which phase an announcement refers to.

Elsewhere, Tulalip Resort Casino completed a three-year expansion in September, and Hard Rock Casino Tejon in Kern County has announced a second phase that would add a 400-room hotel, a live-entertainment venue and a pool complex. Both illustrate how tribal operators now routinely plan multi-year build programs rather than single openings.

Legal and regulatory risk

The third category is distinctive to tribal gaming. A tribal casino can be built on time and still face delay because the legal basis for gaming on the land, the validity of a compact, or the approval of a management contract is contested. North Fork is the clearest example. The tribe’s project traces to a 2011 federal determination and a 2012 compact, a 2014 statewide referendum that rejected the compact, Interior’s 2016 secretarial procedures, and a state appeals court ruling that the vote annulled the governor’s concurrence. A separate federal suit by the Picayune Rancheria challenges the National Indian Gaming Commission’s approval of the tribe’s management contract with Station Casinos; the motions were argued on October 9. We follow that case in our litigation report, and our explainers on land into trust and gaming eligibility and governor concurrence set out the legal background.

Regulatory vacancies can add to this category. The National Indian Gaming Commission has had no chairperson since January, and approvals that depend on the chair, such as certain management agreements, can stall. That has delayed at least one operator transition in Oklahoma, as discussed in our report on the vacancy.

Why tribal projects are different

Commercial casinos also face permitting and licensing delay, but tribal projects add layers: federal land decisions, compact negotiations with state governments, and federal review of financing and management arrangements. Capital markets have adapted. Tribes increasingly finance projects through institutional lenders and real estate investment trusts, as described in our analysis of institutional capital in tribal resort financing. Lenders price legal risk explicitly, which is why financing structures and opening schedules are often linked.

A reader’s checklist

When an opening is announced, three questions are useful. Which phase is opening, and what remains to be built? Has the facility completed its commission licensing and regulatory notices? And is there pending litigation or an outstanding approval that could affect the property? Applying them does not guarantee accuracy, but it separates routine schedule movement from structural risk. Our directory of tribal casinos lists operating properties by state for readers who want to confirm whether a facility is open, and the Legal Guide explains the underlying framework in more depth.
