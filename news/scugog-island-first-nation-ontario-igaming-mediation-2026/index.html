Scugog Island First Nation enters mediation with Ontario over iGaming

Chief Kelly LaRocca says online gambling has cut into Great Blue Heron's revenue and that Ontario expanded the market without the consultation Section 35 requires.

By Linda CardinalPublished June 02, 20264 min read

The Mississaugas of Scugog Island First Nation are heading into mediation with the Government of Ontario over the province's online gambling expansion, in a dispute that has become a test case for how Canada's iGaming buildout interacts with First Nations gaming rights. The First Nation, which owns the Great Blue Heron Casino northeast of Toronto, contends that Ontario expanded its regulated online market without the consultation it was owed and in breach of a revenue agreement signed in 2016.

At the centre of the dispute is the argument that the province's iGaming framework — launched and then steadily widened over the past two fiscal years — has drawn play away from brick-and-mortar properties while delivering little of the resulting revenue back to the host First Nation. Chief Kelly LaRocca has said the community's casino has seen revenue decline since online gambling was legalized, and that the growth Ontario reports from internet gaming has come, in part, at the direct expense of the First Nation's own operations.

A constitutional question, not just a commercial one

The First Nation frames the matter as a question of constitutional obligation rather than a simple commercial grievance. Its position rests on Section 35 of the Constitution Act, which recognizes and affirms existing Aboriginal and treaty rights and gives rise to the Crown's duty to consult and accommodate when its decisions may affect those rights. By that reasoning, a decision as consequential as opening the province to competitive online operators should have triggered formal consultation before launch, not after the market was already running.

The revenue that online gambling generates for the province, the First Nation argues, comes at the direct expense of the host community and of economic reconciliation — not in addition to it.

Ontario's broader revenue-sharing arrangement directs a fixed share of provincial gaming proceeds to First Nations — a figure that sits well below the levels some communities had sought, and that initially did not capture online play at all before being adjusted in a recent fiscal year. The structure and limits of that arrangement are detailed in our coverage of Ontario's First Nations revenue-sharing framework, and the broader question of how provinces divide gaming proceeds is examined in our comparison of Canadian revenue-share models.

Why the case matters beyond Scugog Island

The significance of the Scugog Island dispute extends well past one casino. Canada's provinces are in the middle of an iGaming expansion that is reshaping where gambling revenue is generated and who captures it, and the legal theory the First Nation is advancing — that competitive online launches engage the duty to consult — could apply across multiple jurisdictions. A mediated outcome that requires deeper consultation or richer accommodation would set an influential precedent for host communities elsewhere.

That is already visible in the West, where Alberta's move toward a competitive online market has generated similar concerns from First Nations who fear that internet gambling will siphon revenue from host-nation charitable casinos without an adequate offset. Those parallels are explored in our reporting on Alberta's iGaming launch and its First Nations revenue share. In both provinces, the underlying complaint is the same: a regulated online market can grow provincial revenue quickly while leaving the land-based, community-owned operations that predated it exposed to new competition they did not agree to.

Mediation, rather than a courtroom verdict, gives both sides room to negotiate a settlement without a binding judicial ruling on the constitutional questions — an outcome the province may prefer to avoid given how broadly a finding on the duty to consult could reach. For the First Nation, mediation offers a faster route to relief for a property that is feeling competitive pressure now, while preserving the option to litigate if talks fail.

The Great Blue Heron Casino sits at the centre of this. Operated in partnership on the First Nation's land near Lake Scugog, it has long been the community's principal own-source revenue engine, funding programs and services that provincial transfers do not cover. When a regulated online market opens and a player who once drove to Great Blue Heron can instead wager from a couch through a licensed app — or visit one of the large new land-based resorts that have opened elsewhere in the Greater Toronto Area — the competitive pressure lands squarely on a property the First Nation depends on. That is the concrete harm behind the constitutional argument.

It is also why the dispute is as much about process as about money. The First Nation's complaint is not simply that its share of provincial proceeds is too small; it is that decisions reshaping its economic base were made without the consultation the Crown owes. A settlement that delivered more revenue but left the consultation question unanswered would, by that logic, only postpone the next conflict. Ontario, for its part, has an interest in resolving the matter quietly: a binding judicial finding that competitive iGaming launches trigger the duty to consult could complicate gaming expansion well beyond this one community.

What the process will not resolve is the structural mismatch at the heart of the dispute. Land-based First Nations casinos were built on an implicit promise of a protected regional market, and the arrival of province-wide online competition unsettles that premise. Whether the answer is a larger revenue share, formal consultation protocols for future expansions, or some combination, the Scugog Island case is likely to shape how Canadian governments approach economic reconciliation as the online market continues to grow.
