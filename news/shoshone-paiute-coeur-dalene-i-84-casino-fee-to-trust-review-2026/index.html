Shoshone-Paiute I-84 Casino Awaits Federal Trust Decision, Idaho Concurrence

One year after filing, the only Idaho tribe without a casino is waiting on a Bureau of Indian Affairs determination that carries a state-level veto point.

By Elena RuizPublished October 20, 20266 min read

The Shoshone-Paiute Tribes' proposed casino along Interstate 84 between Boise and Mountain Home is waiting on a federal fee-to-trust decision roughly a year after the application was filed, and on a concurrence from the State of Idaho that the process requires. The tribes' project, backed by the Coeur d'Alene Tribe, is one of two tribal casino proposals discussed for the corridor, as we outlined in our overview of the I-84 corridor proposals.

What the Shoshone-Paiute project proposes

The site lies along I-84 and straddles Ada and Elmore counties. It is not contiguous with the Duck Valley Reservation, the Shoshone-Paiute homeland on the Idaho–Nevada border, which is why the project is treated as an off-reservation gaming proposal. The Shoshone-Paiute Business Council filed an application asking the Department of the Interior to take the land into trust for gaming purposes. The reporting reviewed for this article places the filing about one year before September 2026 but does not give an exact date or the acreage involved.

The Coeur d'Alene Tribe is providing upfront investment and is expected to help manage the resort once it opens. The amount of that investment has not been disclosed in the sources reviewed. Shoshone-Paiute Chairman Brian Mason is among the tribal officials who have discussed the project publicly. Tribal leaders describe it as a way to address a housing deficit, teacher shortages and limited access to health care on Duck Valley, and they project jobs and annual economic activity in the tens of millions of dollars for Idaho. Five percent of casino revenue is slated to go to local schools and education programs.

The tribes also emphasize that the Shoshone-Paiute are the only federally recognized tribe in Idaho without a casino. That fact is part of the policy argument for the project, since IGRA was designed to give tribes access to gaming revenue for self-sufficiency, a theme explored in our piece on tribes without gaming and the IGRA access gap.

Where the federal review stands

The application is under review by the Bureau of Indian Affairs. Tribal officials say fee-to-trust gaming reviews typically take 12 to 18 months, which would put a decision window in the coming months if the review stays on that track. Construction cannot begin until the BIA issues its determination, and the sources reviewed do not report a decision date.

For off-reservation gaming, trust acquisition is not a routine administrative step. IGRA generally bars gaming on land taken into trust after Oct. 17, 1988, unless an exception applies. One of those exceptions, the so-called two-part determination, requires the Secretary of the Interior to find that a gaming establishment would be in the best interest of the tribe and its members and would not be detrimental to the surrounding community, and then requires the governor of the state to concur. Our explainer on governor concurrence and the two-part determination walks through the statutory text and how the steps interact.

It is worth being precise about what has and has not been reported. The sources reviewed state that Idaho's concurrence is required but do not report that a concurrence has been requested, granted or withheld. This article therefore does not characterize the state's position. Which exception the tribes are relying on, and how the Interior Department will analyze the application, will be clearer when the agency publishes its findings.

Why the timeline matters

Fee-to-trust processing has been a persistent bottleneck for tribal development. Environmental review under the National Environmental Policy Act, consultation with local governments, title work and the agency's own staffing all shape the clock. We examined those dynamics in our analysis of fee-to-trust processing timelines. A 12-to-18-month expectation is a tribal estimate rather than a regulatory deadline, and off-reservation gaming applications can run longer when they draw comment or competing interests.

The Idaho corridor adds a second layer: more than one tribe has proposed a project in the same general area, which raises questions about market capacity and how the federal and state decision-makers weigh competing proposals. Those questions are policy matters for Interior and for the state, not issues this article can resolve.

What to watch next

Three developments would move the story. The first is a BIA determination or a published environmental document, either of which would signal where the review stands. The second is any public statement from the governor's office on concurrence. The third is a financing or management announcement from the Coeur d'Alene Tribe clarifying its role. Idaho also has an unusual regulatory backdrop, since tribal gaming there operates under a state constitutional framework that limits the games available; background is available in our Legal Guide to IGRA and Class III gaming.

Until the federal decision lands, the Shoshone-Paiute project remains a proposal, not an approved casino. TribalGaming.com will report on the determination when it is released.
