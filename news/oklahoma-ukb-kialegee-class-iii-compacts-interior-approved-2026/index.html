Interior Lets UKB, Kialegee Oklahoma Gaming Compacts Take Effect

Two contested Class III compacts clear their federal hurdle by operation of law — while state-court questions linger.

By James KingfisherPublished August 08, 20264 min read

Two long-contested Oklahoma gaming compacts have quietly cleared their final federal hurdle. The Class III tribal-state gaming compacts negotiated by the United Keetoowah Band of Cherokee Indians (UKB) and the Kialegee Tribal Town with Governor Kevin Stitt have been deemed approved by the U.S. Department of the Interior, the department confirmed in notices published this summer. The agreements now carry the force of federal law under the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act (IGRA), even though the compacts never won ratification from the Oklahoma Legislature.

The approvals arrived not through an affirmative signature but through inaction. Under IGRA, the Secretary of the Interior has 45 days to approve or reject a submitted Class III compact; if the Secretary does neither, the compact is "considered to have been approved" to the extent it is consistent with the statute. That default mechanism — often called approval by operation of law — is the same route that recently allowed the Wyandotte Nation's compact with Kansas to take effect, and it is increasingly the path down which disputed agreements travel.

A compact fight that started in 2020

The UKB and Kialegee agreements are not new. Both tribes signed model gaming compacts with Governor Stitt in 2020, part of a cluster of deals the governor pursued during his broader battle with Oklahoma's largest tribes over the state's gaming framework. The UKB compact would allow the Tahlequah-headquartered band to conduct Class II and Class III gaming in Logan County, well outside its traditional base in the state's northeast. Kialegee Tribal Town, a small Muscogee-affiliated community, negotiated comparable terms.

The compacts drew fierce opposition almost immediately. In 2023, Oklahoma lawmakers voted to reject both agreements, citing what legislators described as significant legal flaws in how the deals were struck and questions about the tribes' authority to game on the identified lands. The tribes and the governor pressed on, and the resulting dispute has been working its way through the Oklahoma courts, where questions about the governor's power to bind the state without legislative approval remain unresolved.

Approval by the Interior Department settles the federal status of a compact. It does not, by itself, resolve the parallel questions of state law that Oklahoma's courts are still weighing.

That distinction matters. A federally approved compact establishes that the agreement is consistent with IGRA and clears the way for gaming under federal law. It does not extinguish separate challenges rooted in the Oklahoma Constitution or the scope of a governor's authority — challenges that opponents have pursued and that could still constrain how, or whether, the tribes ultimately build. Why the operation-of-law route keeps recurring

For the second time in a matter of weeks, Interior has allowed a contested Oklahoma-adjacent compact to take effect by letting the statutory clock run rather than issuing an affirmative decision. The department used the identical mechanism for the Wyandotte Nation's Kansas compact, and the pattern is drawing attention across Indian Country. Deemed approvals let the federal government avoid taking a public position on politically fraught agreements while still allowing them to become effective — an outcome that pleases tribes seeking to move forward but frustrates states and rival operators who wanted a clear federal ruling.

The stakes in Oklahoma are considerable. The state hosts one of the densest concentrations of tribal gaming in the country, and any new entrant reshapes an already saturated market. Established operators have historically guarded their exclusivity zones closely, and the prospect of UKB gaming in Logan County — roughly 150 miles from the band's headquarters — touches sensitive questions about market geography and the "Indian lands" on which gaming may lawfully occur. What comes next

With federal approval secured, attention turns back to the Oklahoma courts and to the practical work of siting, financing, and licensing any facility. Neither tribe has announced a construction timeline, and the unresolved state-law questions counsel caution against assuming shovels will move quickly. The compacts' effective date starts the clock on federal recognition of the gaming right, but the tribes still must satisfy the National Indian Gaming Commission's requirements for facility licensing, environmental review, and management contracts before a single machine is switched on.

For the broader industry, the episode is a reminder that the compact process does not always end with a clean yes or no. IGRA's 45-day default was designed as a backstop against federal foot-dragging, but in practice it has become a meaningful pathway for agreements that cannot win consensus. Observers who want to understand the statutory framework behind these disputes can consult our Legal Guide on IGRA and the Class III compacting process. As the Oklahoma litigation continues, the UKB and Kialegee compacts stand as the latest test of how far a governor's negotiating power extends — and of how the federal government's silence can speak as loudly as its approval.
