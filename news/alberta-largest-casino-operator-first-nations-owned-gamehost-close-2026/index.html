Alberta's largest casino operator is now First Nations-owned

The Gamehost close moved Indigenous Gaming Partners from market participant to market leader — without a single change to the Criminal Code.

By Linda CardinalPublished August 26, 20265 min read

The closing of the Gamehost Inc. acquisition in late June marked a structural change in Canadian gaming that has drawn less attention than it deserves: the largest casino operator in Alberta is now First Nations-owned. Indigenous Gaming Partners, through Pure Casino Entertainment Limited Partnership, completed the purchase of the publicly traded Gamehost on June 24, 2026, bringing its Alberta portfolio to seven casinos and three hotels. First Nations casino ownership in Alberta has moved from participation to control of the market.

The transaction was announced March 30, 2026 under a definitive arrangement agreement, with Gamehost shareholders receiving $13.65 in cash per common share. The acquired portfolio comprises Great Northern Casino in Grande Prairie, Rivers Casino and Entertainment Centre in Fort McMurray, Deerfoot Inn and Casino in Calgary, and the Service Plus Inns and Suites and Encore Suites hotel properties in Grande Prairie. It followed the court approval process required for the take-private of a TSX-listed issuer.

How IGP assembled the position

This is not a first move. At the end of 2024, Indigenous Gaming Partners acquired the casino operations of Edmonton-based Pure Canadian Gaming in partnership with Sonco Gaming — a portfolio comprising Pure Casino Edmonton, Pure Casino Yellowhead, Pure Casino Calgary and Pure Casino Lethbridge. The Gamehost transaction added three casinos and three hotels on top of that base. In roughly eighteen months, IGP went from a new entrant in commercial gaming acquisition to the province's largest operator by property count.

The strategic logic differs meaningfully from the American model. Under IGRA, a United States tribe conducts gaming on its own Indian lands, and the sovereignty analysis flows from the land base — a structural distinction we set out in our comparison of U.S. tribal and Canadian First Nations gaming legal models. In Canada, the provinces hold the conduct-and-manage authority under the Criminal Code, and First Nations participate through provincial frameworks, revenue-sharing arrangements, or — increasingly — by simply buying the operating businesses. The last route requires no legislative change, no negotiation of a new agreement, and no provincial concession. It requires capital.

Ownership versus revenue sharing

That distinction is the core of what is happening across Canada. The established First Nations gaming frameworks in Saskatchewan, Ontario and British Columbia are revenue-sharing constructs: a defined share of provincial gaming proceeds flows to First Nations trusts or partnerships, distributed according to negotiated formulas. Those arrangements have delivered substantial and reliable transfers — Saskatchewan's model has produced record payments in recent years, as covered in our reporting on SIGA's 2025-26 results, and Ontario's OFNLP framework distributes a fixed percentage of provincial gaming revenue.

Ownership is a different instrument. A revenue share is a claim on a defined slice of someone else's business, sized by a formula that was negotiated at a particular moment and is difficult to reopen. Equity in the operating company is a claim on the entire enterprise, including the upside from capital allocation, cost discipline, non-gaming development and eventual sale. It also carries the corresponding downside, which revenue-sharing recipients do not bear.

Revenue sharing pays a share of the province's outcome. Ownership pays the operator's outcome — including whatever the operator can do to improve it.

The Alberta position is particularly consequential because Alberta is simultaneously opening a competitive online market. The province's iGaming launch this year created a new distribution channel and a new set of questions about how land-based operators defend their floors. An operator that owns the physical properties and is positioned to participate in the online market has a materially different strategic posture than a revenue-share recipient watching the same transition from outside — a dynamic examined in our coverage of Alberta iGaming and First Nations revenue share.

The capital structure question

Acquisitions of this size require financing structures that most First Nations governments have not historically accessed. In the Gamehost transaction, VICI Properties acquired certain Gamehost real properties immediately following the closing and amended its triple net master lease with Pure to cover them. That is a sale-leaseback: the operating entity converts real estate into cash to fund the acquisition, and pays rent going forward.

The trade-off is well understood in gaming finance and is worth stating plainly. Sale-leaseback lowers the upfront capital requirement and accelerates the ability to transact, which is how a First Nations-backed operator competes for a public company against private equity bidders. It also converts a variable ownership cost into a fixed contractual rent obligation, and rent is senior to distributions. In a downturn, an owner with unencumbered real estate has flexibility that a lessee does not. We examined this trade-off in more depth in our analysis of REIT capital in First Nations gaming.

Whether the Alberta model travels is the open question. British Columbia has seen its own wave of First Nations casino acquisitions from Great Canadian Entertainment, driven by different nations with different capital sources. Saskatchewan and Ontario remain revenue-sharing jurisdictions with strong incumbent structures and less obviously available acquisition targets. What Alberta demonstrates is that where a province's casinos are held by willing sellers and financing is available, First Nations buyers are now credible acquirers of scale — not minority partners in someone else's transaction. That is a different position in the market than the one they occupied five years ago, and it was reached without a single amendment to the Criminal Code.
