Naskila Casino Leggett Opens, Extending Texas Class II Gaming Footprint

A 300-machine bridge facility on Highway 59 converts a construction site into revenue two years before the permanent resort opens.

By Marcus WhitehorsePublished August 20, 20264 min read

The Alabama-Coushatta Tribe of Texas has opened Naskila Casino Leggett, a temporary Class II gaming hall on the site of its planned permanent resort, extending the tribe's gaming footprint into a second Texas location for the first time. The interim facility operates 24 hours a day with roughly 300 electronic bingo machines and is designed to generate revenue and build a customer base while the full resort is constructed. Texas Class II gaming remains among the most legally constrained markets in Indian Country, which makes the opening a useful case study in what tribes can and cannot do under federal law in a state that has resisted casino gaming.

The Leggett site sits on approximately 95 acres of tribal land along U.S. Highway 59, roughly ten miles from the tribe's existing Naskila Casino in Livingston. The tribe broke ground on the permanent project in June 2026. That resort, projected at around 685,000 square feet, is planned to include an expanded gaming floor, a 366-room hotel, dining and entertainment venues, a pool complex, and conference space. The permanent opening is targeted for late 2028, meaning the temporary hall is expected to run for roughly two years.

Bingo machines, not slot machines

Everything on the Leggett floor is Class II. That is not a design preference; it is the boundary of what the tribe may lawfully offer. Texas has no Class III compact with the Alabama-Coushatta, and the state has spent decades litigating against tribal gaming within its borders. Under IGRA, a tribe may conduct Class II gaming — bingo and games similar to bingo, including electronic aids — without a state compact, subject to NIGC oversight and its own gaming ordinance. Class III games such as house-banked table games, roulette, and conventional slot machines require a compact the state has not offered.

The practical consequence is that a player at Leggett sees cabinets that look like slot machines but are running networked bingo games underneath. Outcomes are determined by a bingo draw shared across linked players rather than by an independent random number generator inside each cabinet. The differences are covered in our explainer on how Class II gaming machines work. Modern Class II product has narrowed the performance gap considerably, but hold and player experience still differ enough that operators in Class II-only markets tend to compete on service, food and beverage, and proximity rather than on floor variety.

The tribe's legal position in Texas has been contested for most of three decades, running through the Restoration Act that restored federal recognition to the Alabama-Coushatta and Ysleta del Sur Pueblo, and through the Supreme Court's 2022 decision addressing how that statute interacts with state gambling law. That ruling substantially improved the footing for Class II operations in Texas. Our explainer on the 2022 decision lays out what it did and did not settle.

The economics of a bridge facility

The temporary hall is expected to add roughly 110 jobs, on top of employment at the Livingston property, which the tribe has said will continue operating during construction. Polk and Livingston counties are among the lower-income areas of East Texas, and tribal gaming revenue there funds health services, education, and housing in the same way it does elsewhere in Indian Country.

The strategic logic of a bridge facility is well established. It converts a construction site into a revenue-generating asset years before the permanent building opens, which helps service construction debt and demonstrates demand to lenders. It also lets the tribe recruit and train staff gradually rather than hiring several hundred people in a single pre-opening sprint. Against that, interim facilities carry real costs: the capital spent on a building with a two-year life is not recoverable, and a modest temporary product can set customer expectations that the permanent resort then has to reset.

The Leggett opening is less about 300 machines than about establishing a second gaming location in a state where tribal gaming rights have been litigated continuously since the 1990s.

Cross-border demand and the Houston corridor

Leggett's location on Highway 59 places it on the corridor running north out of the Houston metropolitan area toward Lufkin and Nacogdoches. Texas sends an enormous volume of gaming demand across its borders — to Louisiana's riverboats and tribal properties, to Oklahoma's tribal casinos along the Red River, and to Las Vegas. Louisiana and Oklahoma operators have built substantial businesses on Texas players, a dynamic examined in our analysis of Texas cross-border demand.

An in-state Class II property does not capture the customer who wants craps and a sportsbook. It does capture the customer for whom a two-hour drive to Lake Charles is the only alternative to no gaming at all. That is a meaningful segment, and it is one reason the tribe has been willing to commit to a resort of this scale on a Class II-only revenue model.

What to watch

Three things will determine whether the Leggett project meets expectations. The first is whether the temporary hall's win per unit per day supports the permanent resort's underwriting. The second is construction execution on a 685,000-square-foot building in a labor and materials market that has been unforgiving to tribal projects nationally. The third is legislative: any change in Texas policy toward compacted gaming would substantially alter the economics of a property built for Class II. None of the three is likely to resolve quickly.

For now, the tribe has done what tribes in restricted-scope states have increasingly done — built to the edge of what federal law permits and let the market decide the rest. Our operator directory tracks Class II and Class III properties by state.
