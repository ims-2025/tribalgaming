NC Appeals Court Remands Catawba Sovereign Immunity Waiver Dispute

The 2-1 ruling does not decide whether the tribe is immune. It orders a claim-by-claim reading of waivers signed in two easement agreements near Two Kings.

By Daniel YazziePublished November 22, 20264 min read

The North Carolina Court of Appeals has sent the land dispute between the Catawba Indian Nation and Kings Mountain Land Development Partners back to the trial court, ruling that the lower court must take a closer look at the sovereign immunity waivers the tribe signed in two easement agreements before deciding which of the developer's claims can proceed. The 2-1 decision, issued November 18, 2026, vacated a trial court order that had dismissed several of the developer's claims on sovereign immunity grounds.

The ruling does not decide whether the Catawba Nation is immune from the suit. It instead instructs the trial judge to interpret the full language of the waivers and state expressly which claims, if any, are barred. That makes the case a useful study in how a limited waiver of tribal sovereign immunity, drafted years earlier for a specific purpose, can become the central question when a commercial relationship around a tribal casino breaks down.

The sovereign immunity waiver at the center of the case

Kings Mountain Land Development Partners, known as KMLD, originally owned the property where the Catawba Nation's Two Kings Casino Resort now stands and still owns surrounding land. It granted the tribe easements connected to the project. In its lawsuit, KMLD accused the Nation of trespass, conversion, unfair and deceptive trade practices and breach of the easement agreements, and sought injunctive relief. Its allegations concern construction activity it says affected its adjoining property, as described in our earlier report on the KMLD lawsuit against the Catawba Nation.

The parties disagree over how far the tribe's waivers reach. The Catawba Nation argues the waivers in the two easement agreements cover only claims about the easements themselves, and that any waiver of tribal immunity must be clear and unequivocal. KMLD argues the agreements waived immunity for any claim related to the agreements or to the transactions they contemplated, which in its view includes tort claims arising from construction of the casino.

Writing for the majority, Judge Toby Hampson, joined by Chief Judge Chris Dillon, held that the trial court had not adequately engaged with that language. The opinion directs the court on remand to consider what it means for a claim to be "directly or indirectly related to" the agreements and whether particular claims relate to transactions contemplated under them. Only then, the majority said, can the court decide which claims sovereign immunity bars.

Judge John Tyson dissented. He would have upheld the trial court's dismissal of the tort and statutory claims other than those related to the easements, and he argued that the appeal was interlocutory and that KMLD had not shown a substantial right or a risk of inconsistent verdicts sufficient to justify immediate review. One element of the trial court's order, the striking of a claim concerning a parking deck, appeared not to be in dispute between the parties.

Why the scope of a waiver matters for tribal casino projects

Tribal governments are immune from suit unless Congress abrogates that immunity or the tribe clearly waives it, a principle the U.S. Supreme Court reaffirmed in Michigan v. Bay Mills Indian Community. In practice, tribes routinely grant limited waivers in commercial agreements so that lenders, landowners and vendors will do business with them. Those waivers are usually narrow by design: they name the claims, the forum, the remedies and the assets that can be reached.

The question on remand is not whether the Catawba Nation waived immunity, but how much it waived and for what.

The KMLD case shows how the wording of those clauses can determine the outcome years after signing. Broad phrases such as "related to" or "arising out of" are common in commercial contracts, but in the tribal context they collide with the rule that waivers must be unequivocal and are construed narrowly in favor of the tribe. Courts have to reconcile ordinary contract interpretation with that federal-law presumption, and the majority's opinion effectively asks the trial court to do that work explicitly. Our analysis of limited sovereign immunity waivers in tribal casino contracts outlines how lenders and counterparties typically negotiate those terms.

For tribes, the lesson is a familiar one among gaming counsel: easements, ground arrangements and side agreements signed during land assembly can carry immunity provisions that outlast the relationship they were written for. For developers and landowners, the case is a reminder that the enforceability of their rights against a tribal counterparty depends heavily on precise drafting.

What happens next for Two Kings and the litigation

The case now returns to the trial court, which must make the waiver and immunity determinations the appellate majority required. No schedule for those proceedings has been announced. Depending on how the trial court rules, either side could seek further appellate review, and the dissent's view that the appeal was premature could resurface if the matter returns to the Court of Appeals.

The dispute has not interrupted operations at Two Kings, which the Catawba Nation opened as a temporary casino in July 2021 and has been expanding toward a permanent resort with a hotel. The property has become one of the most closely watched tribal developments in the Southeast; our analysis of the resort's final stretch toward a 2027 opening covers the construction timeline, and the North Carolina tribal gaming market explainer places the project alongside the Eastern Band of Cherokee Indians' properties.

The appellate ruling leaves the core question open. But by requiring the trial court to parse the waiver language claim by claim, it sets up a more detailed record on how North Carolina courts read limited waivers of tribal sovereign immunity in commercial agreements tied to a tribal casino.
