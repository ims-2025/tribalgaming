How Federal Rulemaking Works for Tribal Gaming: A Plain Explainer

From proposed rule to Federal Register to consultation, a guide to how the rules governing tribal casinos are made and how tribes shape them.

By Linda CardinalPublished November 03, 20264 min read

Federal rulemaking determines much of the day-to-day regulatory environment for tribal gaming, yet the process is often opaque to readers who encounter it only through headlines about proposed or withdrawn rules. Understanding how federal rulemaking works for tribal gaming, including notice-and-comment procedures, direct final rules and tribal consultation, helps explain why some rules take years and others are pulled back within weeks. This explainer describes the basic steps, the role of the National Indian Gaming Commission and the Department of the Interior, and how tribes participate.

Who writes the rules

Two federal bodies most often issue regulations that affect tribal gaming. The National Indian Gaming Commission, created by the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act of 1988, issues rules in Title 25 of the Code of Federal Regulations covering topics such as gaming ordinances, management contracts, facility licensing, audits and minimum internal control standards. The Department of the Interior issues rules on matters such as the procedures for approving tribal-state compacts and, where compact negotiations fail, the procedures the Secretary may prescribe. Our explainer on how the NIGC regulates tribal gaming describes the Commission's authority in more detail.

Both agencies are bound by the Administrative Procedure Act, the federal statute that sets the baseline requirements for how agencies make rules. The statute gives the public notice of proposed rules and an opportunity to comment, and it allows courts to set aside agency action found to be arbitrary or unlawful. For a related discussion of judicial review, see our explainer on the Administrative Procedure Act and review of Interior tribal gaming decisions.

The notice-and-comment process

In the standard process, an agency publishes a notice of proposed rulemaking in the Federal Register describing the proposed text, its legal basis and the reasons for it. The public, including tribes, trade associations and industry vendors, then has a set period to submit written comments. The agency must consider significant comments and explain its responses when it issues a final rule. A final rule is published in the Federal Register with an effective date, and it is later codified in the Code of Federal Regulations.

The process can be lengthy. Agencies often hold hearings or listening sessions, revise the text in response to comments and coordinate with other federal offices before finalizing a rule. Because a final rule can be challenged in court if the agency failed to follow required procedures or gave inadequate reasons, agencies have an incentive to document their reasoning carefully.

Direct final rules: a shortcut with a safety valve

A direct final rule is a procedural shortcut. The agency publishes a final rule and states that it will take effect on a particular date unless it receives significant adverse comments by a deadline. If no such comments arrive, the rule takes effect without a separate proposal stage. If they do, the agency withdraws the rule and may restart through the standard process. The device is intended for routine or noncontroversial changes.

The NIGC used this approach in 2025 for a change to facility license notification standards, then withdrew the rule after adverse comments, leaving the existing 120-day notice requirement in place. Our analysis of the withdrawn direct final rule on facility license notice explains that episode and what it means for tribes planning new casinos.

Tribal consultation and the government-to-government relationship

Beyond the formal comment process, federal agencies follow policies that call for consultation with tribal governments on actions that affect them. These policies reflect the government-to-government relationship between the United States and federally recognized tribes. Consultation typically involves letters to tribal leaders, virtual or in-person sessions and a period for written input. The NIGC has used consultation as part of its regulatory review, and our analysis of which rules are in play in NIGC consultations shows how it works in practice. Likewise, Interior has held consultations on compact-related procedures, as discussed in our report on Interior fee-to-trust and Class III compact rulemaking consultations.

Consultation is distinct from the formal comment process, and it does not guarantee a particular outcome. Agencies retain the authority to decide, but consultation can surface concerns, identify unintended consequences and build a record that supports or challenges the final rule. Intertribal organizations such as the Indian Gaming Association and the National Congress of American Indians often coordinate tribal feedback, which can amplify the effect of individual comments.

How tribes and operators can participate

Tribal governments, gaming commissions and enterprises can monitor the Federal Register for proposed rules, review agency consultation notices and submit written comments within the stated deadlines. Strong comments tend to be specific: they identify the provision at issue, explain practical effects on operations or sovereignty, cite legal authority and propose alternatives. Organizations that regularly engage in rulemaking often maintain internal procedures for tracking notices and coordinating legal review.

Operators and vendors benefit from understanding the timeline as well. A proposed rule signals possible changes months or years before they take effect, which can influence capital planning, compliance programs and technology decisions. A withdrawn or delayed rule, by contrast, means current requirements remain in place.

Where to learn more

For a broader foundation in the legal framework that governs these rules, read the site's Legal Guide to IGRA and Class III gaming. For upcoming conferences where regulators and tribal leaders discuss rulemaking priorities, see the events calendar. Following the Federal Register and agency consultation pages remains the most reliable way to track proposals as they develop.

In short, federal rulemaking for tribal gaming combines formal legal procedures with government-to-government consultation. Knowing the difference between a proposed rule, a final rule and a direct final rule, and knowing when and how to comment, gives tribal governments and industry participants a practical way to influence the rules that govern their operations.
