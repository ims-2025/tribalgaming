Election Night Read: Nebraska Measure 440 and Indiana Casino Votes

Voters in two states weighed new gambling expansion on November 3. Neither contest was a tribal measure, but both bear on tribal market positions.

By Daniel YazziePublished November 05, 20264 min read

Two gambling questions reached voters on November 3, 2026, and neither was a tribal measure. Even so, the outcomes bear on how tribal operators in the Plains and Midwest think about competition, exclusivity and the politics of expansion. In Nebraska, unofficial partial returns showed Initiative Measure 440, which would permit online sports wagering through licensed racetracks, trailing. In northeast Indiana, voters in three counties rejected local referendums that could have opened a path to a new commercial casino. This Nebraska Measure 440 analysis is limited to what has been reported and flags where results remain unofficial.

Nebraska Measure 440: what was on the ballot

Initiative Measure 440 was an initiated constitutional amendment. According to Ballotpedia, it would amend Article III, Section 24 of the Nebraska Constitution to allow laws authorizing sports wagers placed online by people located in the state, offered by authorized operators within licensed racetrack enclosures. Sports betting is currently permitted in Nebraska only in person at racetrack sportsbooks. Sponsors filed the initiative in February 2026, and the supporting campaign, Tax Relief Nebraska, was backed by national sportsbook operators and the state Democratic Party. Opposition was led by a group called Save Nebraska Sports.

As of Ballotpedia's November 4 update, with roughly 55 percent of precincts reporting, "no" held about 54.4 percent and "yes" about 45.6 percent. Those figures are partial and unofficial and will not be final until certified, so no conclusion should be treated as settled. Ballotpedia's page does not describe any tribal involvement in the measure.

Why a non-tribal measure matters to tribal operators

Nebraska's tribal gaming picture is shaped by the state's unusual structure. Casinos in the state operate on racetrack licenses, and tribal properties have been developing within that framework. Our coverage of the Nebraska ballot and the WarHorse and Winnebago interests and of the South Sioux City groundbreaking describes how the Winnebago Tribe's ventures connect to that licensing model.

The central question for tribal operators in any online-betting expansion is who captures the digital customer. In states with tribal exclusivity, such as the hub-and-spoke arrangements we describe in our explainer on the model, mobile wagering is tied to tribal casinos. A measure that routes online wagering through racetracks instead would create a different competitive map, one where tribal properties compete with racetrack-affiliated operators and national brands for the same players. That is a reason tribal operators watch these contests even when they are not formally parties.

Indiana: three counties say no

In Indiana, voters in Allen, DeKalb and Steuben counties rejected local referendums on Election Day, according to Casino.org. Opposition was about 60 percent in Allen County, about 65 percent in DeKalb County and nearly 60 percent in Steuben County. The referendums grew out of House Bill 1038, which Gov. Mike Braun signed in March 2026, following an effort by Full House Resorts to relocate its license from Rising Star Casino to a more lucrative northeastern market.

Lawmakers later amended the bill so any U.S. casino company could bid and added a $150 million licensing fee and a $500 million minimum investment. The reporting says those changes largely removed Full House's interest, and the company has said it will keep operating Rising Star. No major developer backed the local campaigns, and a group called Save Fort Wayne ran advertising focused on social harms. Indiana remains at 13 licensed commercial casinos; those casinos generated $1.84 billion in gross gaming revenue through September 2026, with licensed sportsbooks reporting $402.6 million.

Casino.org's account does not mention tribal interests, and none should be inferred. Indiana is not a state with a large tribal casino sector. The relevance to tribal gaming is indirect: the vote is another data point on voter appetite for new casino development near existing markets, a theme that also runs through our analysis of saturation and exclusivity in the Northeast.

What the two results suggest, carefully read

It would be a mistake to treat these results as a referendum on gambling generally. Nebraska's contest concerned a specific licensing channel and a specific campaign, funded largely by national operators. Indiana's concerned a local siting question with an unusual statutory backdrop. What they share is that voters, when asked directly, were not reflexively supportive of expansion, and that organized opposition campaigns did not need large budgets to prevail locally.

For tribal governments, the lesson often drawn from such votes is about process. Tribal gaming rests on federal law and compacts rather than local referenda, a distinction we explain in our legal guide. That insulates tribal operations from this kind of direct vote, but it also means tribes seeking new lands or compacts must win approval in other forums, such as governors' offices and Interior reviews.

What to watch

Final certified Nebraska numbers will determine whether Measure 440 fails or passes; if it fails, sponsors and racetrack interests may regroup for another attempt, and tribal operators will watch whether any new proposal addresses their position.
