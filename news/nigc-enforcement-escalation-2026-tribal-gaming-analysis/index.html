A Shorthanded NIGC Presses Landmark Enforcement Cases in 2026

The commission is testing the outer limits of its authority even as it operates with reduced leadership.

By James KingfisherPublished August 06, 20266 min read

The National Indian Gaming Commission is not operating at full strength, yet it has spent 2026 pressing some of the most consequential enforcement actions in its recent history. The paradox is worth attention. A federal regulator running with reduced leadership might be expected to husband its authority and avoid controversy. Instead, the commission has advanced disputes that go to the heart of where and how tribes may open new casinos, most visibly in a Michigan case that could redefine a long-standing rule about gaming on newly acquired lands.

The one-bite rule and a Michigan test

The flashpoint is the Grand Traverse Band of Ottawa and Chippewa Indians, which opened a casino in Benzie County, Michigan, and then found itself accused by the NIGC of operating unlawfully. At issue is the so-called one-bite rule, an interpretation of the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act that governs when a tribe may game on a particular parcel of Indian lands. The commission issued a Notice of Violation and disapproved the tribe's gaming ordinance, prompting the Band to sue, asking a court to declare the rule unlawful, vacate the notice, and bar further enforcement. Our coverage of the Grand Traverse Band's suit against the NIGC lays out the competing positions in detail.

The significance extends well beyond one casino. If the commission's reading prevails, tribes contemplating gaming on recently acquired or reacquired parcels face a higher bar and more uncertainty. If the tribe prevails, the NIGC's enforcement discretion narrows and a category of projects gains firmer footing. Either way, the case forces courts to define the boundary of the agency's authority, a boundary the commission itself chose to test at a moment when its bench is thin.

Enforcement with a reduced roster

That thinness is not incidental. The commission has been operating with fewer confirmed leaders than its full complement, a situation examined in our analysis of the NIGC's leadership vacuum. A regulator short on commissioners typically faces questions about quorum, the durability of its decisions, and its capacity to defend contested actions through appeals. Pressing a landmark case under those conditions is a calculated wager: the commission is betting that its statutory footing is strong enough that leadership gaps will not undermine the outcome.

The approach fits a broader posture. The NIGC's toolkit runs from notices of violation to civil fines to closure orders, escalating instruments described in our explainer on NIGC enforcement. Deploying the sharper end of that toolkit against an operating casino, rather than negotiating quietly, signals a willingness to draw bright lines even when litigation is likely. For tribes accustomed to a more conciliatory federal partner, the shift in tone is itself a development.

What tribes should watch

Several threads bear watching. The first is the durability of aggressive actions taken during a period of reduced leadership; a court that questions the commission's capacity could complicate future enforcement. The second is the facility-side of compliance. Every gaming place requires a facility license and an ordinance approved through the process described in our overview of how the NIGC regulates tribal gaming, and heightened scrutiny of where gaming occurs makes those approvals more than paperwork. A tribe that assumed a parcel was eligible could find that assumption challenged after construction, as the Grand Traverse case illustrates.

A regulator willing to press its hardest cases while shorthanded is signaling that it views its authority as settled, not provisional.

The third thread is precedent. Enforcement actions are not merely about one tribe; they establish the interpretive gloss that guides the next hundred projects. A commission that presses the one-bite rule now is shaping the environment for every tribe eyeing gaming on land that was not part of its historic reservation. That is why tribal attorneys are watching the Michigan docket with an interest far exceeding its geography.

None of this means the commission is acting improperly. Regulators are entitled, and often obligated, to enforce the statutes they administer even when doing so is unpopular or contested. The notable feature of 2026 is the combination: a commission that is institutionally stretched and yet strategically assertive, choosing to litigate foundational questions rather than defer them. For tribes, the lesson is practical. Diligence about land eligibility, ordinance approval, and facility licensing has never mattered more, because the federal regulator has signaled that it will test those questions in court rather than resolve them at the negotiating table. Operators planning new projects would do well to treat every eligibility assumption as something to be proven, not presumed, and to build their compliance record accordingly. That means securing land determinations early, documenting the chain of title and trust status, and keeping ordinance approvals and facility records current rather than scrambling to assemble them under the pressure of a notice of violation. The tribes most exposed are those that moved quickly on a favorable reading of eligibility without papering the file to withstand a challenge. In the current climate, the cost of that shortcut has risen sharply, and a commission willing to litigate has made clear that it will not simply defer to a tribe's own confidence about its land.
