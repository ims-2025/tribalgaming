Theft From Tribal Casinos: How 18 U.S.C. 1163, 1167 and 1168 Work

Congress wrote specific federal crimes for tribal gaming into IGRA. Here is what they cover, who they reach, and how cases move.

By Elena RuizPublished November 26, 20264 min read

When money goes missing from a tribal casino, the case usually lands in federal court. That is not an accident of jurisdiction. When Congress passed the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act in 1988, it added specific federal crimes to Title 18 of the U.S. Code to protect tribal gaming establishments, and those provisions sit alongside an older statute covering embezzlement from tribal organizations generally. Together, they form the core of the federal law on theft from tribal casinos.

This explainer walks through the three main statutes, the penalties they carry, and how investigations typically unfold. For the wider regulatory framework, see the TribalGaming.com Legal Guide.

The three statutes

18 U.S.C. § 1163: Embezzlement and theft from Indian tribal organizations. Enacted in 1956, well before modern tribal gaming, Section 1163 makes it a federal crime to embezzle, steal, knowingly convert or willfully misapply money, funds or other property belonging to an Indian tribal organization. The statute reaches tribal governments and tribally owned entities broadly, not only gaming. Where the value exceeds $1,000, the maximum penalty is five years in prison and a fine; at or below $1,000, the maximum is one year. Because many tribal gaming enterprises are arms of tribal government, Section 1163 is frequently charged in casino-related cases as well.

18 U.S.C. § 1167: Theft from gaming establishments on Indian lands. Added by IGRA, Section 1167 applies to anyone who abstracts, purloins, willfully misapplies or takes and carries away, with intent to steal, money or property belonging to a gaming establishment operated by or for, or licensed by, an Indian tribe under an ordinance approved by the National Indian Gaming Commission. It covers patrons, contractors and outsiders as well as insiders. The maximum penalty is one year for property worth $1,000 or less and ten years for property worth more than $1,000.

18 U.S.C. § 1168: Theft by officers or employees of gaming establishments on Indian lands. Section 1168 targets insiders: officers, employees and individual licensees of those same establishments. Congress set notably higher penalties here, reflecting the breach of trust involved. The maximum is five years for property worth $1,000 or less and twenty years for property worth more than $1,000.

The structure is deliberate: an outsider who steals more than $1,000 faces up to ten years, while an employee or officer who does the same faces up to twenty.

Why the NIGC ordinance matters

Sections 1167 and 1168 both apply to establishments operating under an NIGC-approved tribal gaming ordinance or resolution. That link means the federal protection follows the regulatory framework: a casino operating lawfully under IGRA is covered. It is one reason ordinance approvals, including those deemed approved by operation of law, have consequences beyond regulatory housekeeping.

IGRA also added Section 1166, which incorporates state gambling laws as federal law in Indian country for gaming that falls outside IGRA's authorized framework. Section 1166 gives the United States exclusive jurisdiction over criminal prosecutions under it, unless a tribe has consented to state jurisdiction, for example in a tribal-state compact. Section 1166 targets unauthorized gambling; Sections 1167 and 1168 protect authorized gaming operations from theft.

Who investigates and prosecutes

The NIGC is a civil regulator. It can identify deficiencies, issue notices of violation and impose civil fines, as described in our explainer on how the NIGC regulates tribal gaming, but it does not bring criminal charges. When its compliance work, or a tribal gaming commission's, uncovers potential criminal conduct, the matter is typically referred to federal law enforcement.

In most states, the FBI is the lead federal investigative agency for these offenses, often working with the Bureau of Indian Affairs Office of Justice Services, tribal police and the tribal gaming commission. Cases are prosecuted by the U.S. Attorney's Office for the relevant district. In states covered by Public Law 280, state authorities also exercise criminal jurisdiction over many offenses in Indian country, a framework discussed in our explainer on Public Law 280 and casino crime. Federal statutes of general application, such as wire fraud, money laundering and tax laws, are often charged alongside the gaming-specific provisions in larger schemes.

How cases typically surface

Most theft cases at tribal casinos are uncovered through the control systems that IGRA and the NIGC's Minimum Internal Control Standards require. Discrepancies appear in drop and count reconciliations, in cage and vault records, in slot accounting systems, or on surveillance footage. Our explainer on drop, count and cage operations describes the procedures designed to catch those variances.

Cases vary widely in scale. Many involve individual employees who skim from cash drawers, manipulate jackpot payouts or issue fraudulent player rewards. Others involve executives or officials and can reach well into the millions of dollars through fraudulent contracts, unauthorized payments or kickbacks. Larger schemes are frequently uncovered during annual independent audits, internal audits, or reviews commissioned by new leadership.

Restitution and tribal remedies

Federal sentencing in these cases typically includes restitution to the tribe or gaming enterprise. Tribes also retain their own remedies: tribal gaming commissions can revoke gaming licenses, tribal courts may hear civil claims, and enterprises can pursue recovery through insurance or civil litigation. A revoked tribal gaming license is often reported to other jurisdictions, making it difficult for an individual to work in gaming elsewhere.

The overall framework reflects a balance at the heart of IGRA: tribes regulate their own gaming, and federal criminal law provides a backstop that treats theft from tribal gaming as a serious offense against a sovereign government's revenue.
