Mississippi Choctaw Land $12 Million in EDA Grants for Industrial Park and Training

A 200-acre industrial park and a skills center are the latest signs of a tribe building an economy that does not depend on a single casino floor.

By Sarah BegayPublished November 02, 20265 min read

The Mississippi Band of Choctaw Indians has received just over $12 million in grants from the U.S. Department of Commerce's Economic Development Administration to build a new industrial park and finish a workforce training center, according to reporting published October 9 by Magnolia Tribune. The Mississippi Choctaw EDA grants are a notable example of how a gaming tribe uses outside capital to extend its economy beyond its casino operations.

The awards are not gaming funds, and the reporting on them does not connect them to casino operations. But in Choctaw, Mississippi, the tribe's best-known employer is its gaming and hospitality enterprise, and the investments are best understood against that backdrop.

What the grants will build

About $10.7 million of the total is designated for a new 200-acre industrial park, covering roads, water and sewer systems, and storm drainage. The remaining $1.9 million will finish construction of the tribe's Workforce Training Center and buy training equipment. Instruction at the center is expected to focus on heating, ventilation, air conditioning and refrigeration; electrical work; and welding. A groundbreaking for the industrial park is expected sometime next year.

Tribal Chief Cyrus Ben said the tribe has run out of industrial property, calling the grant a step toward jobs for the community. Mississippi state Representative Donnie Bell, who chairs the House Workforce Development Committee, and Ben Page, the EDA's deputy assistant secretary and chief operating officer, were also named in the coverage.

Infrastructure of this kind is typically the unglamorous prerequisite for private investment. Site preparation such as water, sewer, roads and drainage is the cost that prospective tenants expect to be in place before they sign leases, and it is also the type of expense that grant programs are designed to share.

Why diversification matters for a gaming tribe

Gaming revenue under the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act must be used for specific purposes, including funding tribal government operations, promoting tribal economic development and supporting general welfare. That framework gives tribes the flexibility to reinvest in projects like industrial parks, but it does not provide unlimited capital. Federal grants can stretch tribal dollars further, and they signal to lenders and tenants that a project has public-sector validation.

The Choctaw's gaming operations are anchored by Pearl River Resort, which we profile in our Pearl River Resort enterprise profile. A casino resort is a large employer, but it is also a concentrated one: its fortunes rise and fall with consumer spending, regional competition and regulation. An industrial park offers a different employment base, with tenants in manufacturing, logistics or services whose cycles do not move in lockstep with gaming.

That logic runs through much of the sector. Our analysis of non-gaming revenue diversification traces how tribes are building hospitality, real estate, energy and other lines of business alongside their casinos, and our piece on workforce and higher-education partnerships looks at the training pipelines that make those businesses viable.

The workforce piece

The training center is arguably as important as the land. Industrial tenants look for a local labor pool with credentials in electrical work, HVAC and welding, which are skilled trades that are in demand nationally. A tribe that can offer both a ready site and trained workers is a more attractive location than one that can offer only the former. For tribal members, such programs can mean wage-earning careers that do not require leaving the community.

There is also a governance dimension. A tribe-controlled industrial park allows the tribal government to set lease terms, tax and regulatory policy, and hiring preferences, including preferences for tribal members under tribal employment rights ordinances. Those choices shape how much of the park's economic benefit stays in the community.

What to watch

Scale is part of the story. A 200-acre site is large relative to many tribal land bases, where developable acreage is often constrained by trust status, checkerboarded ownership or environmental limits. Chief Ben's remark that the tribe has run out of industrial property suggests earlier phases of development have already absorbed available sites, which is itself a sign of demand.

Several questions will determine how much the grants matter over time. Whether the park attracts anchor tenants, whether the training center aligns its programs with those tenants' needs, and how the tribe finances any remaining costs are all open. The groundbreaking next year will be the first concrete milestone.

For observers of the industry, the broader point is that gaming revenue and federal economic development funding are increasingly complementary rather than competing sources of capital. A tribe with a strong casino operation is often better positioned to put together the matching funds, planning capacity and credibility that competitive grants reward. The Choctaw award illustrates that dynamic in a single announcement.

Funding timelines also matter. Federal grants of this kind generally come with reporting and construction requirements, so the schedule for the park will depend on design, procurement and environmental review as well as on the tribe's own readiness. Those steps are routine, but they explain why a groundbreaking is described as coming next year rather than imminently.
