State-Licensed vs. IGRA Casinos: How Tribal Ownership Works Off-Reservation

A tribally owned company can run a casino under a state license, with no compact at all. The legal differences are significant.

By Daniel YazziePublished November 07, 20265 min read

When people hear "tribal casino," they usually picture a facility on a reservation operating under a tribal-state compact. That is the core case under the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act of 1988, but it is not the only way a tribe can earn money from gambling. A tribe, or a corporation the tribe owns, can also hold a casino license issued by a state, on land that is not Indian land, and operate under the same rules as any other licensee. Understanding the difference is essential for reading news about ownership, regulation and ballot measures.

The IGRA model

IGRA applies to gaming conducted on Indian lands. It sorts games into three classes. Class I covers traditional social games, Class II covers bingo and similar games, and Class III covers casino-style games such as slot machines and table games. Class III gaming requires three things: the tribe's gaming must be permitted in the state for some purpose, the tribe must adopt an approved ordinance, and the tribe must enter a compact with the state, approved by the Secretary of the Interior. Our Legal Guide covers each step, and our Class II versus Class III explainer details the classes.

In this model, the tribe is the regulator of its own facility, with oversight from the National Indian Gaming Commission, and the compact sets out how the state participates, often through revenue sharing and regulatory cooperation. Net revenue must be used for specified purposes, such as funding tribal government operations, member welfare and economic development.

The state-licensed model

A state-licensed casino sits outside IGRA. The owner applies to the state gaming regulator, receives a license under state law, and pays state tax at the rate state law sets. There is no compact, no Interior approval and no NIGC oversight, because the land is not Indian land and the operator is a licensee like any other. A tribe can participate in this model by owning a company that holds such a license.

Nebraska offers a current example. The WarHorse casinos in Lincoln and Omaha operate on racetrack licenses issued under Nebraska law. They are owned by Ho-Chunk Inc., which the Winnebago Tribe of Nebraska owns. Because the casinos are state-licensed, changes to their offerings, such as the online sports betting measures voters rejected on November 3, run through the state ballot and the Nebraska Racing and Gaming Commission rather than through compact amendments. The measures, as reported, would not have changed compact-based tribal gaming. Related coverage includes our report on the WarHorse groundbreaking in South Sioux City.

Why the distinction matters

The two models differ in at least four ways. The first is regulation: a compact casino is regulated primarily by the tribe, while a state-licensed casino is regulated by the state. The second is taxation: a state-licensed operator pays tax at the state's rate, whereas compact payments are negotiated, and their structure varies widely. The third is exclusivity: compact payments are often tied to exclusivity in the state, while a state-licensed operator holds a competitive license that the state can expand to others. The fourth is sovereign immunity and legal exposure: a tribal enterprise operating under a state license generally operates like a commercial company in its dealings, which can affect how disputes are handled, though the specifics depend on how the corporation is organized.

For readers following ballot measures and legislative battles, the practical lesson is to ask which legal channel a proposal uses. A measure governing state licensees does not alter compacts; a compact amendment does not bind state-licensed casinos. Mixing the two leads to misreading both. Our explainer on tribal versus commercial casinos offers further grounding.

Why tribes choose each route

Tribes use the state-licensed route when they lack suitable Indian land in a favorable market, when state law offers an opening unavailable on trust land, or when a business opportunity arises through acquisition. The IGRA route offers greater sovereignty and typically a higher share of revenue, but it requires land in trust or restored status and often a long compact negotiation. Neither is inherently better; they serve different goals, and some tribes and tribal enterprises use both.

A final caution: the line between the models is a legal one, not a branding one. A casino's marketing may use a tribe's name whether it operates under a compact or a state license. To tell which applies, look at where the land is, who issues the license, and whether a compact is on file with the Interior Department. Those three facts answer most questions.

Two practical examples help. A tribe with a reservation far from population centers might acquire or invest in a state-licensed casino near a metropolitan area, gaining market access that its trust land cannot provide. Conversely, a tribe with a well-located reservation will often prefer a compact, since the tax and regulatory arrangement can be more favorable and the facility is an exercise of tribal sovereignty rather than a commercial venture. Observers sometimes call the first approach an investment and the second a governmental function, a distinction that also surfaces in how revenue is accounted for and distributed to members.

Federal law adds one more wrinkle. If a tribe wants to conduct gaming on newly acquired land outside its reservation, IGRA generally prohibits it unless an exception applies, such as restored lands or a two-part determination with the governor's concurrence. Buying an existing state-licensed operation sidesteps that restriction entirely, which is part of its appeal, but it also means the operation receives none of IGRA's protections.
