How Tribes Intervene in Lawsuits: Intervenor Status and Immunity Explained

When a lawsuit threatens a compact or a tribal gaming law, a tribe may ask to join the case. Here is how that works and what it risks.

By Daniel YazziePublished November 10, 20265 min read

When a lawsuit challenges a state law, a compact or a federal approval that benefits a tribe, the tribe is often not a named defendant. The plaintiff may have sued a governor, a state agency or a federal official instead. In that situation a tribe has a choice: stay on the sidelines, or ask the court for permission to join the case as an intervenor. Tribal intervention in lawsuits has been in the news this month, after a Waukesha County judge allowed eight Wisconsin tribes to join the challenge to that state's tribal sports betting law. This explainer describes how intervention works in general terms, what it means for tribal sovereign immunity, and why outcomes differ from case to case. It is general information, not legal advice.

Why plaintiffs sue officials rather than tribes

Federally recognized tribes are immune from most lawsuits unless Congress has authorized the suit or the tribe has waived its immunity. The Supreme Court reaffirmed the doctrine's breadth in Michigan v. Bay Mills Indian Community, discussed in our Bay Mills explainer. Because suing a tribe directly usually fails at the threshold, challengers tend to target the government actors who negotiated, approved or enforced the arrangement. In Wisconsin, the plaintiffs sued the governor under the state constitution; in other disputes, plaintiffs have sued the Department of the Interior under the Administrative Procedure Act, a path we describe in our explainer on APA review of Interior decisions.

The result is a lawsuit that may decide the fate of a tribe's compact or revenue stream while the tribe itself is not formally a party. Courts recognize that gap. In some cases they have held that the tribe is a required party who cannot be joined because of its immunity, and have dismissed the case, as in the litigation analyzed in our piece on the Maverick Gaming outcome. In others, the case proceeds against the officials, and the tribe must decide how to protect its interests.

How intervention works

Intervention is a procedural mechanism by which a non-party asks to join a pending case. In federal court it is governed by Rule 24 of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure, which recognizes two forms. Intervention as of right is available to a party that claims an interest in the subject of the case, whose ability to protect that interest could be impaired by the outcome, and whose interest is not adequately represented by existing parties. Permissive intervention lies within the court's discretion when the applicant has a claim or defense that shares a common question with the case. State courts have their own rules, which are similar in structure but differ in detail.

Timeliness matters. A tribe that seeks to intervene early, before the briefing schedule is set, is in a stronger position than one that waits until the case is nearly decided. The adequacy-of-representation requirement is often the most contested element. A court may conclude that a governor defending his own conduct adequately represents a tribe's interest, or it may recognize that the tribe's interests, including its sovereignty and its revenue, are distinct from the state's. Outcomes vary. Our report on the denial of tribal intervention in the California cardroom case shows a request that did not succeed, while the Wisconsin intervention was reported as unopposed.

Intervention and sovereign immunity

The central concern for a tribe is whether stepping into a case compromises its immunity. As a general principle, immunity is waived only by a clear, unequivocal act, and tribes that intervene typically do so with an express reservation of their rights. Courts have treated the question of whether intervention waives immunity as dependent on the circumstances, including the claims asserted and the relief sought. A tribe that intervenes solely to defend a law or compact is in a different position from one that files counterclaims or seeks affirmative relief against another party.

Intervention also carries practical consequences beyond immunity. An intervenor is generally bound by the judgment, so the tribe cannot later relitigate the issue. It may be subject to discovery obligations, which can reach tribal records. It takes on the cost of litigation. And it exposes tribal leaders and counsel to scrutiny in a public forum. Against those burdens, the benefit is control: the tribe can make its own arguments, appeal an adverse ruling in its own name, and shape the factual record rather than rely on another party.

What intervention does not do

Intervention does not make a tribe a defendant in the sense the plaintiff originally chose, and it does not expand the claims in the case. It also does not guarantee that the court will accept the tribe's legal arguments. In a case like the Wisconsin litigation, the central question is whether the legislature exceeded limits in the state constitution, and that question will be decided on the law regardless of how many parties defend the statute.

Reading the next headline

When a news item says a tribe has asked to intervene, three questions help place it. Which court is hearing the case, and which rule governs intervention there? Who are the existing defendants, and do their interests align with the tribe's? And has the tribe expressly preserved its immunity? The answers explain why one tribe can secure a seat while another is turned away, and why intervention can be a strategic decision rather than an automatic one.
