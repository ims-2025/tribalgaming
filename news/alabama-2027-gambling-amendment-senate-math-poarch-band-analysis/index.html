Alabama's 2027 Gambling Math After the Election: What It Means for Poarch

The election did not change the three-fifths threshold. It made reaching it somewhat more plausible, and put the question of a tribal compact back on the table.

By James KingfisherPublished November 17, 20265 min read

Alabama's November elections did not change the basic arithmetic of gambling legislation in the state, but they moved it. Democrats flipped two Alabama Senate seats in Madison County, shrinking the Republican supermajority in a chamber that has failed to pass a gambling constitutional amendment three years running, and Governor-elect Tommy Tuberville has said he would support letting voters decide on a lottery. For the Poarch Band of Creek Indians, the only federally recognized tribe in Alabama and the operator of its largest gaming properties, the result reopens a question that seemed closed after this year's session: whether the 2027 Legislature will try again on an Alabama gambling amendment, and what role a tribal compact would play in it.

The three-fifths threshold

Alabama's constitution bars lotteries, and any broad legalization of a lottery, casino gaming or sports betting requires a constitutional amendment. An amendment must win three-fifths approval in both chambers before going to voters, which in the 35-member Senate means 21 votes. The governor's signature is not required.

That threshold has been the obstacle. In 2024, the House passed a broad package covering a lottery, casinos and sports betting. The Senate preferred a narrower approach, and the compromise that emerged fell one vote short in the Senate. Renewed efforts in 2025 and 2026 stalled after Senate leaders acknowledged they lacked the votes. Our earlier analysis of the Alabama gambling stalemate traced how those efforts unraveled.

In the general election, Democrat Jared Sluss narrowly defeated former Republican state Sen. Sam Givhan in a Huntsville-area district, and Democrat Rudolph Drake won the open District 2 seat vacated by retiring Republican Sen. Tom Butler, taking about 56 percent of the vote. The Republican supermajority will be at least two seats smaller in the next quadrennium.

Why two seats do not settle the count

The new seats do not translate automatically into gambling votes. Senate Minority Leader Bobby Singleton said he had not yet asked either senator-elect about gambling, noting that Democrats did not recruit them "because of their gaming perspective." Gambling coalitions in Alabama have never broken cleanly along party lines. Some Republicans have supported a vote on a lottery or broader gaming, some Democrats have resisted particular packages, and disagreements over licensing, the number and location of casinos, and the distribution of revenue have split supporters as often as opponents.

The practical effect of the election is that the coalition-building problem is somewhat smaller. If the 2024 compromise fell one vote short, a modestly different Senate could reach 21 on a similar package, but only if negotiators can hold the votes they had and add new ones. That depends on what the bill contains.

Tuberville's position

The executive branch changes as well. Tuberville, who defeated Democrat Doug Jones to succeed term-limited Gov. Kay Ivey, said during the campaign that he supported letting Alabamians vote on a lottery and that the governor's office should work with legislators to develop a comprehensive proposal before anything goes to voters. He indicated he would not block gambling expansion but would not champion it without a complete plan, and did not commit to specific sportsbook licenses, casino locations or tax rates.

A governor's signature is not needed for an amendment, but the governor's role matters in two ways. A supportive governor can help assemble legislative votes and public backing. And in every serious Alabama proposal of recent years, the governor has been the official authorized to negotiate a Class III compact with the Poarch Band.

Where the Poarch Band fits

The Poarch Band operates Wind Creek casinos in Atmore, Montgomery and Wetumpka on trust land. Because Alabama law does not permit Class III gaming, those properties offer Class II electronic bingo machines regulated under IGRA, the National Indian Gaming Commission and the tribe's own gaming regulators. Without a state law authorizing Class III games, the tribe has no basis to seek a compact for slot machines, table games or sports betting. Our explainer on Class II versus Class III gaming covers the distinction.

The 2024 House package would have authorized the governor to negotiate a compact with the tribe, a provision that recognized a practical reality: any Alabama gaming market will be shaped by the operator that already runs the state's largest gaming properties. It also generated opposition from competing interests, including operators of electronic bingo halls at non-tribal sites, who sought their own licenses. How a 2027 bill handles that tension, between a compact for the Poarch Band and new commercial licenses for other operators, is likely to determine whether it can reach 21 votes.

The tribe's position gives it leverage and exposure at the same time. A Class III compact would allow it to convert its Alabama properties to full casino operations and possibly add sports wagering, a significant upgrade for a tribe that already runs a multistate enterprise described in our Wind Creek Hospitality profile. But a package that authorizes several new commercial casinos without a compact, or that sets compact terms the tribe considers unworkable, could leave it facing new competition with no corresponding expansion of its own rights.

What to watch in 2027

Several signals will indicate whether this cycle differs from the last three. One is whether legislative leaders and the incoming governor agree on a single starting framework before the session, rather than reconciling House and Senate versions at the end. Another is whether the opening proposal is a lottery-only amendment, which Tuberville has endorsed and which is the easiest to pass, or a broader package including casinos, sports betting and a tribal compact. A third is how sweepstakes and unlicensed electronic gaming are addressed, an issue that drew attention after a sweepstakes casino operator contributed to Tuberville's campaign.

A lottery-only amendment would not directly affect the Poarch Band's gaming rights, though it could affect the political appetite for a second, broader measure. A comprehensive package would put the tribe at the center of the negotiation. The election made either path somewhat more plausible. It did not make either one likely, and Alabama's history of near misses counsels caution about reading too much into a two-seat shift.
