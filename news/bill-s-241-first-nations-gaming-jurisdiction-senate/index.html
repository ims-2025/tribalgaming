Bill S-241 puts First Nations gaming jurisdiction back before the Senate

A revised Senate bill — backed by a 15-Nation memorandum of understanding — has cleared second reading and now heads to committee for the deepest scrutiny it has yet received.

By Linda CardinalPublished May 24, 20266 min read

A bill that would fundamentally redraw the line between provincial and First Nations authority over on-reserve gambling is again moving through the Canadian Senate. Bill S-241, sponsored by Senator Scott Tannas, would amend the Criminal Code and the Indian Act to place First Nations on equal jurisdictional footing with the provinces when it comes to conducting, licensing, and regulating gaming on reserve land. The bill has cleared second reading and is now heading to committee — the stage at which it will receive the most substantive scrutiny it has yet had.

The current Criminal Code framework, dating to the 1980s, grants provinces the exclusive authority to "conduct and manage" lottery schemes within their borders. Provincial Crown corporations such as BCLC, OLG, AGLC, and Loto-Québec have built their gaming monopolies on that foundation. Bill S-241 would carve out an explicit exception for First Nations, allowing a Nation's governing body to assume that authority on its own lands by giving notice to the federal government and to the relevant province. Approval from either level of government would not be required.

What changed from the earlier draft

An earlier version of the proposal, Bill S-268, drew opposition from the Mohawk Council of Kahnawà:ke, which already exercises de facto regulatory authority over online gaming through its Kahnawake Gaming Commission and feared the bill would dilute that authority. The redraft addresses those concerns directly, and Kahnawà:ke is now among the bill's supporters. A memorandum of understanding signed in 2024 by fifteen First Nations across multiple provinces lays out a shared vision for a national Indigenous-led gaming authority, and that document has become the political backbone of the S-241 coalition.

The Nations behind the MOU argue that the existing federal framework, written before the United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples was incorporated into Canadian law, is incompatible with the Crown's contemporary obligations. They point to the United States as an instructive contrast: under the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act, tribal governments — not state governments — are the default authority over gaming on Indian lands, subject to a compact requirement for Class III games. Bill S-241 would not import IGRA wholesale, but it would close the most glaring gap between the two countries' frameworks. Readers looking for a side-by-side primer can consult our Legal Guide.

What's already happening in the provinces

The legislative debate is overtaking facts on the ground. In Alberta, the provincial government has structured the launch of its iGaming market to include a dedicated First Nations revenue share, a model we examined in our analysis of the July 2026 framework. Saskatchewan's Indigenous Gaming Authority continues to expand under a long-standing tripartite arrangement; SIGA's most recent capital projects were the subject of our SIGA expansion report. And in British Columbia, a series of First Nations acquisitions of commercial casino properties has put a large share of the province's casino floor under Indigenous ownership for the first time.

S-241 would not import IGRA wholesale, but it would close the most glaring gap between Canada's framework and the one operating south of the border.

Each of those provincial developments has been negotiated inside the existing Criminal Code framework — which is to say, with provincial Crown corporations as the conducting authority and First Nations as licensed operators, employees, or commercial counterparties. Bill S-241 would change the default rather than the practical arrangements, and several provincial governments have signaled they would prefer to keep the current structure in place.

Where the bill goes from here

Committee review is the bill's first real test. Senate committees can call expert witnesses, request legal opinions from the Library of Parliament, and amend the text. The provinces are expected to be heard, as are the gaming Crown corporations, the Assembly of First Nations, the MOU signatories, and at least some industry groups. The most contentious questions will likely be procedural: what happens if a First Nation issues licenses that conflict with a provincial regulator's standards, what role the federal anti-money-laundering regime plays, and how cross-border online wagering would be policed.

Even with committee passage, the bill's path to Royal Assent is uncertain. Senate public bills routinely die on the Order Paper at session's end, and S-241 would need to clear both chambers within the current parliamentary calendar to become law. The political signal, however, may matter more than the immediate legislative outcome. With fifteen First Nations on the record in favor and the Senate willing to debate the proposal substantively, the Crown is being told — clearly and on the record — that the status quo on Indigenous gaming is no longer politically settled.

Industry watchers should also pay attention to which provinces choose to engage constructively at committee and which choose to stonewall. The provinces that have already accommodated First Nations gaming partnerships — Saskatchewan, Alberta, and increasingly British Columbia — have less to lose from formal jurisdictional clarity than provinces that have kept their Crown corporations at the center of every gaming decision. The committee record will be the place where those provincial postures are exposed in detail, and the testimony filed there is likely to become the foundation for whatever the next Parliament does with the file if S-241 itself does not reach Royal Assent in time.
