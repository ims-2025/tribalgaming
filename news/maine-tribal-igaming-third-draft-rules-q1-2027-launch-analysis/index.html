Maine Tribal iGaming: Why the Q1 2027 Launch Hinges on Rulemaking

The Wabanaki Nations hold the exclusive rights. Whether they use them in early 2027 depends on whether regulators must publish a fourth draft.

By Sarah BegayPublished November 12, 20264 min read

Maine's four Wabanaki Nations hold exclusive rights to offer online casino gaming in the state, but the date their platforms go live depends on a rulemaking process that has now produced three drafts. The Maine Gambling Control Unit published its third draft of the iGaming rules on September 30 and accepted written comments through October 30. Regulators have pointed to the first quarter of 2027 as a target, but the agency's executive director has warned that further substantive revisions could push the Maine tribal iGaming launch later.

Where the Maine tribal iGaming rules stand

LD 1164, which the Legislature passed in 2025 and Gov. Janet Mills allowed to become law without her signature in January 2026, grants online casino rights exclusively to the Passamaquoddy Tribe, the Penobscot Nation, the Houlton Band of Maliseet Indians and the Mi'kmaq Nation. Each tribe may partner with one commercial operator, mirroring the structure of Maine's tribal online sports betting law. The law took effect in July 2026, starting the regulatory process. Our earlier analysis of Wabanaki iGaming exclusivity explains why the arrangement matters to tribes whose land-based gaming options have been constrained by Maine's settlement-act framework.

The third draft, as summarized by PlayUSA and The Lines, keeps the financial terms of earlier versions: an 18 percent tax on adjusted gross iGaming receipts, a $50,000 operator license fee, a $500,000 bond, and $10,000 license fees for suppliers and management service providers. It clarifies that live dealer studios may operate outside Maine with the director's approval, while the primary server must remain in the state. It also bars advertising aimed at people under 21 and proposes a third-party, real-time self-exclusion system.

The rulemaking clock

The timeline after the comment deadline is where the uncertainty lies. Under the process described in those reports, the Gambling Control Unit has up to 120 days after comments close to adopt the rules, and the Attorney General's Office has up to 150 days for its legality review. Those are maximums rather than estimates, and either step could move faster. But Executive Director Milt Champion said on October 20 that significant revisions would require the agency to publish the rules a fourth time, opening another public comment period. If that happens, a first-quarter launch becomes difficult.

Administrative readiness is a second constraint. The agency has received approval for a new licensing database and portal, according to PlayUSA, but companies cannot file applications until it is available and can only prepare materials in the meantime. The unit is also hiring six additional staff. Licensing of operators, suppliers and management service providers must follow rule adoption, and platforms must be tested before they accept real-money play. Each stage is routine for a gaming regulator, but the stages run largely in sequence.

The state has projected $3.6 million in iGaming tax revenue for fiscal year 2026-2027, a modest figure that reflects a partial year of operation at best.

Operators and partners

Caesars Entertainment has agreements with the Penobscot Nation, the Mi'kmaq Nation and the Houlton Band of Maliseet Indians and plans three online casino brands: Caesars Palace Online Casino, Caesars Sportsbook & Casino and Horseshoe Online Casino. We covered that expansion in our report on Caesars and the Wabanaki Nations. The Passamaquoddy Tribe partners with DraftKings for online sports betting, and The Lines has reported that DraftKings has a deal in principle with the tribe for the fourth platform. A final online casino agreement has not been publicly detailed.

The partner structure means that the Wabanaki Nations hold the licenses and the exclusive rights while commercial operators supply technology, marketing and product. That division of roles is familiar from tribal sports betting markets elsewhere, and its economics depend on contract terms that are generally not public. For the tribes, the value lies in a recurring revenue stream that does not depend on the location of a casino building.

The litigation overhang

Churchill Downs Inc., parent company of Oxford Casino Hotel, sued in January 2026 to challenge the tribal-exclusive framework, arguing that it unlawfully excludes Maine's two commercial casinos. The Wabanaki Nations joined the case in April to defend the law. The case remained unresolved as of late October, according to The Lines, which also reported that Oxford Casino is for sale. A ruling is not necessarily required before the state can launch, but an adverse decision could complicate the market, and a prolonged case adds uncertainty for operators weighing investment in a small state. Our coverage of the Churchill Downs challenge outlines the claims.

What it means for tribes

Maine's model of tribal exclusivity delivered through commercial partners places it alongside states such as Connecticut and Michigan, where tribes participate directly in regulated online casino markets, though each state rests on a different legal structure. If adopted, Maine's rules would make it the eighth U.S. state with a regulated online casino market. The comments filed by October 30 will determine whether regulators can finalize the third draft or must publish a fourth. That decision, more than the litigation, is the most immediate variable in whether Maine's tribal iGaming market opens in early 2027 or later in the year.
