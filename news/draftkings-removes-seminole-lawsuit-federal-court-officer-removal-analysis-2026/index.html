Why DraftKings Moved the Seminole Lawsuit to Federal Court

The federal officer theory faces a high bar under Supreme Court precedent, but the removal alone can slow the tribe's path to a ruling on Florida law.

By James KingfisherPublished November 16, 20265 min read

The Seminole Tribe of Florida chose a state courthouse in Fort Lauderdale when it sued DraftKings over its prediction-market product in October. A month later, the case is in federal court. On November 9, DraftKings, the entity that operates DraftKings Predictions, and chief executive Jason Robins filed a notice removing Seminole Tribe of Florida v. DraftKings Inc. from Broward County to the U.S. District Court for the Southern District of Florida, according to reporting on the filing and the docket. The move turns a dispute about Florida gambling law and the tribe's compact into a fight over which court system should decide it, and the answer could shape how quickly the Seminole DraftKings lawsuit reaches the merits.

The underlying complaint, filed October 8 in Florida's 17th Judicial Circuit, alleges that DraftKings Predictions is an unlicensed sportsbook operating in violation of Florida law and of the exclusivity the tribe holds under its 2021 compact. It seeks a declaratory judgment, an injunction, restitution and disgorgement. Our report on the original filing summarizes the claims.

The federal officer theory

According to reporting on the notice, DraftKings leads with federal officer removal and also asserts federal question jurisdiction. The federal officer removal statute, 28 U.S.C. § 1442, allows removal of a state-court case brought against a federal officer or agency, or against "any person acting under" one. DraftKings' position is that, through Railbird, the CFTC-registered exchange on which its Predictions contracts trade, it is acting under the Commodity Futures Trading Commission.

That is a demanding argument. In Watson v. Philip Morris Cos. (2007), the Supreme Court held that a private company does not "act under" a federal agency merely because it is subject to detailed federal regulation, even intense regulation. The phrase generally requires something closer to a delegated or assisting role, such as a contractor helping the government carry out a task it would otherwise perform itself. DraftKings will need to show that operating a designated contract market involves more than compliance with CFTC rules. Exchanges do carry self-regulatory duties under the Commodity Exchange Act, and that feature is likely to be the center of the briefing.

The federal question ground faces its own hurdle. Under the well-pleaded complaint rule, a case generally arises under federal law only if the plaintiff's own claims do, and a federal preemption defense ordinarily does not make a state-law case removable. The tribe's complaint is framed under Florida law and the compact. DraftKings may argue that the claims necessarily turn on a substantial federal question, but that path is narrow as well.

Why the forum matters

For DraftKings, the advantages of federal court are practical. Federal judges have been handling the wave of prediction-market cases, including the tribal suits against Kalshi in California and Wisconsin, and a federal forum offers a single body of appellate law in the Eleventh Circuit. Preemption under the Commodity Exchange Act is the company's core defense, and it would rather argue that question to a federal court.

For the tribe, the original choice of a state court was deliberate. The claims are framed under Florida law, the 2021 compact was ratified by the Legislature, and a state judge applying state gambling statutes is a natural forum for a declaratory judgment about what is and is not lawful sports betting in Florida. A remand would also keep the case off the federal track where several tribal plaintiffs have received mixed results. Our analysis of the diverging Kalshi rulings describes how those federal decisions have split.

The procedural stakes are unusually high because of how federal officer removal is treated on appeal. Most orders sending a case back to state court cannot be appealed. Removals under § 1442 are an exception, and after the Supreme Court's 2021 decision in BP p.l.c. v. Mayor and City Council of Baltimore, an appellate court reviewing such a remand order may consider every ground for removal the defendant raised. Even if the tribe wins a remand motion, DraftKings could appeal, which may delay any state-court ruling on the merits.

DraftKings has characterized the suit as retaliatory, saying the tribe filed about three weeks after DraftKings joined a separate state-court legal challenge and did not sue other event-contract or fantasy operators. Those are the company's assertions; the tribe has not adopted them.

The money behind the exclusivity claim

The case matters beyond one product because of what Florida's compact exchanges. In return for exclusive rights to online sports betting through Hard Rock Bet, delivered under a hub-and-spoke model with servers on tribal land, the tribe guaranteed the state at least $2.5 billion over the compact's first five years. The tribe paid Florida $1.04 billion in the fiscal year ending June 30, more than $200 million above the prior year, according to Florida coverage of state revenue figures, and state economists have identified sports betting as a driver of that growth. Our Seminole compact analysis walks through the payment structure.

Like many compacts, Florida's includes exclusivity protections that can reduce or suspend the tribe's payments if the state permits competing gaming. Whether a federally registered prediction market counts as an exclusivity breach by the state is a separate and unsettled question, but the possibility gives Florida a direct fiscal interest in the outcome. Gov. Ron DeSantis said after the suit was filed that he believed the compact clearly contemplated the tribe's exclusive ability to offer this kind of wagering in Florida, and he predicted that the larger question would be settled by federal appeals courts and possibly by Congress.

What to watch

The next filings will be procedural. The tribe has 30 days from removal to seek remand on procedural grounds, and it can contest subject-matter jurisdiction at any time. A remand motion would put the "acting under" question before the district court directly, making it the first test of whether an exchange relationship with the CFTC is enough to move a tribal gaming dispute into federal court. If DraftKings prevails, other prediction-market operators facing state-court suits from tribes or states may try the same route.
