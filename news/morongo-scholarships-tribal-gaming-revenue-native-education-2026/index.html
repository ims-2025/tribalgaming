Morongo Scholarships Show Where Tribal Gaming Revenue Is Legally Required to Go

A small announcement out of Riverside County is a clean illustration of a federal statute that tells tribes what gaming profits must fund.

By Marcus WhitehorsePublished September 07, 20265 min read

The Morongo Band of Mission Indians announced this month that four Native American students from California will each receive $10,000 scholarships funded through the tribe's charitable giving program. In dollar terms it is a modest announcement against a sector that generated a record $46.2 billion in gross gaming revenue in fiscal 2025. In structural terms it is an unusually clean illustration of how tribal gaming revenue is legally required to move, and of where a great deal of it actually ends up.

Morongo, which operates Morongo Casino Resort & Spa near Cabazon in Riverside County, is among the larger gaming tribes in California's tribal gaming market, the biggest tribal gaming state in the country. Its scholarship program is one line in a giving portfolio that tribal enterprises across Indian Country maintain, and that portfolio is not discretionary in the way a commercial operator's corporate social responsibility budget is.

The five permitted uses are the whole story

Section 11 of the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act does something no state gaming statute does to a commercial licensee: it dictates what net gaming revenues may be spent on. There are five permitted uses. Revenue may fund tribal government operations or programs; provide for the general welfare of the tribe and its members; promote tribal economic development; donate to charitable organizations; and help fund operations of local government agencies.

Education spending sits comfortably inside at least three of those categories at once. A scholarship for a tribal citizen is general welfare. A scholarship for Native students beyond the tribe's own citizenry is charitable donation. And a workforce that can staff a $46 billion industry with its own people is economic development. This is why education has become one of the most consistent destinations for tribal gaming dollars regardless of tribe size, geography or property scale. Our explainer on the five permitted uses of net gaming revenue walks through how the allocation framework works in practice.

Why scholarships, specifically

Tribal governments have practical reasons to favor education spending over almost any other charitable category. Scholarships are administratively simple, the outcomes are measurable, and unlike capital projects they do not create recurring maintenance obligations. They also address a structural problem the sector has been slow to solve on its own.

Tribal gaming employs hundreds of thousands of people, but the professional layer of the industry — surveillance directors, compliance officers, controllers, IT security leads, gaming commissioners — requires credentials that take years to build. Tribes that want those roles filled by their own citizens have to fund the pipeline a decade before the vacancy exists. Scholarship programs are the earliest link in that chain, feeding into the college and university partnerships that a growing number of enterprises now maintain. We covered that pipeline in our look at tribal gaming's higher education partnerships.

A tribe that wants its own citizens running the surveillance room in 2036 has to be paying tuition in 2026.

The scale problem nobody advertises

It would be misleading to present a four-scholarship announcement as representative of the sector. It is representative of what a successful gaming tribe in a large market can do. It is not representative of what most gaming tribes can do.

The distribution of tribal gaming revenue is severely concentrated. A relatively small number of operations in dense metropolitan catchments generate a disproportionate share of national revenue, while a long tail of properties in rural markets generate enough to fund basic government services and little more. A tribe operating a 300-machine facility three hours from the nearest interstate is not funding a scholarship program of any size; it is funding a health clinic, a water system and an elder-services budget, and often struggling to fund all three.

That gap is why charitable giving totals from the largest tribal enterprises, while genuinely substantial, should not be read as a proxy for the sector's overall philanthropic capacity. It is also why intertribal giving — larger gaming tribes funding programs that serve citizens of non-gaming or low-revenue tribes — has become a quiet but meaningful feature of Indian Country philanthropy. The Morongo announcement is an example: the recipients are Native students from across California, not exclusively Morongo citizens.

Charitable giving as a policy argument

There is also a political function to this spending, and tribal leaders are candid about it. Every legislative session in which tribal exclusivity is contested, every ballot campaign, every compact renegotiation produces the same question from lawmakers and voters: what does this money actually do?

Scholarships, health clinics, language immersion schools, fire departments and road maintenance are the answer, and they are an answer that survives scrutiny because the spending categories are federally prescribed and independently audited. Unlike a commercial operator's community relations budget, which can be cut in a bad quarter, IGRA's allocation framework is a legal constraint on where the money can go. That distinction has become central to how tribes argue their case in state capitols, particularly in California, where the tribal gaming economy is large enough that its downstream spending is a measurable factor in regional budgets.

What it signals for the fall

Announcements like Morongo's tend to cluster in late summer and early fall, timed to the academic calendar. Their aggregate significance is easy to underrate. Taken together across several hundred gaming tribes, education giving represents one of the largest privately funded scholarship channels available to Native students in the United States — and one that exists entirely because a 1988 federal statute told tribes what they had to do with gaming profits.
