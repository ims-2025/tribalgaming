Isleta Resort & Casino at 40: Albuquerque's Pueblo Gaming Milestones

From bingo tents in the 1980s to self-operated resorts, two pueblo anniversaries show a mature regional industry facing its next compact cycle.

By Sarah BegayPublished November 27, 20264 min read

The Pueblo of Isleta is marking 40 years of tribal gaming this fall, and Isleta Resort & Casino has grown from a bingo tent south of Albuquerque into one of New Mexico's largest pueblo-owned destinations. The anniversary arrives a few months after the neighboring Pueblo of Sandia celebrated 25 years at its current casino site. Together, the two milestones show how Albuquerque-area pueblos built a regional gaming economy that predates the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act and now underwrites much of their governments' work.

Isleta's anniversary coverage, published in September, describes a property that the pueblo wholly owns and operates without a third-party manager. Today it includes a casino floor, a hotel, a sportsbook, a poker room, bingo, the Isleta Showroom, a nightclub, multiple restaurants, a spa, the Isleta Golf Club, Isleta Lakes and an RV park. The pueblo is also the title sponsor of the Isleta New Mexico Bowl, the college football game played each December in Albuquerque, and backs a list of local sports and community events, including the Albuquerque International Balloon Fiesta.

From bingo halls to Isleta Resort & Casino

The origin story follows a familiar path in New Mexico. Like Sandia, Isleta began with high-stakes bingo in the mid-1980s. That was a period when tribes across the country were testing their authority to run games that state law regulated but did not prohibit. The Supreme Court's 1987 decision in California v. Cabazon Band of Mission Indians confirmed that authority, and Congress followed with IGRA in 1988, creating the Class II and Class III framework that still governs tribal gaming. The categories are explained in our Legal Guide to IGRA and Class III gaming.

New Mexico's path to Class III casino gaming was contested. Pueblos and the state fought through much of the 1990s over whether compacts signed by the governor without legislative approval were valid, and the state's first durable compacts were not settled until the late 1990s. The current generation of compacts, adopted beginning in 2015 and running through 2037, sets revenue-sharing rates on net slot win and gives participating tribes exclusivity over casino-style gaming, apart from the state's racetrack casinos. Those agreements are now back in the political conversation as New Mexico's governor-elect prepares to take office, as we noted in our look at the incoming administration and pueblo compacts.

Sandia's 25 years and a shared market

Sandia's milestone came in May. At an anniversary event reported by KOB and CDC Gaming, Sandia Governor Stuart Paisano traced the pueblo's gaming history back about 42 years to Sandia Indian Bingo, then a larger tent, and then the current property on Albuquerque's north side. The celebration launched roughly 150 days of promotions and events. Sandia's resort, with its hotel, golf course and amphitheater, sits on the northern edge of the metro area. Isleta anchors the south.

That geography has shaped both businesses. The Albuquerque metro area is New Mexico's only large population center, and several pueblos have casinos within reach of it, including Sandia, Isleta, Santa Ana and others along the Interstate 25 corridor toward Santa Fe. For decades the pueblos have competed for the same local customers while also cooperating on policy, from compact negotiations to litigation. Most recently, Sandia and Isleta were among the tribes that went to federal court over prediction-market sports contracts offered on tribal lands, a case covered in our report on the New Mexico injunction ruling.

Forty years in, the pueblo casinos around Albuquerque are less a collection of competitors than a mature regional industry, and their anniversaries mark how much of tribal self-government now rests on gaming revenue.

Why self-operation matters

One detail in the Isleta anniversary coverage deserves attention: the pueblo runs the resort itself. Many tribal casinos opened in the 1990s under management contracts with outside companies, which IGRA caps at seven years and a share of net revenues, and which the National Indian Gaming Commission must approve. A long trend in the industry has been tribes buying out or letting those contracts expire and moving to tribal management, building in-house executive teams and regulatory staff. Isleta's anniversary materials named directors in table games and marketing, a reminder that a 40-year-old tribal casino is also a 40-year-old employer and training ground.

Self-operation also changes where the money goes. Without a management fee, more of net gaming revenue stays with the tribal government. Under IGRA, that revenue can be used only for government operations, member welfare, economic development, charitable donations and support for local government agencies. For pueblos with land bases close to a major city, it has funded housing, health and education programs, as well as investment in golf, hospitality and outdoor recreation that diversifies the reservation economy.

What the milestones signal

Anniversaries are partly marketing, and both pueblos have used theirs to promote events and loyalty offers. They also mark a policy point. The New Mexico pueblos that started with bingo tents in the 1980s have now operated full-scale casinos for roughly three decades under state compacts. They face questions that the first generation of operators did not: how to respond to online wagering that reaches their customers from outside the reservation, how to approach the next compact cycle under a new governor, and how to reinvest in properties that are no longer new.
