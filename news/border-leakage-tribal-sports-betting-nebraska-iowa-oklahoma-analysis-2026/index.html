Border Leakage: Why Sports Betting Dollars Cross Tribal Market Lines

Nebraska's Omaha bettors cross into Iowa. Oklahoma is ringed by legal states. Two very different markets reach the same economic problem.

By James KingfisherPublished November 07, 20265 min read

Border leakage describes the revenue a state loses when residents travel, or use apps, to place bets in a neighboring jurisdiction. It has become the central economic argument in two tribal-adjacent sports betting debates this year: Nebraska, where voters rejected online wagering on November 3, and Oklahoma, where a mobile betting bill stalled in April. The two markets are structurally different, yet each illustrates how geography, rather than any single law, determines where sports betting revenue lands.

Nebraska and the Iowa river crossing

Nebraska's in-person sports betting is legal at racetrack casinos, including those operated by the tribally owned WarHorse Gaming. Mobile wagering is not. Following the vote, coverage noted that Iowa is the main beneficiary of the result, since residents of Omaha and Bellevue can cross the river and bet on a phone in a state where mobile wagering is legal. Missouri, Kansas, Colorado and Wyoming also offer online sports betting, so Nebraska is bordered by legal mobile markets on nearly every side.

Supporters of the failed measures cited an estimate from a state senator that Nebraska forfeits roughly $32 million a year in tax revenue without online betting. That figure comes from a proponent and should be read as an advocate's estimate, not an audited number. The underlying point is nonetheless sound: when a population center sits a short drive from a legal market, the policy choice is not between betting and no betting but between taxed and untaxed betting. Our coverage of the Nebraska result details how voters weighed that tradeoff and rejected it.

Oklahoma: surrounded, but exclusive

Oklahoma's situation reverses the geography. It has no mobile sports betting at all, while four neighbors, Kansas, Missouri, Colorado and Arkansas, have statewide legal sportsbooks. Oklahoma's tribes hold gaming exclusivity under the state's model compact and pay the state exclusivity fees in return. Because tribal sports betting requires compact amendments and federal approval, a bill like HB 1047 could not simply authorize commercial operators. It had to route betting through tribes.

The sponsor of that bill has argued that Oklahomans already bet through unregulated channels and that the state captures nothing from it. He has also acknowledged that prediction market apps, which operate statewide, have a competitive advantage over any tribal-only product constrained by reservation boundaries. That point links border leakage to a second, less visible kind: leakage not across a state line but across a legal category. A bettor in Ardmore who uses a prediction market app is no more captured by Oklahoma's tribal system than one who drives to Kansas. We explore the related Texas dynamic in our analysis of cross-border demand and Oklahoma's gaming ceiling.

Why geography complicates the tribal model

Tribal sports betting in compact states usually follows a hub-and-spoke or on-reservation-server design, in which the bet is deemed to occur where the server sits. As we explain in our hub-and-spoke explainer, that legal fiction lets tribes serve bettors statewide while keeping the transaction on Indian lands. It works within a state. It does nothing to stop a bettor from opening a competing app in the next state over.

In Oklahoma, the proposal discussed for the 2027 session would divide the state geographically, with some tiers limited to a tribe's historic reservation. That boundary logic creates an internal version of the border problem: an app partnered with one nation might not work for a bettor in another nation's territory. Internal borders, in effect, can leak as much as state borders do. Readers can compare how different states structure these markets using our comparison tools.

What the two cases suggest

First, leakage arguments are persuasive to legislators but have not been decisive with voters. Nebraska voters heard the revenue-leakage case, backed by tens of millions of dollars in campaign spending, and still said no. Second, exclusivity is valuable precisely because it limits competition, so tribes in exclusive states have little incentive to support designs that reduce their territorial advantage in exchange for broader reach. Third, prediction markets complicate every calculation, because they sidestep both state boundaries and compact categories, which is why tribal organizations have turned to the courts.

None of this makes leakage a decisive factor in every market. Where a state has a single large metro on a border, as Nebraska does, the pressure is acute. Where a state has multiple regional markets and tribal exclusivity, as Oklahoma does, the question is whether to protect existing compact value or chase new volume. Both states will keep wrestling with it, and tribal operators on either side of a border will keep watching which way the money flows.

A further complication is measurement. Neither state publishes a definitive figure for how much betting handle leaves its borders, and the numbers cited in public debate typically come from advocates, consultants or operator surveys. That does not make the underlying phenomenon less real, but it counsels caution in evaluating any single dollar estimate. Policymakers and tribal gaming boards would be better served by independent studies that track licensed handle, geolocation data and illegal-market estimates over time, and that separate Omaha-style metropolitan spillover from the more diffuse leakage that occurs in rural counties far from any border.

Tribal operators have their own tools for responding. Players-club integration, retail sportsbook experiences and hospitality packages can keep bettors loyal to a property even when a competing app is available. That is one reason several tribal operators describe their sportsbooks as an amenity that supports the casino floor, rather than as a stand-alone profit center, an argument we examined in our coverage of tribal sportsbook economics.
