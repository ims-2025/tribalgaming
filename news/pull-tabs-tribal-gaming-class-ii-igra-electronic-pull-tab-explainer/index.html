Pull-Tabs in Tribal Gaming: How IGRA Classifies Paper and Electronic Tabs

A paper ticket and a screen of pull-tab symbols can look alike to a player. Under federal law, they may fall in different classes.

By Daniel YazziePublished November 13, 20264 min read

Pull-tabs are among the oldest and least glamorous games in tribal gaming, and among the most legally interesting. A paper ticket with perforated windows that reveal symbols when pulled open, the pull-tab sits in a specific place in federal law. Understanding pull-tabs in tribal gaming requires looking at how the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act classifies them, why that classification depends on bingo, and how courts have drawn the line when pull-tabs move onto a screen.

Where pull-tabs sit under IGRA

IGRA divides tribal gaming into three classes. Class I covers social games for minimal prizes and traditional ceremonial gaming. Class III covers casino-style gaming, including slot machines and house-banked table games, and requires a tribal-state compact. Class II sits between them. It is regulated by tribes with oversight from the National Indian Gaming Commission, and it does not require a compact. Our Class II vs. Class III explainer covers the overall framework.

The statute's definition of Class II gaming begins with bingo, whether or not electronic, computer, or other technological aids are used. It then adds, "if played in the same location," pull-tabs, lotto, punch boards, tip jars, instant bingo, and other games similar to bingo. That phrase is the key to pull-tab classification. Under the statute's text, pull-tabs qualify as Class II when they are played at the same location as bingo. A tribe offering pull-tabs as part of a bingo operation is in Class II territory; a free-standing pull-tab operation raises different questions.

Class II status has practical consequences. A tribe can offer Class II games in any state that permits such gaming for any purpose by any person, without negotiating a compact and without paying revenue sharing to the state. Class II gaming also carries its own NIGC fee, regulatory, and licensing requirements. The TribalGaming.com Legal Guide summarizes those obligations.

Paper versus electronic pull-tabs

A traditional pull-tab deal is a finite set of tickets with a fixed number of winners. The outcome of each ticket is determined when the deal is printed, not when it is opened, which is part of what distinguishes it from a slot machine. Players buy tickets from a dispenser or clerk, open them, and redeem winners.

The legal complexity arrives when the game is moved onto a machine. IGRA allows Class II games to be played with "electronic, computer, or other technological aids," but it excludes from Class II "electronic or electromechanical facsimiles of any game of chance or slot machines of any kind." An electronic pull-tab device can therefore be either a Class II technological aid or a Class III facsimile, depending on how it works.

The distinction is between a machine that helps a player play a pull-tab game and a machine that has become the game.

Two federal appellate decisions from the D.C. Circuit framed this line in the 1990s. In Cabazon Band of Mission Indians v. National Indian Gaming Commission (1994), the court held that a computerized pull-tab game in which the electronic device itself constituted the game was a facsimile, and therefore Class III. In Diamond Game Enterprises v. Reno (2000), the court reached a different result for a device that dispensed and read paper pull-tabs from a finite deal, treating it as a technological aid and therefore Class II. The paper ticket remained the game; the machine assisted the player.

Those cases, together with NIGC regulations, shape how manufacturers design Class II devices and how tribal regulators evaluate them. The NIGC's regulations include definitions of technological aids and facsimiles, and minimum technical standards for Class II gaming systems. Our explainer on how Class II bingo machines work covers the bingo side of the same question, and the Johnson Act explainer covers the federal gambling-device statute that IGRA interacts with.

Why pull-tabs still matter

Pull-tabs remain part of tribal gaming for several reasons. They are inexpensive to operate, familiar to customers in regions with a charitable gaming tradition, and suited to smaller properties and bingo halls. In the Upper Midwest, paper pull-tabs have long been a staple of charitable gaming in bars and clubs, and tribal bingo halls in the region have historically offered them alongside bingo.

For tribes in states where Class III compacts are unavailable or difficult to obtain, the Class II category, including pull-tabs, can be a meaningful gaming option. The same "same location" and "technological aid" questions that shaped pull-tab case law also shape the broader Class II machine market, which has grown in states without compacts.

Pull-tabs also illustrate a recurring theme in tribal gaming law: classification turns on how a game actually operates, not on its name or appearance. A paper ticket in a dispenser and a screen showing pull-tab symbols can look similar to a customer. Under IGRA they may sit in different classes, with different regulators, different approval paths, and different economics.

Key points

Pull-tabs are listed in IGRA's Class II definition when played at the same location as bingo. Class II gaming is tribally regulated with NIGC oversight and does not require a compact. Electronic devices that assist play of a paper pull-tab deal can qualify as Class II technological aids, while devices that become the game itself have been treated as Class III facsimiles. Tribes considering pull-tab products typically rely on their tribal gaming regulatory authority and, where appropriate, NIGC guidance on game classification before deployment.
