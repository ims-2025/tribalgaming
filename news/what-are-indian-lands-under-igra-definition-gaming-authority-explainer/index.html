What Are Indian Lands Under IGRA? How the Definition Controls Tribal Gaming

The statute's definition of Indian lands is narrower than most people assume, and it now decides technology disputes as well as casino siting.

By Sarah BegayPublished November 09, 20265 min read

Every tribal gaming question eventually returns to a place. Whether a casino may offer Class III games, whether a state may tax or regulate it, whether a court order against an online platform reaches a particular parcel, and whether a tribe can open a second facility all depend on whether the land qualifies as "Indian lands" under the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act of 1988. The phrase is a defined term in the statute, and its meaning is narrower and more technical than the everyday idea of tribal land. This explainer sets out what IGRA means by Indian lands, why the definition matters, and where disputes tend to arise.

The statutory definition of Indian lands

IGRA defines Indian lands in two parts. The first covers all lands within the limits of any Indian reservation. The second covers lands the title to which is either held in trust by the United States for the benefit of an Indian tribe or individual, or held by a tribe or individual subject to a restriction by the United States against alienation, and over which an Indian tribe exercises governmental power. The second part contains a two-step test: the land must have a qualifying federal status, and the tribe must actually exercise governmental power over it. Owning a parcel outright in the ordinary commercial sense, known as fee land, does not meet the definition unless it falls within reservation limits or has been placed in trust.

Our explainer on fee land versus trust land walks through that distinction, and our piece on land into trust and Section 20 covers how land acquired after 1988 may become eligible for gaming.

Why the definition controls gaming authority

IGRA's framework attaches to Indian lands in several ways. Class II gaming, such as bingo and games similar to bingo, is within a tribe's jurisdiction on Indian lands if the state permits such gaming for any purpose by any person and the tribe adopts an ordinance approved by the National Indian Gaming Commission. Class III gaming, which includes slot machines and banked card games, is lawful on Indian lands only if it is authorized by a tribal ordinance, located in a state that permits such gaming for any purpose, and conducted in conformance with a tribal-state compact. Both frameworks speak of gaming "on Indian lands." If the land does not qualify, IGRA's structure does not apply to it, and the tribe's activity there is governed by other law. Our guides to Class II versus Class III gaming and to the scope-of-gaming doctrine explain those categories.

The definition also determines where tribal regulators have primary responsibility. A tribal gaming commission regulates gaming on the tribe's Indian lands, the National Indian Gaming Commission supervises under IGRA, and a state's role is limited to what the compact provides. Outside Indian lands, a tribal enterprise is typically treated like any other business subject to the laws of the jurisdiction where it operates, which is why tribal commercial ventures beyond reservation boundaries face different rules, a theme in our analysis of tribal enterprises beyond Indian lands.

The after-1988 land restriction

IGRA generally prohibits gaming on land acquired in trust after October 17, 1988, with several exceptions. Those exceptions include land taken into trust as part of a settlement of a land claim, as part of the initial reservation of a tribe acknowledged through the federal process, or as part of the restoration of lands for a tribe restored to federal recognition. A further route allows gaming on off-reservation trust land when the Secretary of the Interior determines that gaming would be in the best interest of the tribe and not detrimental to the surrounding community, and the governor of the state concurs. The governor's role in that route is examined in our explainer on governor concurrence, and the restored-lands exception in our Section 20 exceptions guide.

These rules explain why many proposals turn on the eligibility of a specific parcel. A tribe may hold land in trust for housing or government services that cannot host a casino, while another parcel only a few miles away might qualify under an exception. Eligibility determinations are typically contested by neighboring tribes, local governments and state officials, and they are often litigated.

Where Indian lands now matter in new ways

The definition has recently moved from real-estate disputes into technology litigation. In a case brought by Blue Lake Rancheria and Chicken Ranch Rancheria against the prediction-market operator Kalshi, the Ninth Circuit held in September 2026 that sports event contracts placed from tribal land are likely Class III gaming under IGRA. A federal district court then enjoined Kalshi from offering sports contracts on the two tribes' Indian lands, and, according to press reports, ordered the tribes to supply maps of the affected territories by November 13. Our report on the Ninth Circuit ruling explains the holding.

The case illustrates how the statutory definition becomes operational. A bet placed on a phone is "on Indian lands" if the bettor is physically within a qualifying parcel, which means a platform seeking to comply must translate a legal definition into geographic data. Questions that arise include how to treat parcels held in trust but not within a reservation, checkerboarded areas where tribal and non-tribal ownership alternate, and lands under pending trust applications. The same location-based logic underlies tribal mobile wagering models; see our analysis of on-reservation geofenced mobile gaming.

Practical takeaways

For tribal governments, accurate and current land records are not an administrative nicety but a legal asset. Documentation of trust status, reservation boundaries and the exercise of governmental power supports gaming authority, compact negotiations and enforcement actions alike. For operators and vendors, confirming that a facility or activity sits on qualifying Indian lands is a threshold diligence step. For the public, the definition explains why two nearby properties can face entirely different legal regimes.
