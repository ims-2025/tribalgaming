Interior Withdraws Keetoowah Opinion, Narrowing Band's Gaming Path

A struck solicitor's opinion sends the band back to parcel-by-parcel Indian lands analysis — and an Oklahoma compact the state calls invalid.

By Marcus WhitehorsePublished August 17, 20264 min read

The U.S. Department of the Interior has permanently withdrawn a solicitor's opinion that described the United Keetoowah Band of Cherokee Indians as a co-successor to the Cherokee reservation in northeastern Oklahoma, removing the strongest legal foundation the band had assembled for United Keetoowah Band gaming on trust land. The withdrawal, issued on May 22, 2026, overrides the earlier conclusion in its entirety and returns the question of the band's territorial jurisdiction to roughly where it stood before January 2025.

The opinion at issue was signed in the closing days of the previous administration. It concluded that the UKB, a federally recognized tribe headquartered in Tahlequah, held status as an equal successor to the historic Cherokee reservation for certain land-acquisition purposes. Read alongside the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act, that conclusion implied the band could exercise governmental authority over its own trust parcels inside the reservation boundary — and, in turn, that those parcels might qualify as Indian lands eligible for casino gaming. The document was suspended in February 2025 pending review. The May 2026 action makes the suspension permanent and replaces the analysis outright.

What the reversal means for United Keetoowah Band gaming

The practical effect is procedural rather than punitive. Nothing in the withdrawal alters the band's federal recognition, its government-to-government relationship with the United States, or its existing enterprises. What it does is reinstate a considerably harder path to a Class III casino.

Under IGRA, Class III gaming requires Indian lands over which the tribe exercises governmental power, plus a compact with the state or federally issued procedures. For a tribe without an undisputed reservation of its own, that ordinarily means placing land into trust and then satisfying one of the narrow exceptions Congress wrote for land acquired after October 17, 1988. Those exceptions — restored lands, initial reservation, settlement of a land claim, or a two-part determination requiring the governor's concurrence — are the same bottleneck facing dozens of tribes nationwide, and readers can follow the mechanics in our explainer on Section 20 gaming eligibility.

The shared-reservation theory would have let the band bypass much of that analysis by treating its parcels as already sitting inside a reservation it helped hold. With the opinion struck, the band is back to arguing eligibility parcel by parcel, on a record the Interior Department has now twice declined to endorse.

UKB leadership has signaled it does not regard the matter as closed. Chief Jeff Wacoche has described trust lands for gaming as a treaty-based right the band intends to defend, and the tribe has said it will pursue every avenue available under law. Those avenues realistically include federal litigation challenging the withdrawal, a renewed fee-to-trust application built on a different theory, or a legislative fix — each of which operates on a multi-year clock.

Oklahoma's parallel objection

The federal reversal compounds a state-level obstacle. In April 2025 the band executed Oklahoma's model gaming compact and submitted it to the Interior Department for review. Two months later the Oklahoma attorney general issued a formal opinion concluding that the compact was invalid. The opinion did not dispute the band's recognition; it questioned whether the model compact, which the state has executed with dozens of tribes, could validly attach to a tribe whose eligible gaming lands were themselves unsettled.

That leaves the band in an unusual bind. A compact is worth little without qualifying Indian lands, and qualifying Indian lands are worth little without a compact or secretarial procedures. Oklahoma remains the largest tribal gaming market in the country, with roughly 140 facilities operated by more than 30 tribes, and the state's compacting posture carries weight well beyond this dispute — see our Oklahoma state hub for the current operator landscape.

A jurisdictional dispute older than IGRA

The friction between the Cherokee Nation and the United Keetoowah Band predates commercial casino gaming by decades. Both governments trace to the same historic Cherokee community, both are federally recognized, and both claim institutional continuity with pre-removal and post-removal Cherokee governance. Gaming has raised the stakes of a question that was already unresolved: whether one reservation can host two governments with concurrent authority.

Interior's withdrawal does not decide who the Cherokee people are. It decides, for now, which government exercises jurisdiction over a specific set of parcels — and that narrower question is what IGRA actually turns on.

Cherokee Nation leadership welcomed the action, characterizing the 2025 opinion as inconsistent with the legal and factual record and noting that it had been suspended almost immediately after issuance. The nation operates one of the largest tribal gaming enterprises in the United States and has consistently opposed any reading of federal law that would create overlapping jurisdiction inside its reservation boundary.

What to watch next

Three developments would change the picture. First, any UKB filing in federal district court challenging the withdrawal as arbitrary or contrary to treaty obligations; solicitor's opinions are internal guidance rather than final agency action, which complicates but does not foreclose review. Second, movement on a fee-to-trust application resting on the restored-lands or initial-reservation exception rather than on shared jurisdiction. Third, congressional attention, which has occasionally resolved comparable jurisdictional standoffs where the agencies could not.

For the wider industry, the episode is a reminder of how much of tribal gaming's legal architecture sits in documents that can be reversed by a successor administration. Solicitor's opinions, land determinations, and even long-standing interpretive guidance have proven unusually mobile since 2021. Operators planning capital around a favorable federal reading — and lenders underwriting it — have started pricing that volatility in.
