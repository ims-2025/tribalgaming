Judge Orders Kalshi to Block Sports Contracts on New Mexico Tribal Lands

Judge Margaret Strickland found four tribal nations likely to win their IGRA claims and gave Kalshi 14 days to geofence its sports markets off their lands.

By Elena RuizPublished November 25, 20264 min read

A federal judge in New Mexico has granted a Kalshi injunction covering New Mexico tribal lands, ordering the prediction-market operator to stop offering its sports event contracts on the reservations of four tribal nations that sued it in May. U.S. District Judge Margaret Strickland ruled on Monday, Nov. 23, that the Mescalero Apache Tribe and the Pueblos of Isleta, Pojoaque and Sandia are likely to succeed on their claims, and she gave Kalshi 14 days to block access to its sports markets on their lands.

The decision is the second tribal preliminary injunction against Kalshi this month. It arrives as the Commodity Futures Trading Commission presses federally registered exchanges to drop geographic trading restrictions, a directive that sits uneasily beside a growing number of court orders requiring exactly those restrictions.

What the court decided in the Kalshi injunction

The four tribes filed suit in May, arguing that Kalshi's sports contracts amount to unauthorized wagering on Indian lands in violation of the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act and of the tribal-state gaming compacts each tribe holds with New Mexico. Our earlier coverage traced the case from the initial complaint through the closing arguments heard in September.

According to reporting on the order, Strickland rejected Kalshi's central defense: that the Commodity Exchange Act, which gives the CFTC exclusive jurisdiction over contracts traded on registered exchanges, shields prediction markets from tribal gambling restrictions. She also denied Kalshi's motion to dismiss the case.

On harm, the court found that Kalshi's operations infringe the tribes' sovereign authority to regulate gaming on their own lands, an injury that money damages cannot repair. The evidentiary record included roughly 30,000 visits to Kalshi's website over a three-week period originating from Wi-Fi networks at casinos and resorts operated by three of the plaintiff tribes.

Kalshi had argued that because its servers and operations sit off reservation, it is beyond tribal authority. The judge disagreed, reasoning that a person who places a sports contract while on tribal land is in the same position as a bettor at the casino counter: the wager happens where the bettor is. That location-based reading tracks the approach many states and tribes already use to regulate mobile sports wagering.

The court noted that Kalshi already uses geofencing in other contexts, and weighed that capability against the company's claimed compliance burden.

Kalshi also cited CFTC requirements that registered exchanges provide impartial market access. The court was not persuaded that those rules outweighed the tribes' interest in enforcing IGRA and their compacts.

How the ruling fits the national picture

The New Mexico order follows a similar injunction issued earlier in November for California tribes, covered in our analysis of the Blue Lake and Chicken Ranch compliance deadlines. Not every tribe has prevailed at this stage. In Wisconsin, a federal judge earlier this year denied the Ho-Chunk Nation's request for an injunction even while finding the tribe likely to succeed on the merits, a result reporting attributed to the specific language of that tribe's compact. We examined that split in tribal Kalshi rulings diverge.

The federal regulatory backdrop is moving in the opposite direction. On Nov. 5, the CFTC issued guidance directing registered exchanges to remove domestic geographic trading restrictions within 30 days or explain why compliance is not feasible. Nine Democratic senators, led by Sen. Adam Schiff of California, have asked the agency to withdraw that directive, arguing it conflicts with court orders, as detailed in our report on the senators' letter to the CFTC. The CFTC has also sued a dozen states to block enforcement of state gambling laws against federally regulated exchanges.

On the same day as the New Mexico ruling, a New York judge denied a request by state Attorney General Letitia James for a temporary restraining order against Kalshi, a request the company had warned could shut down its exchange nationwide. The contrast underlines how narrowly tailored the tribal injunctions are: they reach specific reservations, not the platform as a whole.

What the order means for New Mexico tribal gaming

The plaintiff tribes operate some of New Mexico's best-known properties, including Inn of the Mountain Gods in Mescalero, Isleta Resort & Casino and Sandia Resort & Casino on the Albuquerque metro edge, and Buffalo Thunder Resort & Casino north of Santa Fe. New Mexico's tribal compacts have permitted on-premises sports wagering since 2018, when the first tribal sportsbooks opened under existing Class III authority. There is no statewide mobile sports-betting market, which is why the tribes view app-based sports contracts reachable from their own gaming floors as a direct incursion on their exclusive gaming rights. Our New Mexico tribal gaming market deep dive describes that structure in detail.

Practically, the order requires Kalshi to geofence the four reservations within 14 days, a deadline that falls in early December and roughly coincides with the end of the CFTC's 30-day window. Any appeal would go to the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit, which has not yet weighed in on the prediction-market question; reporting on the ruling did not indicate whether Kalshi intends to seek a stay.

The preliminary injunction is not a final judgment. The case now proceeds on the merits, and the broader question of whether federal commodities law displaces IGRA is also headed toward the U.S. Supreme Court through separate state-led petitions.
