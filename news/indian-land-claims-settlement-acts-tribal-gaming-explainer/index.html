How Land Claims Settlement Acts Shape Tribal Gaming Rights: Explainer

From Mashantucket to Maine, the jurisdictional language Congress wrote into land claim settlements still decides where tribes can game.

By Linda CardinalPublished November 19, 20264 min read

Some of the most consequential rules in tribal gaming were not written in the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act at all. They were written years earlier, or in parallel, in federal land claims settlement acts: statutes Congress passed to resolve tribal claims that land had been taken unlawfully, usually in violation of the 1790 Trade and Intercourse Act's requirement of federal approval for transfers of Indian land. How land claims settlement acts interact with tribal gaming explains why some tribes operate among the largest casinos in North America, why others are barred from IGRA entirely, and why disputes over decades-old settlement funds still reach federal court today.

Where the settlement acts came from

Beginning in the 1970s, tribes in the East brought suits arguing that state and private acquisitions of their land had never received the congressional consent the Trade and Intercourse Act required. The Supreme Court's 1974 decision allowing the Oneida Indian Nation's claim to proceed in federal court made those suits credible, and the prospect of clouded title over large areas pushed states, landowners and Congress toward negotiated settlements. The resulting statutes typically extinguished the claims in exchange for money, a land base, federal recognition, or some combination. Many also specified, in detail, how state law would apply on the tribe's lands.

Those jurisdictional terms are what matter for gaming. When IGRA was enacted in 1988, it applied to "Indian lands" as the statute defines them. Whether a settlement act's jurisdictional terms survive IGRA, or whether IGRA displaces them, has been litigated tribe by tribe. The answers vary widely, and our explainer on what counts as Indian lands under IGRA covers the baseline definition.

Settlements that enabled gaming

The clearest example is Connecticut. The Mashantucket Pequot Indian Claims Settlement Act of 1983 resolved the tribe's land claim, extended federal recognition and created a fund for land acquisition, giving the tribe a trust land base in southeastern Connecticut. That land became the site of a bingo operation in the 1980s and, after IGRA, the Foxwoods resort. The Mohegan Nation of Connecticut Land Claims Settlement Act of 1994 followed a similar path, and Mohegan Sun opened two years later. Neither statute contemplated the scale of what followed, but both supplied the land and federal status on which IGRA could operate. Our Connecticut state hub profiles both properties.

New York's Seneca Nation Settlement Act of 1990 is a different model. It resolved disputes over leases on Seneca land in and around Salamanca and allowed land the Nation acquired with settlement funds, in defined areas, to be held in restricted fee status with the Secretary of the Interior's agreement. Restricted fee land remains owned by the Nation but is protected from alienation and treated as Indian country. That provision later supported the Nation's casino parcels in Niagara Falls and Buffalo, and it remains contested: New York filed suit in November 2026 arguing that the Act's acquisition funds are exhausted, as covered in our report on the state's Grand Island lawsuit.

Settlements that limited gaming

Other settlement acts had the opposite effect. The Maine Indian Claims Settlement Act of 1980 subjected the Passamaquoddy Tribe, Penobscot Nation and other Wabanaki Nations largely to state law and provided that later federal laws benefiting tribes would not apply in Maine unless Congress specifically said so. A federal appeals court held in 1996 that IGRA was one such law. Maine's tribes therefore cannot open IGRA casinos; their recent gains in online sports wagering and iGaming came through state legislation rather than federal gaming law.

In Rhode Island, the Narragansett Indian Tribe's 1978 settlement act was initially read to permit IGRA gaming, but Congress amended it in 1996 to provide that the settlement lands would not be treated as Indian lands for IGRA purposes. The Catawba Indian Nation's 1993 settlement act likewise provided that IGRA would not apply to the tribe in South Carolina, leaving its gaming there to state law. The Catawba's casino in Kings Mountain, North Carolina, operates under IGRA because that parcel was taken into trust in North Carolina in 2020, outside the settlement act's South Carolina framework. Coverage of that project is collected in our reporting on Catawba Two Kings.

The same kind of statute, a congressional settlement of a land claim, produced Foxwoods in one state and a statutory bar on IGRA gaming in another. The difference lies entirely in the jurisdictional language Congress chose.

IGRA's own settlement exception

IGRA addresses land claims settlements directly in one place. Section 20 generally prohibits gaming on land taken into trust after October 17, 1988, but it contains exceptions, including for lands taken into trust as part of the settlement of a land claim. That exception means a future settlement can serve as a pathway to gaming eligibility without the two-part determination and governor concurrence that off-reservation acquisitions otherwise require. Our explainer on IGRA Section 20 exceptions compares it with the restored lands and initial reservation exceptions.

The settlement exception is narrower than it sounds. It applies only to land actually taken into trust as part of a settlement, and courts and Interior have examined closely whether a given statute or agreement is a land claim settlement at all. Restoration acts, which reestablish a terminated tribe's federal relationship, are a related but distinct category with their own jurisdictional terms, as the Supreme Court's 2022 decision in Ysleta del Sur Pueblo v. Texas illustrated.

Why this history still matters

Settlement acts are not historical artifacts. They continue to determine where tribes can game, what kind of land status their acquisitions receive, and how much authority states retain. When a tribe's gaming rights depend on a settlement act, disputes about the act's funds, geography or jurisdictional language can carry stakes comparable to a compact negotiation.
