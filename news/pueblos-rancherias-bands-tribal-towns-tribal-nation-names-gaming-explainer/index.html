Pueblos, Rancherias, Bands and Tribal Towns: What Tribal Names Mean

IGRA treats every federally recognized tribe the same. The names reveal history, land base and governance that shape each nation's gaming story.

By Linda CardinalPublished November 23, 20264 min read

Read a list of tribal gaming operators and the names vary widely: the Pueblo of Sandia, the Blue Lake Rancheria, the Mille Lacs Band of Ojibwe, Thlopthlocco Tribal Town, the Gila River Indian Community, the Navajo Nation. These tribal nation names are not interchangeable labels. Each reflects a particular history of treaties, land policy and self-government, and those histories often explain why a tribe's gaming operation looks the way it does.

For legal purposes, the differences largely disappear. The Indian Gaming Regulatory Act defines an "Indian tribe" as any tribe, band, nation, or other organized group or community of Indians that the Secretary of the Interior recognizes as eligible for federal Indian programs and as possessing powers of self-government. A rancheria and a nation stand on the same footing under IGRA. Our explainer on federal recognition and gaming eligibility covers that threshold.

Pueblos

The Pueblos of the Southwest are among the oldest continuously inhabited communities in North America. Nineteen Pueblos are located in New Mexico, and Ysleta del Sur Pueblo is in Texas. Their land tenure traces in part to Spanish-era grants later recognized by the United States, which is one reason Pueblo lands have a distinct legal history. Many Pueblos retain traditional governing structures, often with a governor and council selected under customary processes.

Several Pueblos operate major casinos along the Rio Grande corridor, as described in our New Mexico market deep dive. Ysleta del Sur's path has been different: its gaming rights are shaped by a 1987 restoration act, which the Supreme Court interpreted in 2022, as explained in our Ysleta del Sur Pueblo v. Texas explainer.

Rancherias

Rancheria is a term largely specific to California. In the early twentieth century, the federal government acquired small parcels for California Indians who had been left landless after unratified treaties and the dispossession that followed statehood. Many rancherias were only a few acres to a few dozen acres.

The California Rancheria Termination Act of 1958 ended federal recognition for dozens of rancherias, distributing their lands to individuals. Litigation and later legislation reversed much of that policy; the 1983 settlement in Tillie Hardwick v. United States restored federal status to seventeen rancherias, and Congress restored others individually. That history matters for gaming because restored tribes may qualify for IGRA's "restored lands" exception when acquiring new trust land, a question that has shaped several California casino projects. The Picayune Rancheria of the Chukchansi Indians, the Chicken Ranch Rancheria and the Blue Lake Rancheria are among the gaming tribes that carry the name.

Bands

Band is one of the most common terms in federal Indian affairs. It often reflects historical treaty-era designations, in which federal negotiators identified groups by geography or political organization within a larger people. The Mille Lacs Band of Ojibwe in Minnesota, the Eastern Band of Cherokee Indians in North Carolina, the Mississippi Band of Choctaw Indians and the Little River Band of Ottawa Indians in Michigan are examples. A band is fully sovereign in its own right; the name does not imply that it is a subdivision of another tribe.

Tribal towns

Tribal towns are an Oklahoma-specific form rooted in Muscogee (Creek) political tradition, in which towns were the basic unit of governance. Three Creek towns, the Alabama-Quassarte Tribal Town, the Kialegee Tribal Town and the Thlopthlocco Tribal Town, organized under the Oklahoma Indian Welfare Act of 1936 and are federally recognized separately from the Muscogee Nation. Because their members and lands are often within or near the Muscogee reservation, tribal towns have faced distinctive questions about which lands qualify as their Indian lands for gaming purposes. Our coverage of recent Kialegee and UKB compact approvals touches on those issues.

Indian communities and nations

The term Indian Community appears frequently among tribes that organized constitutions under the Indian Reorganization Act of 1934, including the Gila River Indian Community and the Salt River Pima-Maricopa Indian Community in Arizona, the Shakopee Mdewakanton Sioux Community in Minnesota, and the Bay Mills Indian Community in Michigan, whose name is attached to a leading Supreme Court case on tribal sovereign immunity.

Nation, meanwhile, is increasingly a deliberate choice. Tribes such as the Navajo Nation, the Cherokee Nation and the Seneca Nation use the term to emphasize their status as sovereign governments, and several tribes have formally changed their names in recent years to adopt it or to restore a name in their own language.

A note on Canada

In Canada, the Indian Act uses the term band, and most bands now call themselves First Nations, a term in wide use since the 1980s. First Nations gaming operates under provincial authority rather than a federal statute like IGRA, often through organizations such as the Saskatchewan Indian Gaming Authority. Our comparison of U.S. and Canadian legal models describes those differences.

Why the names matter

In everyday coverage, it is tempting to treat these names as stylistic. They are better understood as a short history of each nation's relationship with the United States: whether its land base came from treaty, grant or federal purchase; whether its recognition was ever terminated and restored; and how its government is organized. Those facts frequently determine which land qualifies for gaming, how a tribe negotiates a compact and how it governs its enterprise.
