Two Tribal Casino Proposals Along Idaho's I-84: How Approval Would Work

The governor's office has described, for the first time, the steps the state would take if either off-reservation proposal clears federal review.

By Elena RuizPublished October 15, 20264 min read

Two separate groups of Idaho tribes are pursuing casinos along the Interstate 84 corridor southeast of Boise, and neither project has yet cleared the federal review that would allow construction to begin. New reporting from BoiseDev, which includes the first on-the-record description from the governor's office of how the state would participate, offers a useful look at the multi-step approval path that off-reservation tribal casino proposals must follow under the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act (IGRA). For anyone tracking the question of whether a tribal casino near Idaho's largest metro area will be built, the process matters as much as the plans.

Two proposals, one corridor

The first proposal comes from the Shoshone-Paiute Tribes of the Duck Valley reservation, which straddles the Idaho-Nevada border, and the Coeur d'Alene Tribe. They are seeking to place a casino on 557 acres along I-84 at Simco Road, less than 20 minutes from Boise by car. A fee-to-trust application was submitted to the Department of the Interior in September 2025. New renderings show a six-story, glass-clad hotel with an attached, shorter building; no cost figure has been disclosed. A Meridian developer, Tommy Ahlquist, is working with the Coeur d'Alene Tribe on a mixed-use commercial and industrial project called Peregrine adjacent to the planned casino. Shoshone-Paiute Chairman Brian Mason has said his tribe declined an earlier offer from the Shoshone-Bannock Tribes to collaborate.

The second proposal belongs to the Shoshone-Bannock Tribes of the Fort Hall Reservation, who bought 157 acres off I-84 near Mountain Home in 2020. It would be the tribes' fourth casino and their first off tribal lands. The plan, as described, carries a price tag of $311 million and more than 500,000 square feet, including 2,000 electronic gaming machines, a 250-room hotel, six restaurants, an event center, two movie theaters, an arcade and a horse track with a grandstand. The application was formally submitted to the Interior Department in 2023, and the tribes' planning director, Alonzo Coby, has said there is "a lot of behind-the-scenes work happening to meet the application requirements."

We have covered each project individually, including the Coeur d'Alene and Shoshone-Paiute plan near Boise and the Shoshone-Bannock Mountain Home project. What is new is the governor's office's explanation of the state's role.

The approval path under IGRA

Both proposals involve land that is not currently held in trust for the tribes, which puts them in the category of off-reservation gaming. IGRA generally prohibits gaming on land acquired in trust after October 17, 1988, unless an exception applies. One such exception is the so-called two-part determination, described in detail in our explainer on Section 20 and off-reservation gaming.

As the governor's office outlined it to BoiseDev, the steps run as follows. The Secretary of the Interior must find that a casino would be in the tribe's best interest and not detrimental to the surrounding community. The governor must then concur with that determination. Only after concurrence do the tribe and the state negotiate a Class III gaming compact, which the Secretary must approve before construction can proceed. Our overview of governor concurrence explains why that step is often the decisive political hurdle: the governor has no obligation to concur, and a refusal effectively ends a two-part-determination application.

A Department of the Interior spokesperson told BoiseDev that gaming land reviews historically take 18 to 36 months or longer. An environmental assessment under the National Environmental Policy Act is expected to open for public comment on the Coeur d'Alene and Shoshone-Paiute proposal in the coming months; our NEPA explainer describes what that comment period involves. Neither tribal group has made its federal review public in full.

Why Idaho is a distinctive case

Idaho's gaming history is unusual. Boise banned gambling in 1949, and the activity moved to nearby Garden City. The Idaho Supreme Court closed loopholes for non-tribal casinos in 1953, and Cactus Pete's opened across the border in Jackpot, Nevada, in 1954. Tribal gaming in the state has been confined mainly to reservations, including the Coeur d'Alene Tribe's operations in northern Idaho and the Shoshone-Bannock Casino Hotel at Fort Hall. Our analysis of the Idaho lottery-machine model and its constitutional limits describes the legal backdrop.

That history helps explain why an off-reservation casino near the Treasure Valley would be a significant departure. The Boise metropolitan area is the state's largest population center, and a casino 20 minutes away would sit in a very different market than a reservation facility in a rural county. It also helps explain why concurrence would be a statewide political question rather than a purely technical one. Other off-reservation efforts nationally have shown that opposition from neighboring tribes can complicate approval; see our analysis of intertribal opposition to off-reservation casinos. In Idaho, the fact that two groups of tribes are competing for the same corridor adds another layer.

What to watch

Several milestones will signal progress. The first is publication of the NEPA environmental assessment, which would open a formal public comment window. The second is any Interior determination on the fee-to-trust applications and the best-interest finding. The third, and likely the most consequential, is whether the governor indicates a position on concurrence. The governor's office has so far described the process without taking a stance on either project.

It is also possible that only one proposal advances, or that neither does. Federal review timelines are long, and applicants frequently revise plans during that period. As with any off-reservation proposal, readers should treat renderings and cost figures as plans rather than commitments.
