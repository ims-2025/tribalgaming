When Tribes Sue Tribes: Intertribal Gaming Litigation in 2026

Comanche, Picayune and Coquille-related cases show competition between tribes moving from the market into the courtroom.

By James KingfisherPublished October 22, 20264 min read

Three cases in 2026 show a pattern in tribal gaming: competition between tribes is increasingly being fought in federal court. A Comanche Nation challenge to a neighboring tribe's Oklahoma casino, a Picayune Rancheria challenge to the federal approval behind a new California casino, and a years-long Oregon fight over the Coquille Indian Tribe's Medford property all involve tribes contesting one another's gaming, not merely disagreeing with states or the federal government. Understanding why intertribal gaming litigation is growing helps explain a large share of the sector's legal calendar.

Three cases, three procedural postures

The cases differ in what they ask courts to do. In Comanche Nation v. Ware, decided by the Tenth Circuit on April 21, 2026, the Comanche Nation sued officials of the Fort Sill Apache Tribe over the Warm Springs Casino near Lawton, which opened in 2022. The Nation operates two casinos nearby and has said the new facility reduced its profits. According to legal press reports, the appeals court allowed part of the challenge to proceed. Our report on the decision has details.

In California, Picayune Rancheria of the Chukchansi Indians sued the chair of the National Indian Gaming Commission in a case before U.S. District Judge Kirk E. Sherriff. It challenges the Commission's approval of a 2024 management contract between the North Fork Rancheria of Mono Indians and Station Casinos, arguing that post-1988 off-reservation land required a two-part determination. See our coverage of the North Fork opening and pending ruling.

In Oregon, a federal judge, U.S. District Judge Amit P. Mehta, granted summary judgment to the federal government and the Coquille Indian Tribe, upholding an Interior Department decision to take about 2.4 acres into trust in South Medford and authorize gaming there. The Cow Creek Band of Umpqua Tribe of Indians, the Karuk Tribe and the Tolowa Dee-ni' Nation had challenged the decision after more than a decade of litigation. Our Medford ruling report covers that decision.

Why tribes litigate against each other

The common thread is market protection. Each plaintiff operates, or has an interest in, a gaming market that a new facility would change. Because the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act places the federal government in the approval chain for land, management contracts and certain gaming determinations, rival tribes often cannot challenge a competitor directly. They instead challenge the federal action that enabled the competitor, naming federal officials as defendants. That is visible in the Picayune and Coquille cases, where the federal government was a principal defendant.

The Comanche case is different in that it named tribal officials directly. Both routes carry difficulties. Plaintiffs must establish standing, meaning a concrete injury traceable to the challenged action, a subject we examine in our standing doctrine analysis. Defendant tribes, for their part, often intervene to defend the federal action, as the Coquille Tribe did in Oregon.

The costs of the strategy

Intertribal litigation is expensive and slow, and the Oregon dispute illustrates the time horizon: a decade or more is not unusual. It can also strain relationships among tribes that cooperate elsewhere, including on joint advocacy before Congress and state legislatures. Our analysis of intertribal opposition to off-reservation casinos discusses how some tribal organizations have sought to manage that tension.

There is also an outcome asymmetry. A plaintiff tribe that wins may delay or unwind a competitor's project, but a defendant that prevails gains legal certainty that makes financing and construction easier. In the Medford case, the summary judgment ruling favored the federal government and the Coquille Tribe. Whether the plaintiffs pursue further review is a matter we track in our fall court calendar.

What the pattern suggests

As more markets mature, new facilities increasingly come at the expense of existing ones, and the incentive to litigate grows. Courts will keep receiving these cases, and each decision refines the rules on who may sue, whom they may name and what relief is available. For operators, that argues for treating legal risk as a permanent part of expansion planning. For policymakers and readers of the Legal Guide, it is a reminder that IGRA disputes are not only between tribes and states. Increasingly, they are between tribes.

Implications for tribal leaders and counsel

For a tribal council weighing a new casino or an expansion, the lesson is practical. Legal exposure from neighboring tribes should be assessed early, ideally before significant capital is committed, and the documentation supporting land status, management arrangements and compact authority should be organized with the expectation that a competitor may someday scrutinize it. That is not an argument for avoiding growth. It is an argument for treating the litigation record as part of the project file.

Tribal associations also have a role. Organizations that bring tribes together can provide forums for discussing siting and market impacts before disputes harden into lawsuits, though they cannot compel agreement. The more concentrated and mature a market becomes, the more valuable those conversations are likely to be, and the more difficult.
