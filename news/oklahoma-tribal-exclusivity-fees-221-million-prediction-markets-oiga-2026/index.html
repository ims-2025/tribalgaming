Oklahoma Tribal Exclusivity Fees Top $221M as OIGA Targets Prediction Markets

The Oklahoma Indian Gaming Association links a $221 million fee stream to its argument that sports prediction apps violate IGRA and the state's compacts.

By Marcus WhitehorsePublished October 12, 20264 min read

Oklahoma tribes that hold Class III compacts with the state paid more than $221 million in exclusivity fees in the 2025 budget year, according to figures cited by the Oklahoma Indian Gaming Association (OIGA). That number now sits at the center of a new argument: OIGA chair Matthew Morgan told Oklahoma Voice on October 9, 2026 that sports prediction market apps undermine the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act and the Oklahoma tribal-state compacts, and that they infringe on the exclusivity for which tribes pay. Understanding Oklahoma tribal exclusivity fees is essential to understanding why the dispute matters to state budgets as well as tribal governments.

How exclusivity fees work in Oklahoma

Under Oklahoma's compact structure, tribes pay the state a recurring fee in exchange for the exclusive right to offer certain Class III games within the state. The fee is calculated as a percentage of revenue from covered games, with rates that step up as revenue grows, and payments are made on a regular schedule rather than annually in a lump sum. The state directs the proceeds to public purposes, with education a principal beneficiary. The arrangement is a classic example of what IGRA practitioners call meaningful concessions: the state grants something of value, here exclusivity, and the tribe pays for it, a principle that is explained in our primer on revenue sharing in tribal-state compacts.

The OIGA figure of more than $221 million for the 2025 budget year shows the scale of that bargain. It also frames the stakes of any competitor that offers a similar product without paying into the system. If a competing product captures wagers that would otherwise be placed through a tribal channel, the revenue base shrinks, and the fee shrinks with it. For readers new to the framework, the Legal Guide explains why exclusivity is the foundation of most compact payments, and our explainer on exclusivity clauses and payment suspension describes what happens when exclusivity is breached.

The competing positions

Morgan, who leads an association of 25 member tribes, called the apps illegal Class III gaming and a violation of tribal sovereignty. He noted that none of the tribes that have signed agreements with Kalshi-backed apps belongs to OIGA, which means the association is not speaking for them. State Sen. Bill Coleman, a Ponca City Republican, offered a more cautious assessment. He said prediction markets currently hold a competitive edge over tribes, raised concern about use by people under 21, and doubted that the state can legislate the issue, suggesting the courts should resolve it first. He added that if traditional sports betting arrives in Oklahoma while Kalshi and Polymarket still exist, the market would remain competitive.

Kalshi's response was set out in a September open letter from CEO Tarek Mansour, who argued that tribal gaming has reached record revenue even as prediction markets have grown and that one does not need to displace the other. A company spokesperson, Jacki McGavick, said prediction markets fall under exclusive federal jurisdiction. Both statements echo points made elsewhere in the national debate, and the record-revenue argument has been tested in our own reporting on record revenue and mid-2026 headwinds.

Why the legislative path is stuck

The Oklahoma Legislature has not resolved how, or whether, to legalize sports betting, and the question of how tribal exclusivity would fit into any framework has stalled. Earlier coverage details the Senate defeat of a House bill and the later turn toward the ballot; see the Senate defeat of HB 1047. The stall has a practical consequence. Without a state law, there is no negotiated channel through which tribes could add sports wagering under their compacts, while prediction market apps are available to adults nationwide under a federal theory that tribes dispute.

That combination explains why Oklahoma tribes have chosen to pursue the issue through federal channels. The same federal question is now before the Supreme Court in a petition supported by more than 140 tribal entities, as described in our report on the amicus filing. For the state, the fee stream is an interest of its own: a decline in covered-game revenue would reduce the amount paid into the education and public-purpose funds the fees support.

None of this means a fee decline has occurred. The $221 million figure is a historical total, and the argument about future erosion is a projection that the platforms contest. What the Oklahoma debate does show is how directly exclusivity connects tribal sovereignty, compact law and public budgets. Readers can follow developments through the Oklahoma state hub, which tracks operators, compacts and regulatory actions across the state.

It is also worth noting how the fee model interacts with growth. Because payments are tied to revenue from covered games, a rising tide lifts the state's share automatically, which gives Oklahoma officials a continuing interest in the health of tribal gaming operations. That alignment has historically made the state a partner as well as a counterparty, and it is one reason the legislative stalemate over sports betting is watched so closely: any framework that adds a new product must decide whether the existing fee structure extends to it, or whether a separate rate and a separate exclusivity covenant apply.

Analysts who follow the compact system also point out that disputes over exclusivity are normally resolved through the dispute-resolution provisions of the compact itself, not in ordinary court. The prediction market question is unusual because the alleged competitor is not a party to the compact at all, which is why federal courts, not the compact's arbitration clause, are the venue where the matter is being fought.
