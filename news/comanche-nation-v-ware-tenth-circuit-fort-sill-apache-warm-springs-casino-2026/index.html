Tenth Circuit Lets Comanche Nation Casino Challenge Proceed in Part

An April appeals ruling over a Lawton-area casino continues to shape how tribes litigate against one another, and against the officials who run gaming.

By Marcus WhitehorsePublished October 22, 20264 min read

A federal appeals court has allowed part of a Comanche Nation challenge to a competing tribe's casino to go forward, in a decision that bears on how tribes can litigate against one another over gaming. The Tenth Circuit's ruling in Comanche Nation v. Ware, No. 24-6221, was issued on April 21, 2026, and is reported at 174 F.4th 717. Although the opinion predates the fall, it remains a live reference point for Oklahoma operators and for attorneys advising tribes on the limits of sovereign immunity in gaming disputes.

The dispute behind the decision

The Fort Sill Apache Tribe opened the Warm Springs Casino near Lawton, Oklahoma, in 2022. The Comanche Nation operates the Spur Casino and the Comanche Casino a few miles away, and it has said the new competition reduced profits at its properties. The Nation sued, naming Fort Sill Apache officials, including the tribe's chairwoman, and members of its gaming commission, in both their individual and official capacities. Federal officials at the Department of the Interior, the Bureau of Indian Affairs and the National Indian Gaming Commission were also named in their official capacities.

The three-judge panel consisted of Circuit Judges Hartz, Tymkovich and Bacharach, with Judge Tymkovich writing. Legal press coverage characterized the outcome as a split decision on tribal immunity. According to that reporting, the court found that the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act invalidates sovereign immunity in the dispute, and it rejected an argument that tribal sovereign immunity covers racketeering claims against tribal officials sued in their individual capacities. The full opinion should be consulted for the precise scope of each holding, which this summary does not attempt to reproduce beyond what has been publicly reported.

Why a neighboring-tribe lawsuit matters

Disputes between tribes over casino siting are not new, but they rarely reach the question of whether tribal officials can be sued personally. The conventional assumption in Indian country is that tribal sovereign immunity shields the tribe, its enterprises and officials acting in their official roles. The Tenth Circuit's reasoning, as reported, tests where that shield ends when plaintiffs frame their claims against individuals.

For Oklahoma, where dozens of tribes operate gaming under the state's model compact, that matters. The state's market is dense and highly competitive; our analysis of Oklahoma market saturation describes how closely spaced facilities draw on overlapping customer bases. Where one tribe believes another's casino was opened unlawfully, litigation becomes a plausible competitive response, and the availability of claims against individuals changes the risk calculation for tribal leaders and gaming commissions.

What the ruling does not decide

It is important to be precise about what the decision is. It allows part of the Comanche Nation's challenge to proceed; it does not decide the underlying claim that the Warm Springs Casino was opened unlawfully. The case returns to the lower court, where the merits remain to be addressed. Nothing in the reporting available to us indicates how that question will be resolved, and it would be speculative to suggest otherwise.

The ruling also does not change tribal sovereign immunity in general. Tribal immunity from suit remains a bedrock principle, as our explainer on Michigan v. Bay Mills describes. The decision turns on the particular statutory and factual context of an IGRA-governed gaming dispute and on claims aimed at named individuals.

Implications for operators and counsel

Several practical points follow. Tribal gaming commissions and governing bodies may want to document the legal basis for each new facility with care, since the record could matter in a later challenge. Competitors weighing their own litigation options will note that a pathway to federal court exists, though it is neither quick nor guaranteed. And lenders and vendors, who already examine immunity waivers closely, as our piece on limited sovereign immunity waivers discusses, may add questions about pending intertribal litigation to their diligence.

The Oklahoma directory pages on TribalGaming.com provide context on the tribes and facilities in the state's market. We will report on further proceedings in the case as the lower court acts.

Reading the decision in context

Appellate rulings in gaming disputes rarely settle a market fight by themselves; they define the procedural lanes in which the fight continues. Here, the practical effect is that the Comanche Nation's case stays alive in part, while the lower court must still decide what the Nation can prove and what relief, if any, is available. Observers should expect a lengthy sequence of motions before any final answer, and should treat early commentary on the eventual outcome with caution.

For the broader industry, the value of the decision lies in the questions it raises for intertribal planning. Tribes weighing new facilities near existing ones now have a recent circuit-level data point on the litigation exposure that can accompany a launch. That does not make such projects inadvisable. It does suggest that legal review of the authority for each new gaming site, and early communication with neighboring tribes where practical, belong in the planning process alongside financing and construction schedules.
