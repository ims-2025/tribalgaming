NIGC Litigation Authority Gap: Why Prediction-Market Fights Run Through DOJ

Federal gaming regulators told tribal leaders they must work through the Justice Department to sue or defend in court, a structural limit that shapes how the prediction-market dispute can unfold.

By Daniel YazziePublished October 12, 20266 min read

The National Indian Gaming Commission's limited litigation authority is one of the least discussed constraints in the prediction-market dispute. At a tribal gaming conference workshop in late March 2026, an NIGC associate general counsel told attendees that the commission lacks independent litigation authority and must work with the Department of Justice to bring or defend federal court actions. For tribes hoping the federal gaming regulator would take a public or legal stand on prediction markets, that structural fact matters as much as any policy position.

What the NIGC said about prediction markets

According to trade reporting published March 31, 2026, the Indian Gaming Association had announced the previous day that it was raising millions of dollars to fight prediction markets and planned to sue the Commodity Futures Trading Commission. At a Monday workshop that week, tribal leaders said they hoped the NIGC would take a public stance. The commission's Vice Chair, Jeannie Hovland, said the agency would not say whether it would take a position. The commission planned a listening session with tribal leaders and regulators on April 1 at the IGA conference in San Diego, seeking feedback on sports-betting prediction markets, and it had held a Zoom roundtable with tribes on the issue in January.

Rea Cisneros, an NIGC associate general counsel, explained the legal limit. The agency lacks independent litigation authority, so any federal court action it might bring or defend has to be handled in cooperation with the Justice Department. She noted that prediction-market lawsuits were pending in many state and federal courts and that the matter could eventually reach the Supreme Court. Hovland emphasized the breadth of the commission's responsibilities, describing oversight of more than 250 tribes and over 500 gaming operations, and the importance of hearing from as many of them as possible.

The reporting also observed that the NIGC sits within the Department of the Interior, part of an administration that has backed prediction markets. That context explains why tribal leaders were seeking a signal from the commission and why the commission was reluctant to give one.

Why independent litigation authority matters

The NIGC's core powers come from the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act: approving tribal gaming ordinances and management contracts, reviewing background investigations, inspecting and auditing gaming operations, and taking enforcement actions such as notices of violation, civil fines and closure orders. Our explainer on how the NIGC regulates tribal gaming covers those tools. They operate largely through administrative processes aimed at tribal operators. None of them is a general power to sue another party in federal court.

That distinction is important because the prediction-market conflict is not primarily a dispute between the NIGC and a tribe. It involves event-contract platforms regulated, or claimed to be regulated, by the CFTC, state regulators asserting authority over sports wagering, and tribes asserting that sports betting within their jurisdiction is a Class III activity governed by IGRA and by compact. In that landscape, an agency without independent litigation authority cannot sue a prediction-market platform on its own initiative. It would need the Justice Department to represent the government's interest, and the Justice Department decides, within the executive branch's priorities, whether and how to proceed.

The practical result is that the NIGC's role is largely advisory and informational unless the Justice Department chooses to act. Tribes seeking legal remedies have been pursuing their own cases, often alongside states, a pattern reflected in our coverage of the Five Civilized Tribes' call for federal oversight. They have also turned to Congress and to the CFTC itself, which is where much of the policy fight has moved.

The congressional and CFTC track

With the NIGC constrained, tribal advocates have pressed other federal actors. A July 27, 2026 letter from the Senate Committee on Indian Affairs to the CFTC addressed tribal consultation for a rulemaking identified as RIN 3038-AF65, according to the committee's posting. In August, the committee also held a roundtable on prediction markets, which we covered in our report on the Senate Indian Affairs roundtable, and the CFTC convened a session on tribal consultation, summarized in our piece on the CFTC roundtable with NCAI and IGA.

These tracks reflect a straightforward strategic reading. If the gaming regulator cannot litigate and may not take a position, the venues that can change outcomes are the courts where tribes are plaintiffs or intervenors, the commission that regulates the platforms, and the legislators who oversee both. Consultation obligations give tribes a procedural hook with the CFTC. Congressional oversight gives them a political one.

What this means for tribal gaming policy

Three points follow from the structure. First, silence from the NIGC should not be read as indifference or as agreement; it may reflect legal limits and the commission's position within the executive branch. Second, tribes cannot count on federal enforcement to protect Class III exclusivity from event-contract platforms, so the private litigation and state-level actions underway carry more weight. Third, the question of who speaks for federal gaming policy remains unsettled, because the agency charged with regulating Indian gaming is not positioned to act alone.

Readers should note the limits of what is documented here. The March statements are a snapshot from a conference workshop and do not describe any later change in the NIGC's posture. We have not seen a formal NIGC position on prediction markets, and nothing in the cited reporting indicates the commission has asked the Justice Department to litigate on the issue. For the statutory background, the Legal Guide explains the roles IGRA assigns to the commission, the Interior Secretary and the courts.

The litigation gap is a reminder that the architecture of federal Indian gaming regulation was built for oversight of tribal operations, not for contests with outside industries. As prediction markets test that architecture, tribes will keep looking for the forum that can deliver an answer.
