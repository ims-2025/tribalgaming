A 1988 Petition, Still Open: Federal Acknowledgment and Tribal Gaming

A notice in the Federal Register shows how long the acknowledgment process can run, and why recognition is only the first of several steps toward any tribal gaming operation.

By Elena RuizPublished October 31, 20266 min read

A notice in the October 21, 2026 Federal Register is a reminder of how long federal acknowledgment can take. The Bureau of Indian Affairs announced that the Juaneño Band of Mission Indians, Acjachemen Nation, has supplemented its documented petition for federal acknowledgment as an American Indian tribe. The petition was first submitted on February 24, 1988, and comments and evidence must be postmarked by February 18, 2027. The notice does not concern gaming, but federal acknowledgment is the threshold through which any tribal gaming under the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act must pass.

What the notice says

According to the notice, the Juaneño Band submitted its petition under the older acknowledgment regulations and chose to complete the process under the revised 25 CFR part 83 rules, which took effect on July 31, 2015. The petitioner has supplemented its documentation, and the petition materials are posted by the Office of Federal Acknowledgment (OFA), whose director, Nikki Bass, is listed as the contact. The same Federal Register issue also carried a separate notice that a tribe and state had extended an existing Class III compact to December 21, 2026, which we covered in our report on that compact extension. The two notices sit at opposite ends of the same legal framework: one concerns who is a tribe, the other concerns what an existing tribe may do.

We do not know, and the notice does not say, whether the Juaneño Band has any gaming plans. Nothing in the filing suggests it does, and this article draws no such inference. The petition is relevant here as an example of the process, not of an intended casino.

How the Part 83 process works

Part 83 sets out criteria a petitioning group must meet to be acknowledged. Under the revised regulations, they include that the group has been identified as an American Indian entity since 1900, that it comprises a distinct community, that it has maintained political authority over its members, that it has a governing document, that its members descend from a historical tribe, that its membership is not primarily enrolled in another federally recognized tribe, and that it has not been the subject of congressional termination. The Office of Federal Acknowledgment reviews the documentation, and the process includes public comment periods such as the one this notice opens.

The length of the Juaneño petition, filed in 1988 and still pending in 2026, is unusual but not unique. Acknowledgment decisions depend on historical evidence that can take years to compile and evaluate, and petitioners have the option to switch to newer rules mid-process, as this petitioner did. The existence of a long timeline is a feature of the administrative process rather than a statement about the merits of any petition.

Recognition and gaming are separate questions

For readers following tribal gaming, the key point is that acknowledgment does not itself authorize a casino. Our explainer on federal recognition and gaming eligibility sets out the chain of requirements. A recognized tribe must have Indian lands on which gaming is allowed, as described in the IGRA definition of Indian lands. For land acquired after October 17, 1988, an exception such as restored lands or an initial reservation must generally apply, which our explainer on the Section 20 exceptions addresses. The tribe must adopt a gaming ordinance approved by the NIGC, and for Class III gaming it must negotiate a compact with the state.

The sequence matters because public discussion sometimes treats recognition as the finish line. In practice, it is the start of a multi-year path. The Lumbee Tribe, whose recognition was granted by Congress rather than through Part 83, illustrates the point: even after recognition, its gaming effort still depends on a referendum, land questions, and a compact, as our analysis of the Lumbee land purchase explains.

Why the process matters to the gaming industry

Acknowledgment affects the broader gaming landscape in several ways. Existing tribes and states pay close attention to petitions in markets where new gaming could change competitive dynamics, and intertribal disputes have followed some recognition and land decisions, as in the cases we reviewed in intertribal gaming litigation. Recognition also carries benefits unrelated to gaming, including access to federal programs for housing, health, and education, which is why many petitioners pursue it regardless of any economic development plans.

The state context also matters. California, where the Juaneño Band is based, has one of the largest concentrations of tribal gaming in the country, and its compacting process involves the governor and legislature, as seen in the recent ratification bills we covered. Readers can explore the state's operators in the California state hub. Whether any newly acknowledged group would seek gaming would depend on land, state policy, and the tribe's own priorities.

What to watch

The next concrete date is February 18, 2027, the postmark deadline for comments and evidence on the Juaneño petition. After that, the OFA process continues according to its own schedule, and we do not expect a final determination on any timeline we can responsibly predict.
