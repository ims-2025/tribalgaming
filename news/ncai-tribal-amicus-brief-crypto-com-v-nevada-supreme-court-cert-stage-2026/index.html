NCAI Seeks Tribal Sign-Ons for Supreme Court Brief in Crypto.com v. Nevada

The cert-stage brief, built around IGRA and tribal sovereignty, puts Indian Country on record as the justices weigh whether to take up the sports event contract fight.

By Marcus WhitehorsePublished November 25, 20264 min read

The National Congress of American Indians is asking tribal nations to sign on to a Crypto.com v. Nevada amicus brief at the U.S. Supreme Court, supporting Nevada in the dispute over whether states can regulate sports event contracts offered by federally registered exchanges. Tribes and tribal organizations have until 5 p.m. Eastern on Thursday, Dec. 3, to confirm participation. The brief is scheduled to be filed Dec. 7, the date Nevada's response to Crypto.com's petition for certiorari is due.

The call to action, circulated this week, is the latest step in a coordinated tribal legal strategy on prediction markets that now runs from federal district courts to the nation's highest court.

What NCAI is asking tribes to do

According to the notice, a tribe's authorized representative or attorney must email confirmation to Melody McCoy of the Native American Rights Fund, copying the other contacts listed in the alert, and must give the tribe's or organization's full name exactly as it should appear on the brief. NCAI stressed that confirmation is required even for tribes that joined earlier amicus filings in related litigation, including the tribal brief filed in October in the New Jersey case discussed in our coverage of tribal nations asking the Court to review Flaherty v. Kalshi.

The brief is expected to build on arguments tribes have made in prior prediction-market cases, focusing on tribal sovereignty and the framework the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act establishes for regulating gaming on Indian lands. The notice did not detail the brief's specific recommendation on whether the Court should grant review, and NCAI did not release a draft.

The case behind the Crypto.com v. Nevada amicus brief

The dispute began when the Nevada Gaming Control Board moved to treat sports event contracts as sports wagering under state gaming law. Crypto.com, along with Kalshi and Robinhood, argued that the contracts are derivatives regulated exclusively by the Commodity Futures Trading Commission under the Commodity Exchange Act. A district court ultimately dissolved an injunction that had shielded the platforms from Nevada enforcement, and a unanimous Ninth Circuit panel affirmed, with Judge Ryan D. Nelson writing that the federal statute likely does not preempt Nevada's gambling laws. Our analysis of the Ninth Circuit preemption ruling covers what that holding means for IGRA.

Crypto.com and Robinhood each petitioned the Supreme Court; Crypto.com's petition is docketed as No. 26-344. The question has been answered differently elsewhere. In April, a divided Third Circuit panel held 2-1 that Kalshi's sports contracts are swaps under the Commodity Exchange Act, and New Jersey has filed its own petition asking the justices to review that decision. A coalition of states has also urged review, as covered in our report on state attorneys general and the Kalshi petitions.

As of this week, the Supreme Court has not decided whether to hear the case. The tribal brief is a cert-stage filing, not a merits brief.

That distinction matters. At the certiorari stage, amicus briefs are aimed at persuading the justices whether a case is worth taking, typically by explaining the practical stakes and the extent of disagreement among lower courts. For a primer on that process, see our explainer on how the Supreme Court decides tribal gaming cases.

Why tribes are weighing in now

For tribal governments, the preemption question is not abstract. If the Commodity Exchange Act displaces state gaming law for sports event contracts, tribes worry the same reasoning could be used to sidestep IGRA, the compacts negotiated under it, and the exclusivity those compacts protect. Sports contracts account for the large majority of trading volume on Kalshi, according to a Congressional Research Service review cited in industry reporting, which helps explain why the platforms are pressing the issue so aggressively.

Tribes have won several recent rounds in lower courts. A federal judge in New Mexico this week ordered Kalshi to geofence its sports markets off the lands of four tribal nations, following a similar injunction for California tribes earlier in November. At the same time, the CFTC has directed registered exchanges to drop geographic trading restrictions within 30 days, setting up a direct conflict with those court orders. Tribal leaders see a Supreme Court resolution, whichever way it goes, as likely to set the outer boundary of their regulatory authority over app-based wagering.

Coordinated amicus participation has become a hallmark of tribal litigation strategy, as described in our analysis of tribal coalition amicus strategy. A brief joined by a large number of federally recognized tribes signals to the Court that the outcome will reach well beyond Nevada's borders.

What comes next

Once Nevada files its response on Dec. 7, the petitioners will have an opportunity to reply, after which the petition can be distributed for one of the justices' private conferences. A decision on whether to grant review could come in the new year. If the Court grants certiorari, a separate round of merits briefing would follow, and tribes would likely file again. Tribal leaders and counsel tracking the issue can find the underlying IGRA framework in the TribalGaming Legal Guide.
