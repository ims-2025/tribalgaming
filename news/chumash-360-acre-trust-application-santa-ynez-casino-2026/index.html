Santa Ynez Chumash Seek Trust Status for 360 Acres Beside Casino

The band has not said what it will build, but the county wants assurances on gaming as a November 10 hearing approaches.

By Elena RuizPublished October 27, 20264 min read

The Santa Ynez Band of Chumash Indians has begun the process of asking the federal government to take roughly 360 acres beside its casino resort into trust, a step that would largely remove the parcel from Santa Barbara County's land-use rules. The tribe announced the application in mid-October, and the county Board of Supervisors discussed it in closed session on October 20. A public item is expected at the board's November 10 meeting. The tribe has not said what it intends to build, and as of this writing no change to the property is imminent. For anyone following the Chumash trust land application, the open question is less about the casino than about what the land will eventually be used for.

What the application covers

The parcel sits along Highway 154 and Meadowlark Road, next to the Chumash Casino Resort in the Santa Ynez Valley. Today it is used for grazing and occasional events; the recent Intertribal Powwow was held on it. Placing it in federal trust would shift regulatory authority over the land from the county to the tribe and the Interior Department. In practical terms, county zoning, building permits and special-event rules would no longer govern what happens there, although environmental review under federal law and any agreements the tribe chooses to sign with local governments would still matter.

County Supervisor Joan Hartmann has said she discussed with tribal leaders easing special-event permit requirements and a possible partnership on new sports fields and glamping-style amenities. The tribe did not respond to press inquiries before the story that first reported the application was published, and it has not released a development plan. Absent a plan, speculation about the parcel's purpose is just that, and this article does not assume one.

A familiar playbook in the Santa Ynez Valley

The application is not the tribe's first land acquisition in the valley. About fifteen years ago the band bought the 1,400-acre Camp 4 property from the late actor Fess Parker and pursued trust status for it. Santa Barbara County sued over that effort and lost. The tribe subsequently completed an environmental impact report, and federal action cleared the way for 143 homes on the site. The county has shown little appetite for repeating that fight, and officials have indicated the board has limited legal standing to mount a new challenge. That history shapes the current conversation: a county with a record of losing in court is more likely to seek negotiated assurances than to litigate.

For readers new to the band, our enterprise profile of the Santa Ynez Band covers its business holdings, and the California state hub lists the state's compacted tribes and their facilities.

The casino context: compact, slots and softer revenue

The trust application arrives against a backdrop of recent state action on the tribe's compact. According to local reporting, the tribe's compact permits two gaming facilities, though it operates only one. A bill co-authored by state Senator Monique Limón would raise the slot machine limit from 2,500 to 3,000, extend the compact by 20 years and shield the tribe from further state environmental review. The measure passed the Senate unanimously and was signed by Governor Gavin Newsom. We examined the package in our coverage of the California compact package.

Gambling revenue at the property has also softened, which local reporting attributes to competition from sports betting and so-called predictive markets. That pressure is a reason many California tribes are weighing diversification, and adjacent land provides optionality for non-gaming uses such as events, recreation and lodging-adjacent amenities. Whether Santa Ynez has such uses in mind is unknown.

What the county wants to know

Supervisor Hartmann has said she is inclined to ask tribal leaders for assurances that the tribe does not plan a second gaming facility on the adjacent property known locally as the Triangle Property. That request goes to the heart of the matter. Land taken into trust is not automatically eligible for gaming; under the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act, gaming on newly acquired trust land is generally restricted unless an exception applies, and the tribe would also need its compact to authorize the facility. Our explainer on land into trust and IGRA Section 20 walks through those exceptions. Because lands contiguous to an existing reservation are often treated differently from distant parcels, the eligibility analysis for this acreage may turn on how Interior characterizes it, a point neither the tribe nor the department has addressed publicly.

The county has a defined window to notify the public, hold a hearing, accept comment and weigh its options. Counties routinely use that window to seek memoranda of understanding covering road impacts, emergency services and event traffic, an approach described in our guide to intergovernmental agreements with host communities.

What to watch

Three developments will clarify the picture. First, whether the tribe publishes a purpose-and-need statement, which federal trust applications typically require. Second, what the Board of Supervisors decides to say at its November 10 session, and whether it seeks written assurances on gaming. Third, how Interior handles the application, since trust decisions have grown more contested in several states over the past year. For now the safest reading is a land-management step by a tribe with a long record of valley development, not an announcement of new gaming. We will update this story as the county posts its agenda and as federal notices appear.
