Michigan Tribal Online Gaming Payments Rise 36% in September Data

Inside the MGCB's monthly release, the tribal line is growing faster than the market, even as the land-based payment dispute deepens.

By James KingfisherPublished November 13, 20264 min read

Michigan's internet gaming and online sports betting market posted $355.7 million in gross receipts in September 2026, according to the Michigan Gaming Control Board. Inside that headline is a figure that gets less attention: Michigan tribal online gaming operators reported $8.3 million in payments to their tribal governing bodies for the month, up from $6.1 million in September 2025. That is an increase of roughly 36 percent year over year, faster than the market's overall growth.

The number is small next to the state's monthly tax take, but it is one of the few public windows into how tribal governments are participating in a regulated online market that commercial operators and national platforms dominate in most states. This analysis compares the September 2026 and September 2025 releases and explains what the figures do and do not show.

What the September data shows

Combined adjusted gross receipts across iGaming and online sports betting reached $313.04 million in September 2026, compared with $256.6 million a year earlier, an increase of about 22 percent. iGaming accounted for most of it: $288.15 million in adjusted receipts, up 18.4 percent from September 2025. Online sports betting adjusted receipts were $24.89 million, up 88.2 percent year over year, on handle of $544.8 million as the football season began.

Payments to the State of Michigan rose to $62.4 million from $51.6 million. Payments to the City of Detroit, which flow from the three Detroit commercial casinos' online operations, rose more modestly, to $14.0 million from $13.4 million. Tribal operators' payments to governing bodies, the line the MGCB uses for tribal online operations, rose from $6.1 million to $8.3 million.

The operator count also changed. In September 2025, 15 commercial and tribal operators were authorized, with 14 offering iGaming and 11 offering online sports betting. In September 2026 the count was still 15, but all 15 offered iGaming and 13 offered online sports betting. The release does not say which operators added product lines over the year.

How tribal operators fit into Michigan's online framework

Michigan's 2019 Lawful Internet Gaming Act and Lawful Sports Betting Act opened online gaming to both the three Detroit commercial casinos and the state's federally recognized tribes that operate casinos under Class III compacts. Tribal participation in online gaming is therefore built into state law rather than carved out after the fact, a structure that differs from states where online gaming launched without tribes or over their objection. Our Michigan tribal gaming market deep dive covers the land-based side of that market, and the Michigan state hub lists the state's tribal properties.

Most licensed tribal operators in Michigan partner with national platform providers, which run the apps and handle much of the technology and marketing. The tribe holds the license and its gaming enterprise receives a share of revenue under the platform agreement. The MGCB's "payments to governing bodies" line reflects what tribal operators report remitting to their tribal governments, not total tribal-side revenue, and the board's monthly release does not break it out by tribe.

Tribal online payments in Michigan grew about 36 percent year over year in September, against roughly 22 percent for the market as a whole.

That distinction matters when comparing the tribal figure to state tax payments. The state number reflects taxes and fees collected under the state's online gaming laws. The tribal number reflects what flows back to tribal governments from their own licensed operations. The two are not directly comparable measures of market share.

Why the land-based contrast matters

The online numbers stand in contrast to the land-based relationship between Michigan's tribes and the state. Compact payments that several tribes make to the state, tied to exclusivity provisions, have fallen sharply, as we reported in our coverage of the 71 percent drop in tribal payments to the Michigan Strategic Fund. That dispute turns on whether expanded state-authorized gambling has eroded the exclusivity tribes pay for.

Online gaming sits at the center of that tension. It is a source of new revenue for the tribes that participate, and it is also part of the broader expansion of gambling options that some tribes argue undercuts their land-based exclusivity. The September data suggests tribal operators are capturing a growing share of online value, but it does not resolve the question of whether the overall bargain still balances for every tribe, particularly those without strong online partners.

Limits of the data

Monthly figures are volatile, and September is shaped by the start of the football season, especially for sports betting. A single month-over-year comparison should not be read as a trend line. The MGCB publishes operator-level results separately, and a fuller picture of tribal participation requires looking at which tribal licensees are active and how their receipts compare with commercial peers over several months.

The MGCB also does not publish tribal adjusted receipts in its headline release, so the "payments to governing bodies" figure is the most direct public indicator available. It understates total tribal economic activity, since platform partners retain a share of revenue and tribal enterprises carry their own costs.

What the figures do establish is that Michigan's tribal online channel is growing, not just holding steady, and that tribal operators have become a larger part of a market that is itself expanding. For tribes elsewhere weighing online gaming, as in states covered in our explainer on federal law and tribal online gaming, Michigan remains one of the clearest working examples of tribes participating alongside commercial operators under a single state framework.
