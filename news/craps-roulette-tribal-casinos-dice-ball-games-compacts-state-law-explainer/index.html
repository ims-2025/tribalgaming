Craps and Roulette at Tribal Casinos: Why the Rules Differ by State

From California's card-based versions to Oklahoma, Florida and Arizona's live dice, the table game you find depends on state law and the compact.

By James KingfisherPublished November 18, 20264 min read

Visitors who play craps and roulette at tribal casinos often notice that the games are not the same everywhere. In Florida, Oklahoma, Arizona and Connecticut, tribal casinos offer dice and wheel games that look like those on the Las Vegas Strip. In California, the same games are typically played with cards substituting for dice or a ball. The differences are not a matter of house preference. They follow directly from how the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act ties tribal Class III gaming to state law and to the terms of each tribal-state compact.

This explainer covers why the rules differ, how states have changed them, and what to look for when a compact amendment adds new table games.

Why craps and roulette at tribal casinos depend on state law

IGRA divides tribal gaming into three classes. Class I covers traditional and social games. Class II covers bingo, pull-tabs and certain non-banked card games. Class III covers everything else, including house-banked card games, slot machines, and casino games such as craps and roulette. Our guide to Class II versus Class III gaming explains the categories in more detail.

Class III gaming is lawful on Indian lands only if three conditions are met: the tribe has authorized it by ordinance, the activity is conducted under a tribal-state compact approved by the Secretary of the Interior, and the gaming is located in a state that "permits such gaming for any purpose by any person, organization, or entity." That last clause, discussed in our scope-of-gaming explainer, is the reason state law matters so much. Federal courts, including the Ninth Circuit in Rumsey Indian Rancheria v. Wilson in 1994, have held that a state is required to negotiate only over the types of games it permits, not over every form of Class III gaming.

In practice, that means a compact usually lists the specific games a tribe may offer. If a state's constitution or statutes prohibit a category of game, the compact generally will not authorize it, and adding it later may require a change in state law as well as a compact amendment.

California: card-based alternatives

California is the clearest example. Proposition 1A, approved by voters in 2000, amended the state constitution to allow federally recognized tribes to operate slot machines, lottery games, and banking and percentage card games on Indian lands under compacts. It did not authorize games in which the outcome is determined by dice or a ball, and California law has long prohibited them.

As a result, California tribal casinos commonly offer versions of the games that use cards to determine outcomes. In one form of California roulette, a wheel or spinner selects a playing card that corresponds to a roulette number and color. In California craps, cards replace or supplement the dice so that the game qualifies as a banked card game. State regulators have issued advisories treating house-banked games decided by dice as unauthorized Class III gaming, although tribes may argue that a particular game falls within their compact. The California state hub lists properties and compact details across the state.

How Oklahoma, Florida and Arizona added the games

Other states have changed their laws to permit dice and ball games, and tribal compacts followed.

In Oklahoma, Governor Mary Fallin signed House Bill 3375 on April 10, 2018, authorizing ball and dice games for compacted tribes through a supplement to the state's model compact. Tribal casinos began offering craps and roulette that August. The change came with exclusivity fees paid to the state, consistent with the fee structure in the model compact, which our Oklahoma model compact explainer describes.

In Florida, the 2021 compact between the Seminole Tribe and the state authorized craps and roulette at the tribe's casinos, along with sports betting. After litigation over the sports betting provisions, the Seminole Tribe launched craps and roulette on December 7, 2023. Our analysis of the Seminole compact covers the agreement's broader terms.

In Arizona, the 2021 compact amendments signed by Governor Doug Ducey expanded the table games tribes may offer to include craps, roulette, baccarat, pai gow and sic bo. Before the amendments, Arizona tribal casinos were limited to games such as blackjack and poker, along with keno and bingo.

In each case, the sequence was the same: the state changed what it permits, and the compact was amended to reflect it. A tribe cannot add a Class III game unilaterally, even on its own land.

Electronic versions and other variations

Electronic table games add another layer. Automated roulette wheels, electronic craps and stadium-style terminals may be treated differently from live games depending on how a compact defines gaming devices and table games. Some compacts count these terminals against machine caps, while others treat them as table games. Our explainer on electronic table games covers those classification questions.

Class II operations face a separate limit. Because Class II excludes house-banked games, a tribe operating without a Class III compact cannot offer banked craps or roulette at all, regardless of format. The distinction between banked and non-banked games is set out in our banked versus non-banked games explainer.

What to look for in a compact amendment

When a state and tribe negotiate new table games, several details determine how the games operate in practice: whether the state's underlying law authorizes the game for any person; whether the compact lists the game by name or by category; how revenue from new games is treated in any revenue-sharing or exclusivity-fee formula; and whether electronic versions are counted as machines or tables.

Those details explain why the same game can look different from one state to the next. They also show how compact negotiations, often discussed in terms of slot counts and revenue sharing, shape the experience on the casino floor.
