Kalshi Injunction: First IGRA Order Sets Nov. 13 and Nov. 25 Deadlines

A California judge has put the Ninth Circuit's IGRA holding into an enforceable order, with dates attached for tribal maps and Kalshi's compliance report.

By Elena RuizPublished November 09, 20265 min read

A federal judge in California has granted what the plaintiffs describe as the first preliminary injunction against Kalshi under the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act, barring the prediction-market operator from offering sports event contracts on the Indian lands of two tribes while their lawsuit proceeds. The order, issued by U.S. District Judge Jacqueline Scott Corley on a Wednesday in early November 2026 and reported on November 6, turns a September appellate holding into an operational obligation with dates attached. For tribal gaming regulators and operators, the Kalshi injunction is now less a question of legal theory and more a question of maps, compliance reports and enforcement.

The plaintiffs are Blue Lake Rancheria and Chicken Ranch Rancheria of Me-Wuk Indians. Their case reached this point after a three-judge Ninth Circuit panel ruled in September 2026 that the tribes were likely to succeed on their claim that Kalshi's sports contracts, when placed from tribal land, constitute Class III gaming under IGRA, and that federal commodities law does not displace tribal authority over gambling on Indian lands. Judge Corley had denied the tribes' request for an injunction a year earlier; after the remand, she reached the opposite result. Our earlier analysis of what the remand left open flagged harm, equities and the public interest as the remaining questions. This order answers them in the tribes' favor, at least for the two plaintiffs' lands.

What the Kalshi injunction requires, and when

According to reporting on the order, Kalshi is barred from offering sports contracts on the plaintiff tribes' Indian lands for the duration of the litigation. Two near-term dates structure the compliance process. The tribes must provide Kalshi with maps identifying the affected territories by November 13. Kalshi must then submit a report on its compliance efforts by November 25. The lawsuit itself continues toward a merits resolution.

The map requirement is the practical heart of the order. An injunction that bars activity "on Indian lands" is only as enforceable as the definition of those lands is clear to the party being restrained. Kalshi had already begun blocking access to its sports contracts on three reservations in late September, a voluntary step the company took after the Ninth Circuit's ruling. The tribes dispute that the measure is effective and allege that tribal gaming officials were still able to place bets from tribal land. The court's schedule therefore does two things: it gives Kalshi a defined geography to restrict, and it creates a documented record against which the tribes can test the company's report.

Readers unfamiliar with the statutory term should note that IGRA defines Indian lands to include land within a reservation's limits, as well as land held in trust for a tribe or individual, or held subject to a restriction against alienation, over which the tribe exercises governmental power. That definition is why a map is not a trivial exercise, and why the boundary between covered and uncovered parcels can matter. Our explainer on fee and trust land covers the distinction in more detail.

Why a first injunction under IGRA matters

Prediction-market litigation has largely been fought over preemption: whether the federal Commodity Exchange Act, administered by the Commodity Futures Trading Commission, displaces state gambling law. Tribes raise a different argument. Their claim is that IGRA, a federal statute of equal standing, makes Class III gaming on Indian lands lawful only when conducted under a tribal ordinance and a tribal-state compact, and that a sports contract placed from tribal land is such gaming regardless of the platform's federal registration. The Ninth Circuit accepted that framing as likely correct. Judge Corley's injunction is the first time a district court has put that reasoning into an enforceable order.

The Commodity Futures Trading Commission has taken Kalshi's side in the broader dispute, asserting exclusive federal jurisdiction and, according to the reporting, suing states over enforcement efforts. Kalshi maintains that its sports contracts are federally regulated financial instruments rather than gambling. The company's formal response to this specific injunction had not been reported at the time of publication. Any appeal would go to the Ninth Circuit, which has already addressed the likelihood-of-success question, a fact that narrows the grounds on which Kalshi could realistically challenge the order.

The injunction protects two tribes' lands. It does not, by itself, protect every tribe, and it does not resolve the preemption question that other courts are answering differently.

The limits of the ruling

It is important not to overstate what the order does. It applies to the plaintiff tribes' lands, not to Indian country generally. Other tribes with similar claims would need to bring their own actions or benefit from the precedent in their own circuits. The Ninth Circuit's holding binds district courts within that circuit, which includes California, but courts elsewhere have diverged. The Ho-Chunk Nation's case in Wisconsin proceeds on the merits, though a preliminary injunction was denied there. Appeals courts have also allowed states such as Nevada, Ohio and Tennessee to enforce their gambling laws against Kalshi, while Kalshi has won rulings in Illinois and New Jersey, and New Jersey has asked the U.S. Supreme Court to resolve the conflict. We have tracked that divide in our coverage of the circuit split and of the Ho-Chunk ruling.

The California setting adds a further wrinkle. The state has no legal sports betting, and Kalshi has offered sports contracts to Californians since January 2025. The state has not taken enforcement action against prediction markets, leaving tribes as the principal litigants defending the exclusivity that California voters granted tribal governments in 2000. For a broader view of the state's tribal market, see the California state hub.

What to watch next

Three developments will show how durable the injunction is. First, whether the November 13 maps and the November 25 compliance report are delivered on schedule and whether the tribes contest the report's adequacy. Second, whether Kalshi seeks appellate review or a stay. Third, whether other tribes file similar claims in reliance on the Ninth Circuit's holding, or whether the Supreme Court takes up the preemption question and supersedes the regional picture. Tribal leaders will also be watching federal agency guidance on geographic trading limits, which some have said could undercut localized relief.

For operators and regulators, the immediate lesson is procedural. Court-ordered exclusion zones depend on precise land descriptions, and tribes that anticipate similar litigation would be well served by confirming that their trust and reservation boundaries are documented in a form a defendant and a court can use.
