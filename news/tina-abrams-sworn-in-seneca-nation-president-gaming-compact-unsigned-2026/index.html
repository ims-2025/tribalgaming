Tina Abrams Sworn In as Seneca Nation President With Compact Still Unsigned

The new president blames Albany for the stalled compact, says she will meet Gov. Hochul, and pledges to keep defending Seneca land in Buffalo and Grand Island.

By Elena RuizPublished November 11, 20264 min read

Tina Abrams was sworn in on November 10, 2026 as president of the Seneca Nation of Indians, becoming the first woman elected to lead the nation and taking office with its gaming compact with New York State still unsigned. Speaking before thousands of attendees at Seneca Allegany Casino, Abrams said she was prepared to meet with Gov. Kathy Hochul to resolve the impasse but placed responsibility for the lack of progress on the state's negotiators.

Abrams won both the nation's caucus and its general election by strong margins, according to WAMC reporting on the inauguration. She succeeds J. Conrad Seneca, who was term-limited as president and lost a bid for treasurer in this year's caucus. For the next two years, all three of the nation's elected executive offices will be held by women: Abrams as president, Lenith Waterman as treasurer and Bethany Johnson as clerk. Abrams's term runs to 2028.

The Seneca gaming compact negotiations she inherits

The compact is the legal foundation for Class III gaming at the nation's three Western New York properties, Seneca Niagara, Seneca Allegany and Seneca Buffalo Creek, operated by Seneca Gaming Corporation. The nation has operated under short-term extensions while it and the state negotiate a successor to the agreement first signed in 2002. The structure of the existing deal, which pairs regional exclusivity with slot revenue payments to the state and host cities, is described in our earlier coverage of the Seneca compact deadline.

Abrams told reporters that no progress had been made with the governor's team and said Hochul has "got to educate herself" on the nation's position. She also signaled that she wants an agreement. Her comments add a new voice to a negotiation that turned contentious in early October, when then-President Seneca said the two sides had reached an agreement in principle that could go to the Legislature in the spring. A spokesperson for the governor responded that there had been productive discussions but no agreement, and called public discussion of a deal premature.

The substance of that October exchange is still relevant. President Seneca said state negotiators had rejected continuing the slot revenue pass-throughs that host cities Niagara Falls, Buffalo and Salamanca receive under the current compact. Niagara Falls Mayor Robert Restaino said the city could lose $11 million to $13 million a year if revenue sharing is not part of a new deal, a significant figure against a city budget of a little more than $100 million. State Senate Minority Leader Rob Ortt, a Niagara County Republican, said a compact without exclusivity and a payment did not sound like an agreement at all. Our analysis of the reported terms and their effect on host cities examines those stakes in more detail.

Any new compact requires approval from state lawmakers, which means the Legislature, not only the governor's office and the nation, will shape the final terms.

Land disputes in Buffalo and Grand Island

Abrams also pledged to continue defending the nation's sovereignty in disputes with the City of Buffalo and New York State over Seneca-owned land in Buffalo and on Grand Island. One current flashpoint is a gas station the nation is building on a three-acre parcel in Buffalo. The city objected to the project, and a state judge in October rejected the city's request to halt construction. The state has since challenged in federal court the federal government's approval of restricted fee status for the parcel.

Restricted fee status matters because it determines whether land is treated as Indian country, subject to tribal and federal jurisdiction rather than state and local regulation. It is distinct from trust land, a difference covered in our fee land vs. trust land explainer. The gas station dispute is not itself a gaming matter, but jurisdiction over Seneca land in Buffalo has been a recurring theme in the nation's relationship with the city and the state, including over the Buffalo Creek casino, and the outcome of the federal case could inform future land questions.

"I'm not going to let anybody push us around," Abrams said at the ceremony, framing her approach to both the compact and the land cases.

What comes next

The immediate questions are procedural. The first is whether Abrams's administration keeps the negotiating team and framework her predecessor described in October, or reopens points the nation previously characterized as settled. The second is whether a meeting with Hochul materializes, and on what timeline relative to the Legislature's 2027 session, since any compact must clear lawmakers before it takes effect. The third is how revenue sharing for host cities is resolved, an issue on which local officials across Western New York have been vocal.

The broader market is also shifting. The state's downstate commercial casino licensing process and the debate over online casino gaming both bear on the value of Seneca exclusivity, a theme explored in our analysis of competition inside the Western New York exclusivity zone. For a broader view of how New York compares with other tribal gaming states, see our state comparison tool.

Abrams begins her term with a full agenda: an unsigned compact, host communities waiting on budget certainty, and active litigation over land. Her opening remarks suggest she intends to press the nation's position on all three while leaving the door open to a negotiated outcome with Albany.
