New York Sues Interior Over Seneca Nation's Grand Island Restricted Fee Land

The state's federal suit targets a single undeveloped parcel, but it questions the statute behind the Nation's Western New York land base weeks before the compact expires.

By Elena RuizPublished November 19, 20265 min read

New York State has gone to federal court to undo the Interior Department's decision to place 207 acres of Seneca Nation land on Grand Island under restricted fee status, opening a new legal front in a relationship already strained by an expiring gaming compact. The complaint, filed November 6 in the U.S. District Court for the Western District of New York, asks the court to vacate the February 24 determination and to declare that the land-acquisition funds created by the Seneca Nation Settlement Act of 1990 have been exhausted, a ruling that would effectively close the statute's expedited path for future Seneca parcels.

The Seneca Nation restricted fee dispute is not about a casino. The Grand Island parcel, adjacent to Interstate 190 on the island's northwest corner, has no announced development plan, and the Nation has said only that its leadership will review opportunities for the site. But the case goes to the heart of the legal machinery that built the Nation's Western New York gaming footprint, and the state has expressly tied its argument to the Seneca gaming compact.

What the state is challenging

The Seneca Nation Settlement Act was enacted to resolve long-running disputes over leases on Seneca land in and around the City of Salamanca. Among its provisions, the Act allows land the Nation acquires with settlement funds, within defined geographic areas, to be held in restricted fee status once the Secretary of the Interior agrees. Restricted fee land is owned by the Nation rather than held in trust by the United States, but it carries a federal restriction against alienation and is treated as Indian country, outside most state and local taxing and regulatory authority. The distinction from trust land is covered in our fee land vs. trust land explainer.

According to reporting by Tribal Business News, Interior found in February that the Grand Island parcel satisfied the Act's geographic and funding requirements. The state disputes both findings. It argues that the settlement money eligible for land purchases was spent years ago, that Grand Island lies outside the areas the statute covers, and that the transaction itself cannot qualify. The parcel was conveyed to the Nation for $1 and recorded as a land donation; Interior relied on evidence that the dollar came from an account the Nation identified as holding settlement funds. The state also says Interior never addressed its objections, including its requests for an accounting of the remaining funds.

Most consequential for gaming observers is the state's claim that the Interior decision conflicts with the tribal-state gaming compact, which the state says governs how remaining settlement funds may be used. The Nation's Niagara Falls and Buffalo casino parcels were placed in restricted fee status under the same Act in the years following the 2002 compact, which is why any judicial finding about the Act's remaining reach carries weight well beyond one undeveloped tract.

A crowded docket over Seneca land

The state's case is the latest of several. The Town of Grand Island filed its own federal suit against Interior in June, arguing that the February decision violated the Settlement Act and citing lost property tax revenue, lost zoning and environmental authority, and impaired drainage easements. The town has said the parcel accounts for more than 40 percent of its undeveloped commercial land. Separately, the City of Buffalo has challenged a 2023 restricted fee decision covering a roughly three-acre parcel near the Buffalo River where the Nation is building a gas station; a state judge in October declined to halt that construction, and the state's new filings also target the Buffalo site.

The Nation has responded sharply. In remarks reported by Tribal Business News, then-President J. Conrad Seneca described the suit as Governor Kathy Hochul's latest attempt "to extinguish us and our economic ability to serve our people," and said the Nation learned of it through the media. A spokesperson for the governor said Hochul respects tribal sovereignty but must ensure that development follows applicable laws and oversight, and that the state believes Interior incorrectly qualified the parcels.

The narrow question is whether one parcel qualified for restricted fee status. The broader question is whether the 1990 Settlement Act still has any land-acquisition capacity left at all.

Why the timing matters for the Seneca compact

The lawsuit lands weeks before the Seneca compact's scheduled December 9 expiration. The 2002 agreement, extended once, gives the Nation exclusivity over casino-style gaming across a defined Western New York zone in exchange for revenue shared with the state and host communities, and it underpins the Seneca Niagara, Seneca Allegany and Seneca Buffalo Creek properties. Tina Abrams, sworn in as Seneca Nation president on November 10, has said she is prepared to meet with the governor but has placed responsibility for the stalled talks on Albany. Our coverage of Abrams's inauguration and the unsigned compact details where negotiations stood as she took office.

Land status and compact negotiations are formally separate tracks, but in practice they are rarely insulated from one another. A state that is simultaneously asking a federal court to declare the Settlement Act's acquisition funds spent, and negotiating the revenue terms of a successor compact, has signaled that it views the Nation's land base and its gaming economy as connected questions. For the Nation, a ruling for the state would limit one of the few statutory tools it has to expand jurisdiction over land in its historic territory without going through the slower federal trust-acquisition process.

What happens next

Interior will have to answer the complaint, and the Nation, which is not a named defendant in the reported filing, may seek to participate. Tribes in that position often weigh intervention carefully because joining a case can raise questions about the scope of their sovereign immunity; our explainer on tribal intervention walks through the trade-offs. With the town, city and state suits all pending in the same district, consolidation or coordinated briefing is a realistic possibility.

Any decision is likely months away, well past the compact deadline. In the near term, the case adds a layer of friction to talks that already lacked momentum.
