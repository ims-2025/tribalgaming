Saint Regis Mohawk Tribe: Akwesasne Mohawk Casino Gaming Profile

A cross-border community, a compact that took eleven years to secure, and an eight-county exclusivity zone shape one of New York's oldest tribal casinos.

By Daniel YazziePublished November 24, 20264 min read

The Saint Regis Mohawk Tribe's gaming operation at Akwesasne is one of the most distinctive in Indian Country. It sits on a reservation that crosses the international border, runs under a tribal-state compact that took more than a decade to secure, and is protected by an eight-county exclusivity zone that New York agreed to respect. This profile covers the history of the Akwesasne Mohawk Casino, the compact under which it operates, and the revenue-sharing dispute that changed the Tribe's relationship with the state.

The Akwesasne Mohawk Casino and its setting

Akwesasne is a Mohawk community on both sides of the St. Lawrence River. Its territory spans northern New York, Ontario and Quebec. The U.S. portion, recognized in the 1796 Treaty with the Seven Nations of Canada, is governed by the Saint Regis Mohawk Tribe. The Mohawk Council of Akwesasne governs the Canadian portion. That arrangement means one community lives under two federal systems, and gaming has always developed with the cross-border situation in the background. On the Canadian side, Mohawk gaming regulation is best known through the Kahnawà:ke Gaming Commission near Montreal, covered in our report on Kahnawake and Canadian iGaming sovereignty.

The Akwesasne Mohawk Casino Resort opened on April 12, 1999 at 873 State Route 37 in Hogansburg, New York. It has grown in stages. A $74 million expansion in 2011 was followed in May 2013 by a seven-story, 150-room hotel with an indoor pool, spa and restaurants. Published descriptions put the gaming floor at about 140,000 square feet, with more than 1,800 slot machines and about 30 table games. The Tribe also runs Mohawk Bingo Palace, a separate bingo hall that includes a poker room. For many visitors it is the more traditional part of the Tribe's gaming business.

A compact that took eleven years

The Tribe signed a Class III gaming compact with Governor Mario Cuomo in 1993, but the agreement did not hold up legally. In June 2003, New York's Court of Appeals ruled that the 1993 compacts with the Saint Regis Mohawk Tribe and the Oneida Indian Nation were invalid, because the governor had entered them without legislative ratification. The casino had been operating since 1999, so the decision put its legal footing in doubt.

The legislature then acted. The state Senate ratified the compact in late 2003, and the Assembly followed on June 23, 2004. Under the ratified terms, the Tribe agreed to install 1,000 slot machines within a year and to share between 18 and 25 percent of slot revenue with the state. Franklin and St. Lawrence counties, which surround the reservation, receive a quarter of the state's share. The case shows a point that remains true in compact law: under the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act, a compact is only as secure as the state-law authority of the official who signs it. The revenue-sharing dispute and the 2013 settlement

In 2010, the Tribe stopped making revenue-sharing payments. It argued that New York had violated its exclusivity by allowing competing gaming in the region. Over the following years the disputed amount grew to about $59 million, and the conflict became one of several between the state and its gaming tribes.

Under the May 2013 settlement signed by Governor Andrew Cuomo, the Tribe paid about $30 million in past revenue sharing, resumed regular payments at 25 percent of net gaming revenue, and received a defined eight-county exclusivity zone in northern New York.

Of the $30 million, a quarter ($7.5 million) went to St. Lawrence and Franklin counties, $3.75 million each. Going forward, the counties would continue to receive a quarter of the state's share. The state's remaining share was placed in escrow pending resolution of separate land-claim issues and issues involving the New York Power Authority. Chief Ron LaFrance, Jr. supported the agreement. In return, the state agreed to keep commercial casino siting out of an exclusivity zone covering Clinton, Essex, Franklin, Hamilton, Jefferson, Lewis, St. Lawrence and Warren counties. This was a significant concession, made as New York was preparing to authorize new upstate commercial casinos.

The settlement was part of a broader set of agreements between New York and its gaming tribes that year, each built around geographic exclusivity in exchange for revenue sharing. Why the Akwesasne model matters

The Saint Regis Mohawk experience shows three things about tribal gaming outside the large Sun Belt and West Coast markets. First, a casino in a rural, cross-border region depends heavily on the terms of its compact. Exclusivity in a sparsely populated area protects a modest market, but losing it would cause real damage. Second, the payments benefit more than the state. Franklin and St. Lawrence counties have a direct financial interest in the casino's success, which gives the Tribe local allies in Albany. Third, the 2003 court ruling showed that the legal basis of a compact can be challenged long after a casino opens. Tribes elsewhere took note.
