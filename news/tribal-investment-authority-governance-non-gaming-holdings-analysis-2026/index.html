Tribal Investment Authorities: Governing Non-Gaming Holdings

San Manuel's Scottsdale deal highlights a quiet structural shift: tribes housing diversification in governmental investment vehicles apart from their casinos.

By James KingfisherPublished October 14, 20264 min read

When the San Manuel Investment Authority closed on the Fairmont Scottsdale Princess in October 2026, the headline was a 750-room resort in Arizona. The more instructive detail for industry readers was the identity of the buyer: not a casino operator, but an investment authority described as an unincorporated governmental instrumentality of the Yuhaaviatam of San Manuel Nation. The rise of the tribal investment authority as a distinct vehicle for non-gaming capital is one of the quieter structural shifts in Indian gaming, and it deserves a closer look.

Why separate investing from operating

A tribal casino is an operating business. It has regulators, a management team, a licensed workforce and a budget tied to gaming revenue. Under the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act, net gaming revenues may be used only for specified purposes: funding tribal government operations or programs, providing for the general welfare of the tribe and its members, promoting tribal economic development, donating to charitable organizations, and helping fund operations of local government agencies. Per capita payments are permitted only under an approved revenue allocation plan. Our explainer on the five permitted uses of net gaming revenue details these limits.

Economic development is the category that supports reinvestment. But deciding to buy a hotel in another state is a different kind of decision from approving a slot floor refresh. It involves real estate underwriting, financing, hospitality management contracts and long-term portfolio planning. Housing that work in a separate governmental body allows tribes to staff it with investment professionals, set its own risk policies and report results to the tribal council separately from casino performance.

Governmental instrumentality versus corporation

Tribes choose among several legal forms for enterprises. Some charter corporations under tribal law, others organize under federal Section 17 of the Indian Reorganization Act, and others create authorities or instrumentalities that are arms of the tribal government. The form matters because it affects sovereign immunity, taxation, liability exposure and how lenders and counterparties structure their agreements. Our explainer on the Section 17 corporation and other enterprise structures compares the options in detail.

Courts have addressed when an entity qualifies as an arm of a tribe for purposes of immunity, generally looking at how it was created, its purpose, the tribe's control and the relationship between its finances and the tribe's. The label alone is not dispositive. For counterparties, this is why commercial agreements with tribal entities frequently include negotiated limited waivers of immunity and defined dispute-resolution forums, a topic covered in our piece on limited sovereign immunity waivers. The public reports of the Scottsdale transaction do not describe any such terms, and we have not assumed them.

What the San Manuel portfolio illustrates

The Nation's holdings give a concrete picture of how an investment authority can be used. On the gaming side are Yaamava' Resort & Casino in Highland, California, and the Palms Casino Resort in Las Vegas, purchased in May 2021 for $650 million in cash from Red Rock Resorts. On the non-gaming side are the Waldorf Astoria Monarch Beach in Dana Point, in which the tribe acquired a 40 percent stake in July 2023 and later majority control, and now the Scottsdale resort. Hotel operations at Monarch Beach are managed by Hilton, and golf by Troon, which shows another common feature of this model: owners often retain established brands and operators rather than managing luxury hospitality themselves.

That division of labor is conservative in a useful way. The tribe supplies capital and long-term ownership; specialists supply operating expertise. Fairmont will continue to operate the Scottsdale property, according to local reporting.

Governance questions worth asking

Because investment authorities are less visible than casino commissions, the quality of their governance depends heavily on internal tribal law. Several questions are worth asking of any such vehicle, and none can be answered from outside:

Who sits on the authority's board, and how are members appointed and removed? What are the limits on single investments as a share of the tribe's assets? How are returns distributed between reinvestment, reserves and tribal programs? What reporting goes to the tribal council and to members? Our analysis of enterprise board governance capacity and the piece on financial disclosure and transparency examine how uneven these practices are across Indian Country. San Manuel's size and the sophistication of its advisers do not necessarily describe smaller tribes considering similar moves.

Why it matters beyond one tribe

Outside capital providers, including lenders and real estate investors, increasingly encounter tribal governments as buyers, not just borrowers or landlords. Our coverage of REITs and outside capital in tribal resort financing describes the other side of that relationship. As more tribes follow the model, the institutional details of investment authorities, such as their charters, immunity provisions and approval thresholds, will become standard diligence items.

The Scottsdale acquisition does not change federal gaming law or the compact framework. What it does is show how a mature tribal enterprise can use governmental structure to turn gaming income into a durable, diversified asset base. For tribes weighing similar strategies, the central lesson is less about hotels than about governance: the investment vehicle is only as strong as the rules that bind it.
