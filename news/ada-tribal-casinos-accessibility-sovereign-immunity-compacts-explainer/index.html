Does the ADA Apply to Tribal Casinos? Accessibility, Immunity and Compacts

Federal courts have said the ADA's public-accommodation rules can reach tribal casinos. Sovereign immunity means the real obligations usually come from somewhere else.

By Daniel YazziePublished November 17, 20265 min read

Tribal casinos welcome millions of guests each year, a growing share of them older adults and people with disabilities. Yet the question of whether the Americans with Disabilities Act applies to tribal casinos has a more complicated answer than it does for a commercial resort down the road. The short version: federal courts have held that the ADA's public-accommodation rules can apply to tribal gaming facilities, but tribal sovereign immunity generally prevents private individuals from suing a tribe to enforce them. In practice, accessibility at tribal casinos is shaped as much by tribal-state compacts, tribal law and market expectations as by the federal statute itself.

This explainer covers how the ADA is structured, what courts have said about its reach on Indian lands, and where the real accessibility obligations at tribal casinos tend to come from.

How the ADA is organized

Congress enacted the ADA in 1990. Two parts are most relevant to casinos. Title I prohibits disability discrimination in employment. Title III prohibits discrimination by places of public accommodation, a category that explicitly includes hotels, restaurants, places of entertainment and other facilities open to the public, and it sets design standards for new construction and alterations.

The statute treats tribes differently in those two titles. Title I expressly excludes "an Indian tribe" from its definition of an employer, the same exclusion Congress wrote into Title VII of the Civil Rights Act. A tribal casino operated by the tribe or an arm of the tribe is therefore generally outside the ADA's employment provisions, though tribal employment codes and policies often address disability discrimination on their own terms. Title III contains no comparable exclusion, which is where the legal question begins.

Does Title III reach tribal casinos?

When a federal statute is silent about tribes, courts apply a set of doctrines to decide whether it covers them. Under the general-applicability rule traced to Federal Power Commission v. Tuscarora Indian Nation, a statute of general application is often presumed to apply to tribes unless it would interfere with exclusive rights of self-governance in purely intramural matters, abrogate treaty rights, or conflict with evidence of congressional intent. Our explainer on which federal laws apply to tribal casinos covers that framework and how circuits differ in applying it.

The leading appellate decision on the ADA is Florida Paraplegic Association v. Miccosukee Tribe of Indians of Florida, decided by the Eleventh Circuit in 1999. A disability-rights group sued the tribe over accessibility at its restaurant and gaming facility. The court concluded that Title III does apply to a tribe's commercial public accommodations such as a casino, reasoning that a business serving the general public is not a purely intramural matter of tribal self-government.

But the court also held that the plaintiffs could not sue. Congress had not clearly and unequivocally abrogated tribal sovereign immunity in the ADA, and the tribe had not waived it. The statute applied, in other words, but the private suit could not proceed. That combination of substantive coverage without a private remedy is the central feature of ADA law at tribal casinos.

Applicability and enforceability are different questions. A federal law can govern a tribal casino's conduct while tribal immunity still blocks a private lawsuit to enforce it.

Why immunity matters so much

Tribal sovereign immunity protects tribes and their gaming enterprises from suit unless Congress abrogates it or the tribe waives it. The Supreme Court reaffirmed the doctrine's breadth in the gaming context in Michigan v. Bay Mills Indian Community in 2014, declining to carve out an exception for off-reservation commercial activity. Our Bay Mills explainer discusses that decision.

Immunity applies to suits by private parties and, generally, by states. It does not ordinarily bar suits brought by the United States, which means federal enforcement of Title III against a tribal facility remains a theoretical avenue. In practice, federal enforcement actions targeting tribal casinos over accessibility have been rare, and most disputes are resolved through tribal processes rather than in federal court.

Where accessibility obligations actually come from

Because private ADA suits are largely unavailable, the obligations that shape accessibility at tribal casinos usually come from three other sources.

Tribal-state compacts. Many compacts address facility construction and public health and safety, and some expressly require ADA compliance. California's modern compacts are a clear example. The 2020 compact between the state and the Shingle Springs Band of Miwok Indians, for instance, provides that the gaming facility will comply with the federal Americans with Disabilities Act in construction, expansion, modification and renovation. A tribe that agrees to such a provision has a contractual obligation to the state, enforceable through the compact's dispute-resolution process, regardless of whether a private plaintiff could sue under the statute. Readers can find more on compact structure in our Legal Guide.

Tribal law. Tribes frequently adopt building codes, accessibility standards and patron-complaint procedures through their own legislation. Tort claims ordinances, often required by compacts, provide a forum and a limited waiver of immunity for injury claims, and some tribal codes extend similar procedures to accessibility complaints. Those remedies run through tribal courts or administrative processes rather than federal court.

Financing and market expectations. Lenders, insurers, hospitality brand partners and management companies commonly require compliance with recognized accessibility standards as a condition of financing or licensing a brand. Large tribal resorts built in recent years are generally designed to the same standards as commercial properties, in part because their customer base expects it. An aging core gaming audience gives operators a direct commercial incentive to invest in accessible parking, routes, gaming positions, hotel rooms and entertainment seating.

New questions: digital and mobile access

Accessibility questions are extending beyond the physical casino. Tribal operators now run websites, loyalty apps, self-service kiosks and, in some states, mobile sportsbooks and online casinos. Courts have been divided over how Title III applies to websites and apps generally, and the tribal immunity analysis adds another layer. Where a tribe's online gaming operates under a state framework or through a commercial partner, the contracts and state rules governing that arrangement may impose their own accessibility requirements.

The bottom line

The ADA's public-accommodation rules likely apply to tribal casinos, at least in the Eleventh Circuit and in courts that follow similar reasoning, but sovereign immunity generally prevents private plaintiffs from enforcing them. That does not leave accessibility unregulated. Compacts, tribal codes, financing requirements and the competitive demands of the market together create a set of obligations that, at most modern tribal resorts, look much like the standards a commercial casino follows. For guests with accessibility concerns, the practical route is usually the property's own guest-relations process and, where it exists, the tribe's gaming commission or patron-dispute procedure.
