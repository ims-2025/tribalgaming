Fort Mojave Signs 25-Year California Compact With No Revenue-Sharing

The tri-state tribe's new California agreement drops the revenue-sharing payment—the clearest sign yet of how the state's compact template is changing.

By Elena RuizPublished July 24, 20265 min read

California has signed a new 25-year gaming compact with the Fort Mojave Indian Tribe, clearing a path for the tri-state tribe to operate class III casinos on the California side of its reservation without sharing gaming revenue with the state. Governor Gavin Newsom signed the agreement in late July 2026, and it now moves to the U.S. Department of the Interior for the federal review that every tribal-state compact must clear before it takes effect.

The deal is notable less for its size than for its structure. Under its terms, the Fort Mojave Indian Tribe may develop up to two class III gaming facilities on eligible trust land, and—unlike many of the older compacts that still govern California's largest operators—it does not obligate the tribe to remit a share of net win to Sacramento's general fund. For a tribe whose reservation straddles the point where California, Arizona and Nevada meet along the Colorado River, the agreement gives its California footprint a legal foundation that matches the gaming already operating on the Arizona side.

Why revenue-sharing is fading from new California deals

The absence of a revenue-sharing payment is the compact's most consequential feature. Federal law limits what a state may demand in exchange for a compact: under the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act, a state can negotiate for payments only where it offers the tribe something of meaningful value in return, most often a measure of market exclusivity. Courts have repeatedly held that a state cannot simply tax tribal gaming through the compact process, and California has spent much of the past decade recalibrating its demands after litigation tested how far revenue-sharing clauses could stretch.

The result is a template that increasingly channels tribal payments toward two state-administered funds—one that supports non-gaming tribes and another that offsets the regulatory and public-service costs a casino generates—rather than toward the general treasury. The Fort Mojave agreement follows that pattern, and it lands amid a broader wave of California compacting activity that has reshaped how the nation's biggest tribal-gaming market allocates its proceeds. Readers tracking that shift can see it in the tribe-by-tribe terms explained in our guide to compact revenue-sharing.

A modern California compact increasingly asks a tribe to fund the state's regulatory costs and support non-gaming tribes—not to hand a percentage of its win to the general fund.

What it means for the tribe and the region

For the Fort Mojave Indian Tribe, the compact converts a long-standing ambition into a workable business case. The tribe already runs gaming on the Arizona portion of its land, and a California-side facility would let it capture demand from the Needles corridor and the broader Mohave Valley without ceding players to commercial operators across the Nevada line. Whether the tribe builds one modest hall or pursues a larger destination property will depend on financing and on how the Interior Department treats the eligibility of the specific parcels involved.

The agreement also reflects the steady, market-by-market reinvestment that defines mid-sized tribal operators, a pattern visible across the state's gaming map. For a fuller picture of how California's tribes are positioned—from the coastal resorts to the desert properties—our California state hub tracks the operators, the compacts and the pipeline projects now under review.

Fort Mojave joins a lengthening list of California tribes that have signed or extended compacts in the current cycle, including the recent Yurok Tribe extension advanced under state enabling legislation. Each deal is negotiated on its own terms, but together they signal a maturing framework in which the state increasingly trades regulatory certainty for support of tribal self-sufficiency rather than a cut of the revenue.

The road through Washington

Signing is not the final step. The compact must be submitted to the Secretary of the Interior, who has 45 days to approve it, reject it, or allow it to take effect by operation of law to the extent it is consistent with the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act. Only after the agreement is published in the Federal Register can gaming proceed under its terms. That review is typically a formality for compacts built on established templates, but it remains the checkpoint at which any structural novelty—including the treatment of revenue-sharing—is measured against federal law.

For now, the Fort Mojave compact stands as another data point in a clear trend: California's newest tribal-state agreements are being written without the revenue-sharing obligations that defined the first generation of Golden State gaming, a shift that tilts more of the economic benefit back toward the tribes that generate it.

A tri-state tribe with room to grow

Context matters for reading the deal's scale. The Fort Mojave reservation is unusual in spanning three states, and the tribe already operates gaming on its Arizona land, where it recently invested in a new hall to modernize an aging property. A California compact does not force the tribe to choose between the two sides of the Colorado River so much as it lets the tribe build a coherent strategy across a market that has always been split by state lines. How aggressively the tribe develops the California side will hinge on financing conditions and on demand in the Needles corridor, a smaller market than the coastal metros but one with limited competition on the California bank of the river.

The compact's structure also carries a quieter policy message. By routing tribal contributions toward regulatory costs and support for non-gaming tribes rather than the general fund, the state effectively frames tribal gaming as an engine of tribal self-sufficiency rather than a revenue source to be taxed. That framing has become the through-line of California's recent compacting, and each new agreement that adopts it makes the template harder to reverse in future negotiations. For tribes still operating under older, revenue-sharing-heavy deals, the Fort Mojave agreement is one more benchmark to cite when their own compacts come up for renewal.
