Title 31 Explained: Currency Reporting Rules at Tribal Casinos

The cash-reporting regime that shapes every tribal casino cage, in plain terms.

By Daniel YazziePublished September 12, 20266 min read

Ask a tribal casino cage supervisor what shapes their day and the answer will not be gaming regulation. It will be Title 31 — the shorthand the industry uses for the currency reporting and anti-money-laundering obligations that flow from the Bank Secrecy Act. Title 31 currency transaction reports sit alongside, and entirely apart from, the tribal and state gaming rules that govern what games a property may offer. Understanding the difference is essential to understanding how a tribal casino is actually regulated.

The short version: under federal law, a casino above a modest revenue threshold is treated as a financial institution. It must run a written anti-money-laundering program, report large cash transactions, report transactions it finds suspicious, and keep records that let examiners reconstruct what happened. Tribal casinos operating under the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act have been inside this regime since the mid-1990s, when Treasury extended the casino rules to tribal gaming operations.

Who is covered, and by whom

The trigger is revenue. A casino with gross annual gaming revenue above $1 million falls within the definition of a financial institution for Bank Secrecy Act purposes. That threshold captures essentially every Class III tribal casino and the large majority of Class II operations of any meaningful size. Very small bingo halls and gaming centres can fall below it.

The rule-writer is the Financial Crimes Enforcement Network, a bureau of the Treasury Department. Examination authority for casinos is delegated to the Internal Revenue Service, which conducts Title 31 examinations of gaming properties. This is the point that surprises people new to the sector: the federal agency most likely to audit a tribal casino's cash controls is not the National Indian Gaming Commission. The NIGC enforces IGRA and the minimum internal control standards, a function described in our explainer on how the NIGC regulates tribal gaming. Title 31 is a separate federal track with separate examiners and separate penalties.

Tribal sovereignty does not exempt a tribal casino from these obligations. The Bank Secrecy Act applies to the gaming operation as a business, and tribal gaming regulatory authorities generally incorporate Title 31 compliance into their own oversight rather than contesting its application.

The three core reporting duties

Currency transaction reports. A casino must file a CTR when cash transactions by or on behalf of one person exceed $10,000 in a single gaming day, counted separately for cash in and cash out. Cash in includes buy-ins at a table, cash inserted for slot play, front money deposits, and wire transfers funded with currency. Cash out includes chip redemptions, jackpot payments in cash, and withdrawals of front money. A patron who buys in for $6,000 at one table and $5,500 at another has crossed the threshold, even though neither transaction did on its own.

Suspicious activity reports. A casino must file a SAR when it knows, suspects, or has reason to suspect that a transaction at or above $5,000 involves funds from illegal activity, is designed to evade reporting requirements, has no apparent lawful purpose, or is being used to facilitate criminal activity. The judgment-based nature of this obligation makes it harder than CTR filing. The classic pattern is structuring: a patron who buys in for $9,500 repeatedly, or who asks whether a smaller amount avoids paperwork, has generated a reportable event regardless of whether any single transaction crossed $10,000. SARs are confidential; a casino may not tell the subject that one was filed.

Recordkeeping. Certain transactions at or above $3,000 require the casino to record and retain identifying information even though no report is filed. This is the layer examiners most often find deficient, because it generates no filing and therefore no obvious paper trail of compliance.

Aggregation is where properties get into trouble

The reporting thresholds are only meaningful if a property can see all of a patron's activity across the floor within a gaming day. That requires the cage, the table games pit, the slot department, and the sportsbook to feed into a common view, and it requires a clearly defined gaming day — a 24-hour period the casino designates, which need not align with the calendar day but must be applied consistently.

Title 31 failures are almost never a failure to understand the rule. They are a failure to see all of a patron's activity in one place.

Multi-property operators face an additional wrinkle. Where a tribe operates several casinos, the aggregation obligation depends on how the operations are structured and whether they share a common patron database. Tribes running portfolios of three, five, or more properties — a common structure in Oklahoma, among others, as reflected across our Oklahoma state hub — have to resolve this deliberately rather than assume each property reports in isolation.

What a compliance program has to contain

The regulations require a written program reasonably designed to ensure compliance, and examiners look for five elements: internal controls and procedures; independent testing of those controls; a designated compliance officer with genuine authority; ongoing training for affected employees; and automated data processing capable of supporting the required aggregation and monitoring.

Each of those elements has a practical failure mode. Internal controls fail when written procedures drift from floor practice. Independent testing fails when it is performed by the same department it reviews. The compliance officer role fails when it is a title added to a revenue-generating job without protected time or reporting lines. Training fails when it is annual and generic rather than role-specific. Systems fail when a new pit or a new sportsbook is added without extending the monitoring that covers the rest of the floor.

The cost of all this is not trivial, particularly for smaller and mid-sized properties that carry essentially the same compliance obligation as a major resort across a much smaller revenue base — a burden examined in our analysis of AML compliance costs in tribal gaming.

What players should know

Two persistent misconceptions are worth correcting. First, a CTR is not a tax document and filing one does not create a tax liability. Gambling winnings are taxable, but that obligation runs through a different form entirely; our explainer on casino winnings, W-2G, and withholding covers that track. A CTR reports the movement of currency, not income.

Second, being asked for identification at the cage is not an accusation. It is a legal requirement the casino cannot waive, and a patron who declines to provide it will not be able to complete the transaction. Staff have no discretion on this point, and the request carries no implication about the patron.

The broader takeaway is that a tribal casino sits inside two distinct regulatory systems at once: a gaming system rooted in tribal sovereignty and IGRA, and a financial-crimes system that applies to it the same way it applies to a bank. Conflating the two is the most common source of confusion about how tribal gaming is overseen.
