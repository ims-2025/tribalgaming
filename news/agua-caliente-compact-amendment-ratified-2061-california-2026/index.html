California ratifies Agua Caliente compact amendment running to 2061

AB 2173 adds up to 500 devices, restructures Revenue Sharing Trust Fund contributions, and leaves one federal step outstanding.

By Marcus WhitehorsePublished September 19, 20265 min read

California has ratified an amended tribal-state gaming compact with the Agua Caliente Band of Cahuilla Indians that runs through December 31, 2061 and authorizes up to 500 additional gaming devices, extending one of the state's longest-running gaming relationships by more than three decades.

Governor Gavin Newsom signed Assembly Bill 2173, carried by Assemblymember Greg Wallis, on September 15. The bill cleared both houses of the Legislature without opposition. The amended compact itself was executed on July 13, 2026, before being sent to Sacramento for ratification — the sequence California uses for every compact amendment, and one that leaves a final federal step outstanding.

Alongside the term extension and the device authorization, the amendment restructures the tribe's contributions to the Revenue Sharing Trust Fund, the mechanism through which gaming tribes in California support tribes that operate limited gaming or none at all.

What the Agua Caliente compact amendment actually changes

Three provisions carry the weight here, and they are not equally significant.

The device authorization is the most immediately operational. Agua Caliente runs Agua Caliente Casino Resort Spa in Rancho Mirage, Agua Caliente Cathedral City and Agua Caliente Palm Springs, a three-property cluster in one of California's stronger destination markets. An allocation of up to 500 additional machines is a capacity decision rather than a new-market decision: it gives the tribe headroom to expand floors it already operates, or to weight devices across properties as demand shifts. In a mature market, marginal device authorizations tend to be used for product mix and peak-period capacity rather than raw square footage, and the practical value depends heavily on where the tribe is already bumping against its cap. Our explainer on how device caps work in tribal-state compacts covers the mechanics in more detail.

The 2061 term is the more consequential provision for anyone underwriting the tribe's debt. A 35-year runway is long even by California standards, and compact term length has become a live issue in tribal capital markets precisely because lenders and rating agencies discount cash flows that extend past a compact's expiration. A tribe negotiating a hotel tower or an entertainment venue against a compact with eight years left faces materially different financing terms than one with a horizon into the 2060s.

The Revenue Sharing Trust Fund restructuring is the least visible and, over time, possibly the most contested. The RSTF was designed in the 1999 compacts to distribute payments from gaming tribes to non-gaming and limited-gaming tribes, and its funding formula has been reworked repeatedly as individual compacts have been renegotiated. Recent California amendments have generally moved away from percentage-of-net-win payments to the state's general fund while preserving some form of RSTF contribution, and the details of each restructuring determine how much the fund actually receives.

One amendment in an unusually busy California year

The Agua Caliente bill is part of a cluster. The same legislative cycle produced Senate Bill 542, which modifies and extends the compact with the Santa Ynez Band of Chumash Indians, and Senate Bill 1235, which finalizes the fifth amendment to the compact with the Picayune Rancheria of Chukchansi Indians and ratifies a new compact for the Fort Mojave Indian Tribe. Separate measures handled the Yurok Tribe's compact extension and earlier ratifications for Tule River and Twenty-Nine Palms.

Taken together, the 2026 batch reflects a state that has settled into a recognizable posture: long terms, incremental device relief, and a steady retreat from the broad revenue-sharing obligations that characterized the 1999 and 2004 compact generations. California's gaming tribes have argued for years that general-fund payments function as a tax on tribal government revenue and sit uneasily with IGRA's limits on what a state may demand in exchange for a compact. The state, for its part, has been settling litigation risk on that question rather than defending it.

The practical effect is that California compacts increasingly look less like revenue instruments for the state and more like long-dated regulatory agreements. Readers following that shift can find our analysis of the move toward revenue-sharing-free compacts and the broader California tribal gaming landscape in the directory.

The step that has not happened yet

State ratification is not the end of the process. A Class III compact or amendment takes effect only when the Secretary of the Interior approves it — or allows it to take effect by operation of law after 45 days — and notice of approval is published in the Federal Register. Until that publication, the additional devices are authorized on paper rather than available on the floor.

That final step is rarely dramatic, but it is not automatic, and the timeline has been less predictable in 2026 than in prior years. Interior has been working through a heavier compact docket while operating under leadership and budget constraints that have slowed approvals across Indian gaming generally. Tribes that have completed state ratification have in several cases waited longer than expected for the Federal Register notice, and a handful have taken effect by operation of law rather than by affirmative approval.

None of that suggests a problem with the Agua Caliente amendment specifically. The document was negotiated with the state, executed in July, and ratified without a dissenting vote — about as clean a record as a compact amendment produces. It does mean that the operative date for the tribe's new device allocation is a federal question rather than a state one, and that the tribe's planning calendar depends on a notice it does not control.

For the Coachella Valley, the amendment removes a question that had been hanging over one of the region's largest employers. Agua Caliente is among the most urban-adjacent gaming tribes in the country, with landholdings interleaved with the city of Palm Springs, and its gaming revenue underwrites tribal government services alongside a substantial non-gaming portfolio. A compact horizon into the 2060s gives that planning a stability few tribal governments enjoy. Background on the tribe's operations is available in our Agua Caliente enterprise profile.
