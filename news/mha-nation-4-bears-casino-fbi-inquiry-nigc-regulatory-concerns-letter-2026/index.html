FBI Inquiry, NIGC Letter Put MHA Nation's 4 Bears Casino Under Scrutiny

A federal inquiry and a regulator's warning letter land in the same week, just as a new tribal administration takes office on Fort Berthold.

By Marcus WhitehorsePublished November 26, 20264 min read

The FBI is examining what has been described as "fraud matters" at 4 Bears Casino, the flagship gaming property of the Mandan, Hidatsa and Arikara Nation (MHA Nation) on the Fort Berthold Reservation in western North Dakota. The disclosure, first reported by the Las Vegas Review-Journal on November 25, arrived alongside a separate development: a November 17 letter from the National Indian Gaming Commission citing "serious regulatory concerns" at the casino involving record keeping, accounting, internal controls and federal compliance.

Neither the FBI nor the NIGC has commented publicly, and no charges have been filed against anyone. What exists so far is a set of disclosures by the tribe's own leadership and a regulatory letter whose full contents have not been released. That distinction matters, and it shapes how the situation should be read.

What the tribe has disclosed

According to the reporting, the MHA Nation's newly elected chairman told a November 19 tribal leadership meeting that he had been informed of the inquiry by the casino's co-chief executive and that FBI agents had visited his office. He pledged the tribe's full cooperation. The chairman also placed the tribe's chief financial officer on paid administrative leave, while stating publicly that she is not a subject of the FBI inquiry.

The timing is notable. The new administration took office after a leadership change on Fort Berthold, and the casino questions now sit alongside a broader review of decisions made under the prior administration. Chief among those is the tribe's purchase of roughly 23 acres on the Las Vegas Strip for about $115 million under former Chairman Mark Fox, a transaction that was completed without a public membership vote. That land, covered in our earlier report on MHA Nation's Las Vegas Strip plans, remains undeveloped. Nothing in the public record ties the Strip parcel to the FBI inquiry, and the two matters should not be conflated.

The MHA Nation has roughly 17,800 enrolled members. Its gaming operations, centered on 4 Bears near New Town, have grown in recent years alongside the tribe's oil-and-gas revenue, including a sportsbook build-out discussed in our coverage of the 4 Bears sportsbook expansion.

What an NIGC regulatory-concerns letter means

The NIGC is a civil regulator. It does not prosecute crimes, but it oversees tribal compliance with the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act (IGRA) and its implementing rules, including the Minimum Internal Control Standards for Class II gaming in 25 C.F.R. Part 543 and the annual independent audit requirement in Part 571. Its compliance staff routinely review audits, conduct site visits and identify deficiencies.

A letter flagging "serious regulatory concerns" sits early on the agency's escalation ladder. In practice, the NIGC generally prefers to resolve problems through technical assistance and corrective-action plans. If deficiencies persist, the Chair can issue a notice of violation, impose civil fines, or, in serious cases, order a temporary closure, as described in our explainer on how NIGC enforcement works. A concerns letter is not itself an enforcement action, and it does not mean the agency has concluded that any violation occurred.

A regulatory letter and a federal criminal inquiry are separate tracks with separate standards. One asks whether the controls work; the other asks whether anyone broke the law.

The two tracks can inform each other. Weak record keeping and internal controls are exactly the conditions under which irregularities go undetected, and federal investigators often rely on the same audit trail that regulators review. But the existence of an NIGC letter does not establish that a crime occurred, and an FBI inquiry does not establish that the casino's regulatory framework failed.

The federal criminal framework

Because 4 Bears operates under an NIGC-approved tribal gaming ordinance, theft or embezzlement involving the casino falls within federal criminal statutes enacted specifically to protect tribal gaming establishments. Sections 1167 and 1168 of Title 18, added by IGRA in 1988, cover theft from gaming establishments on Indian lands and theft by officers or employees of those establishments, respectively; Section 1163 separately covers embezzlement from tribal organizations. Our companion explainer walks through how those federal theft statutes apply to tribal casinos.

That framework explains why the FBI, rather than state authorities, is the investigating agency. In North Dakota, which is not a Public Law 280 state, federal agencies hold primary criminal jurisdiction over offenses of this kind on reservation lands, often working alongside the Bureau of Indian Affairs and tribal law enforcement.

It is also not the first time federal authorities have scrutinized financial dealings on Fort Berthold. In 2020, federal prosecutors charged three MHA Nation officials in a separate bribery and kickback case. That earlier matter has no established connection to the current inquiry.

What to watch

Several developments will determine how significant this becomes. The first is whether the tribe releases the NIGC letter or describes the corrective steps it intends to take; tribes frequently respond to concerns letters with remediation plans, outside audits and changes in personnel or procedures. The second is whether the tribal gaming commission, the casino's primary on-site regulator, announces its own review. The third is whether the FBI inquiry produces any public filings, which can take months or longer, if they come at all.

For the broader industry, the episode is a reminder that tribal gaming's regulatory structure is layered by design: tribal gaming commissions regulate day to day, the NIGC oversees compliance, and federal criminal law backstops the system. A new tribal administration inheriting a casino under federal scrutiny will be tested on how quickly and transparently it uses each of those layers.
