Historic Horse Racing Machines Explained: Why Tribes Watch HHR Expansion

They look like slot machines and pay like slot machines, but legally they are bets on races that already happened. That gap is where tribal exclusivity disputes begin.

By Daniel YazziePublished November 25, 20264 min read

Historic horse racing machines, usually called HHR, have become one of the most contested forms of gaming expansion in states with tribal casinos. To a casual player, an HHR terminal is indistinguishable from a slot machine: it has a screen, spinning reels, bonus rounds and a bill acceptor. Legally, however, many states treat it as pari-mutuel wagering on horse races, the same category as a bet placed at a racetrack window. That classification determines who may offer the machines, how they are taxed and whether they trigger the exclusivity protections in tribal-state compacts.

How historic horse racing machines work

An HHR system draws from a large library of previously run races, typically thousands of them, with identifying details such as track, date and horse names hidden from the player. When a player makes a wager, the system assigns a race, and the player picks finishers, either by handicapping using summarized past-performance data or by letting the machine choose automatically. The race result, often shown only briefly in a corner of the screen, determines whether the wager wins. The spinning reels and bonus animations are entertainment displays keyed to that outcome.

The feature that separates HHR from a slot machine, in legal terms, is the pool. In a true pari-mutuel system, wagers go into a common pool, the operator takes a commission, and winners are paid from what remains. HHR systems maintain such pools, often seeded so that payouts are available continuously. Supporters argue that this makes HHR a modern form of the pari-mutuel wagering states have authorized at racetracks for decades. Critics, including many tribal governments, argue that the race is incidental and that the product is functionally a slot machine. Our explainer on pari-mutuel wagering as Class III gaming describes where that category fits under federal law.

How states have classified HHR

State treatment varies widely and has often been decided by courts as much as legislatures. Kentucky offers the best-known example. After the Kentucky Supreme Court held in 2020 that the HHR system operating at the state's tracks did not meet the definition of pari-mutuel wagering, the legislature responded in 2021 by amending state law to expressly include historic racing within the pari-mutuel definition. Virginia, Wyoming, New Hampshire, Kansas and Louisiana are among the states that have authorized HHR in some form, generally tied to licensed racetracks or off-track betting facilities.

Idaho went the other direction. The state legislature repealed authorization for historic racing terminals in 2015, and in 2018 voters rejected a ballot measure that would have restored them. The Coeur d'Alene Tribe was among the leading opponents, arguing the machines amounted to slot machines the state constitution does not permit outside tribal compacts. That history is part of our analysis of Idaho's casino history.

Under IGRA, HHR offered on Indian lands would be Class III gaming, which requires a tribal-state compact. The dispute arises when states authorize the same machines off reservation for non-tribal operators.

Why tribal exclusivity is at stake

Many tribal-state compacts grant tribes exclusive rights to operate slot machines or other Class III games in exchange for revenue sharing paid to the state. When a state authorizes HHR at racetracks, the threshold question is whether the machines fall within the games covered by that exclusivity. If they do, the expansion could allow a tribe to reduce or suspend its payments under the compact. If they do not, the state can expand gaming without consequence under the compact, even if tribal properties lose market share. Our explainer on exclusivity clauses and payment suspension covers how those provisions are typically drafted.

The answer depends heavily on compact language. Compacts that define protected games functionally, by how a device plays and pays, tend to give tribes stronger arguments than compacts that refer narrowly to slot machines as defined in state law. This is one reason drafting precision has become a priority in recent compact renegotiations, and it is why tribes monitor HHR bills even in states where racing interests are modest.

Current HHR fights involving tribes

HHR is now an active issue in several tribal markets. In California, tribes have challenged historic racing proposals tied to racetracks, as discussed in our report on Santa Anita and tribal exclusivity. In Massachusetts, legislation that would allow HHR has drawn scrutiny over its potential effect on the Mashpee Wampanoag Tribe's long-sought compact, as covered in our Massachusetts HHR analysis. Other states with large racing industries periodically revisit the idea as a way to support purses and breeding programs.

For tribal leaders and regulators, the practical lesson is that HHR is rarely just a racing issue. Its classification touches compact exclusivity, revenue sharing, consumer protection standards and the broader question of which entities a state permits to offer slot-style gaming.
