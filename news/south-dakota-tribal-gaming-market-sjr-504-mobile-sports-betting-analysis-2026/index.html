South Dakota Tribal Gaming: Retail Sportsbooks and the Stalled Mobile Debate

Nine-plus tribal properties, a Deadwood-centered commercial market and a one-vote House loss on mobile betting define the state's next gaming fight.

By James KingfisherPublished November 25, 20264 min read

South Dakota tribal gaming is one of the least discussed tribal markets in the country, but it sits at the center of a policy question that will return in 2027: whether the state will authorize statewide mobile sports betting, and if so, whether tribal nations will have a place in it. The question nearly reached voters this year before a single vote in a House committee ended it, and the November defeat of a proposed supermajority rule for constitutional amendments has kept the path open.

Who operates in South Dakota tribal gaming

South Dakota's tribal casinos are owned by several of the state's Lakota, Dakota and Nakota nations and are concentrated in rural reservation communities rather than population centers. Properties include Prairie Wind Casino & Hotel at Pine Ridge and East Winds Casino in Martin, operated by the Oglala Sioux Tribe; Rosebud Casino near the Nebraska line and Turtle Creek Crossing in Mission, operated by the Rosebud Sioux Tribe; Royal River Casino & Hotel in Flandreau, owned by the Flandreau Santee Sioux Tribe; Fort Randall Casino in Pickstown, owned by the Yankton Sioux Tribe; Golden Buffalo Casino & Resort at Lower Brule; Lode Star Casino in Fort Thompson, owned by the Crow Creek Sioux Tribe; Grand River Casino and Resort in Mobridge, operated by the Standing Rock Sioux Tribe; and Dakota Connection in Sisseton and Dakota Sioux Casino in Watertown, operated by the Sisseton-Wahpeton Oyate through Dakota Nation Gaming Enterprises.

Each operates under a tribal-state compact negotiated with South Dakota under the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act, several of which have been renewed or amended in recent years. The commercial side of the market is concentrated in Deadwood, the historic Black Hills town where roughly 20 licensed casinos operate under state regulation. That geography matters: Deadwood draws destination and regional traffic, while most tribal properties serve local and highway markets far from Sioux Falls and Rapid City. Our explainer on why location decides tribal gaming revenue explains how that dynamic shapes rural tribal operators.

Retail sports betting and the SJR 504 vote

South Dakota voters approved a constitutional amendment in November 2020 permitting sports wagering in Deadwood and at on-reservation tribal casinos, and wagering began in 2021. The law requires bets to be placed in person on licensed premises; mobile apps are permitted only within Deadwood casino premises. Tribal sportsbooks operate under IGRA and compact authority, with Dakota Connection and Dakota Sioux Casino among the tribal venues listed as offering sports wagering.

Efforts to go further have repeatedly stalled. In 2022, HB 1148 and SJR 502 failed at the end of session. In 2026, Sen. Casey Crabtree introduced SJR 504, a resolution that would have asked voters to permit sports betting on mobile and electronic platforms. It cleared the Senate Taxation Committee 4-3 and the full Senate 23-10 on Feb. 11. On Feb. 25, a do-pass motion in the House State Affairs Committee failed 6-7 and the resolution was deferred, ending its chances for the year.

Enforcement has already tested the edges of the in-person rule. The South Dakota Commission on Gaming filed a complaint in February against BetMGM for accepting mobile wagers outside Deadwood city limits; the operator paid a $55,000 penalty in June.

Why Amendment L's defeat matters for tribes

Because South Dakota authorizes sports betting in its constitution, any expansion to statewide mobile wagering requires another amendment approved by voters. On the November 2026 ballot, Amendment L would have raised the threshold for approving constitutional amendments to 60 percent. Voters rejected it, meaning a future mobile-betting amendment would still need only a simple majority.

For tribal nations, the critical issue in any 2027 proposal will be the structure of the market rather than simply whether mobile wagering is allowed. A model limited to Deadwood licensees would extend the commercial operators' reach into every reservation community without a corresponding tribal channel. Alternatives used elsewhere include tribal-exclusive mobile markets, as in Florida and Washington, or hub-and-spoke arrangements in which statewide wagers are deemed placed on servers located on tribal lands. Our hub-and-spoke explainer outlines how that legal theory works, and neighboring North Dakota offers a parallel debate, covered in our analysis of North Dakota's constitutional path.

Any statewide model touching tribal gaming would also require compact amendments approved or deemed approved by the Interior Department, which adds time but also gives tribes leverage at the negotiating table. Border competition is a further factor: tribal operators in the southern part of the state already compete with Iowa, where statewide mobile wagering is legal, a dynamic examined in our report on border leakage in tribal sports betting.

The outlook for 2027

The narrow House committee loss suggests that supporters will try again, and the defeat of Amendment L lowers the bar at the ballot box. Whether tribes support or oppose the next resolution is likely to turn on drafting details: who may hold mobile licenses, how wagers placed on reservations are treated, and whether compacts are reopened on terms tribes can accept.
