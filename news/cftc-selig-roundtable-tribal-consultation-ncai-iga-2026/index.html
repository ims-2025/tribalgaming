Tribes to CFTC: A Roundtable Is Not Consultation on Prediction Markets

After a September 14 meeting with Chairman Selig, NCAI and IGA pressed for formal government-to-government consultation — and made clear why the distinction matters.

By Elena RuizPublished September 18, 20264 min read

A meeting between federal derivatives regulators and tribal leaders in Washington has hardened rather than resolved the central procedural dispute in the prediction markets fight: whether the Commodity Futures Trading Commission has satisfied its obligation to consult with tribal nations before setting policy that reaches into gaming.

CFTC Chairman Michael S. Selig convened a roundtable on September 14 bringing together tribal representatives, industry participants, and agency staff to discuss sports-event contracts — the exchange-traded instruments that let users take positions on the outcome of games and that tribes have argued for more than a year are gaming by another name. The National Congress of American Indians and the Indian Gaming Association followed the session with a joint statement reiterating that sports-event contracts constitute gaming and should be subject to existing federal, state and tribal gaming law.

Their objection was as much about form as substance.

"While we acknowledge the effort that Chairman Selig took to convene Monday's discussion, to be candid: a roundtable is not consultation." — NCAI President Mark Macarro

Why CFTC tribal consultation is the fight within the fight

Federal agencies operate under a longstanding policy of government-to-government consultation with tribal nations on actions that affect tribal interests. The practical distinction the organizations are drawing is well understood in Indian Country: a roundtable is a discussion the agency convenes and controls, with an invited list and no procedural record. Consultation is a formal process with notice, tribal-leader participation, a documented record, and an obligation for the agency to respond to what it hears.

The difference matters because it shapes the administrative record a court would later review. If the CFTC issues a rule on sports-event contracts without a documented consultation record, tribes gain an argument that the agency failed to consider an important aspect of the problem — the standard basis for setting aside agency action as arbitrary and capricious. If the record shows robust consultation and reasoned response, that argument narrows considerably. Both sides know it.

NCAI and IGA framed the stakes broadly, warning that the commission's approach could affect IGRA itself, tribal-state gaming compacts, tribal regulatory authority, and the revenue streams that fund tribal government programs. Oklahoma Indian Gaming Association Chairman Matthew Morgan was blunter, characterizing the commission's posture as permitting illegal Class III gaming to infringe on tribal sovereignty and erode revenue that supports tribal services.

A week of mixed signals

The roundtable landed in the middle of an unusually consequential stretch. On September 15, the Senate failed to advance the Digital Asset Market Clarity Act, the crypto market structure bill that tribal organizations had spent months trying to amend after concluding that its treatment of event contracts could preempt tribal and state gaming authority. The stall was not framed as a tribal victory by its sponsors, but it removed, for now, the most immediate legislative vehicle that tribes viewed as a threat.

In the courts, the picture has tilted toward tribal and state regulators. A federal appeals court cleared Nevada to enforce its gambling laws against KalshiEX, a ruling that undercut the platform's core preemption theory — that Commodity Exchange Act registration displaces state and tribal gaming law entirely. Separate litigation brought by New Mexico tribes and by California tribes remains active, and the reasoning in the Nevada decision gives those plaintiffs material to work with.

Against that backdrop, the consultation dispute is not a side issue. Tribes have won enough in court to make the agency's rulemaking the decisive venue. A CFTC rule that codifies broad federal jurisdiction over sports-event contracts could unsettle favorable judicial outcomes; a rule that carves out gaming, or that declines to preempt state and tribal authority, would effectively ratify them.

What tribal regulators are watching next

Three things will indicate whether the commission is moving toward the process tribes are demanding. The first is whether the agency issues a formal consultation notice with dates, a defined scope, and a stated comment deadline, rather than additional convenings. The second is whether any proposed rule text addresses IGRA and tribal-state compacts directly instead of treating gaming as a state-law question alone — a framing tribes have objected to repeatedly, since compacted Class III gaming is a matter of federal law. The third is timing: an agency that wants a durable rule generally builds its record before proposing, not after.

For tribal gaming regulators, the operational stakes are concrete. Sports-event contracts are available on mobile devices inside the geographic footprints where tribes hold compacted exclusivity, in states where tribes paid for that exclusivity through revenue sharing. Where exclusivity is breached, some compacts allow tribes to reduce or suspend payments to the state — which makes the question of who regulates prediction markets a state fiscal question as well as a tribal one.

Neither the CFTC nor the tribal organizations announced a next meeting. NCAI and IGA said they would continue pressing for formal consultation as the commission develops its approach.
