Flanagan, Peltola and a New Senate Majority: The Tribal Gaming Agenda

Two Native senators-elect and a change in control reset the committee map for prediction markets, NIGC nominations and land-into-trust legislation.

By Sarah BegayPublished November 23, 20264 min read

The 2026 elections will send two Native women to the U.S. Senate and hand Democrats control of the chamber, a combination that reshapes the tribal gaming agenda for the 120th Congress. Peggy Flanagan, an enrolled member of the White Earth Nation and Minnesota's lieutenant governor, won Minnesota's open Senate seat on November 3, defeating Republican Michele Tafoya. In Alaska, former Rep. Mary Peltola, who is Yup'ik, unseated Republican Sen. Dan Sullivan; the Associated Press called the race in mid-November, with preliminary results showing Peltola at about 50.1 percent of first-choice votes, enough to avoid a ranked-choice tabulation. State officials expected to certify the Alaska result on November 25.

Flanagan is the first Native American woman elected to the Senate. According to the Minnesota Reformer, only four enrolled tribal members had previously served in the chamber, all men. Peltola, who in 2022 became the first Alaska Native woman to serve in Congress, joins her as one of the first Indigenous women in the Senate. Alaska Beacon reported that Peltola will be the 56th Democrat when the new Senate convenes in January.

Why control of the chamber matters for tribal gaming

Tribal gaming policy runs through a small number of congressional channels: the Senate Committee on Indian Affairs, the House Natural Resources Committee and its Indian affairs subcommittee, the agriculture committees that oversee the Commodity Futures Trading Commission, and the appropriations bills that fund the National Indian Gaming Commission and Interior's Office of Indian Gaming. Our explainer on how congressional IGRA oversight works describes those roles.

A change in Senate control moves committee gavels and the power to set hearing agendas. Sen. Lisa Murkowski of Alaska chairs Indian Affairs in the current Congress, and the committee has historically operated on a bipartisan basis, but the majority sets the calendar and controls which bills receive markups. In January, those prerogatives shift to Democrats, and committee assignments for the new members have not yet been announced.

Prediction markets: the issue that will test the new Congress

The most pressing gaming question before Congress is the status of sports event contracts offered by prediction-market platforms. Tribal governments and their national associations have argued that these products amount to sports wagering offered outside IGRA and state law, and that they undercut the exclusivity tribes receive under compacts. Bills addressing the issue, including the House measure known as the Prediction Markets Are Gambling Act, were introduced in the current Congress. As we reported in our analysis of tribal bills facing expiration in January 2027, any measure that does not pass before the 119th Congress ends must be reintroduced.

A new majority does not by itself change the CFTC's jurisdiction. It changes which committee chairs decide whether that jurisdiction gets a hearing.

Jurisdiction over event contracts sits largely with the agriculture committees, not Indian Affairs, which is one reason tribal advocates have pursued a multi-committee strategy. Members from states with large tribal gaming economies, including Minnesota, have historically been among the most engaged on these questions. Flanagan's arrival adds a senator whose state's tribes have been active on both prediction markets and sports betting policy; the Minnesota Reformer noted that the Walz-Flanagan administration protected tribal gaming interests during her tenure as lieutenant governor.

Nominations, appropriations and land into trust

Beyond prediction markets, three other items belong on the watch list. First is the National Indian Gaming Commission. The NIGC chair is a presidential appointment subject to Senate confirmation, and the commission has operated with a vacancy in the chair for much of 2026. The Senate's role in confirming a nominee, and the pace at which a new majority schedules confirmation hearings, will affect the agency's ability to act on enforcement and approvals that IGRA reserves to the chair.

Second is appropriations. The NIGC is funded primarily through fees paid by tribal gaming operations, but Interior's Office of Indian Gaming, which reviews compacts and amendments, depends on annual appropriations. Funding levels shape how quickly compact amendments, including the steady stream of Washington and California amendments seen this year, move through federal review.

Third is land-into-trust legislation. Tribes have sought a legislative response to the Supreme Court's 2009 decision in Carcieri v. Salazar, which limited the Interior Secretary's authority to take land into trust for tribes not under federal jurisdiction in 1934. Proposals to address Carcieri have been introduced repeatedly without becoming law. Because trust status is often a precondition for gaming eligibility, the issue matters to tribes pursuing new facilities; our explainer on land into trust and IGRA Section 20 lays out the connection.

Measured expectations

It would be a mistake to treat the election results as a guarantee of legislative outcomes. Gaming bills often divide tribes themselves, as recent disagreements over tribal partnerships with prediction-market operators have shown, and Senate procedure still requires broad support for most legislation. House control and the administration's positions also matter.

What the results do change is representation and agenda-setting. Two senators-elect with deep ties to tribal governments, and a majority that will organize the chamber in January, give tribal gaming advocates new points of access in a Congress where the most consequential questions, from event contracts to NIGC leadership, remain unresolved.
