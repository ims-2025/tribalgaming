Quebec's Indigenous Gaming Gap: Online Authority, No Land-Based Path

Every other province built something. Quebec's eleven Indigenous nations still have no provincial gaming framework at all.

By Linda CardinalPublished September 11, 20264 min read

Quebec occupies an anomalous position in Canadian Indigenous gaming. The province is home to the oldest and most internationally recognised Indigenous gaming regulator in the country — the Kahnawake Gaming Commission, licensing online operators since 1999 — and yet it has no First Nations land-based casino framework of the kind that operates in Saskatchewan, Manitoba, Ontario, Alberta and British Columbia. The gap between digital authority and land-based access is the defining feature of Indigenous gaming in Quebec, and it has persisted for a quarter-century.

Two regimes in one province

Canada's Criminal Code gives provincial governments the authority to conduct and manage lottery schemes, and in Quebec that authority is exercised by Loto-Québec, which operates the province's casinos and launched its Espacejeux online platform in 2010. Every First Nations gaming framework elsewhere in the country has been built by negotiating within that provincial authority: revenue-sharing agreements, operating agreements, or delegated conduct-and-manage arrangements reached between a province and First Nations organisations.

Kahnawake took a different route. The Mohawk Council of Kahnawake enacted its own gaming law and established the Kahnawake Gaming Commission in 1996, adopting Interactive Gaming Regulations in 1999 at a point when almost no government anywhere had written rules for internet gambling. The jurisdictional basis asserted is not a provincial delegation but inherent Indigenous jurisdiction recognised under section 35 of the Constitution Act, 1982. That claim has never been fully resolved in Canadian courts, and the practical result has been a long-running standoff rather than a settlement: the Commission licenses operators, the province does not recognise the authority, and neither side has forced the question to judgment.

On the ground, the land-based footprint is correspondingly thin. Kahnawake hosts licensed poker rooms operating under Commission authority, but there is no First Nations casino in Quebec comparable to SIGA's properties in Saskatchewan or the First Nations-owned casinos in Manitoba and Alberta. Our comparison of Canadian First Nations gaming frameworks sets out how those provincial models differ.

What Quebec's First Nations are not receiving

The absence of a framework has a measurable cost, and the cleanest way to see it is by comparison.

In Ontario, the Ontario First Nations Limited Partnership receives 1.7 percent of provincial gross gaming revenue under a 2008 agreement, distributing to 132 First Nations; the payment reached roughly $186 million in fiscal 2024-25. In Saskatchewan, the Gaming Framework Agreement between the province and the Federation of Sovereign Indigenous Nations channelled about $153.5 million to First Nations and Métis organisations for 2025-26, on top of SIGA's own operating profits, and the framework was recently extended to preserve SIGA's online exclusivity through 2029. Manitoba, Alberta and British Columbia each have their own structures, with British Columbia's recent First Nations casino acquisitions representing a shift from revenue sharing toward direct ownership.

Quebec's eleven Indigenous nations participate in none of these mechanisms. There is no provincial revenue-sharing formula tied to Loto-Québec's gaming revenue, no negotiated share of Espacejeux, and no delegated conduct-and-manage authority for a First Nations-operated casino. Whatever gaming revenue reaches Quebec First Nations comes almost entirely from Kahnawake's own licensing and hosting operations, which benefit one community rather than the province's Indigenous population broadly.

Why the impasse has held

Three factors have kept the situation static.

The first is that neither party gains much from litigating. Kahnawake's licensing operation is established and generates real employment and revenue; forcing a constitutional ruling on section 35 gaming jurisdiction risks losing a position currently held in practice. Quebec, for its part, has limited appetite for a Supreme Court case that could produce a broad ruling on Indigenous jurisdiction reaching well beyond gaming.

The second is that Quebec's relationship with its Indigenous nations runs through a different set of files. Land claims, forestry, policing and language policy have consistently occupied more political oxygen than gaming, and no Quebec government has treated an Indigenous gaming framework as a priority requiring cabinet-level attention.

The third is the demand geography. Quebec's population is concentrated around Montreal and Quebec City, and Loto-Québec's casinos are positioned to serve those markets. Many Quebec First Nations communities are remote, which weakens the commercial case for a destination casino and makes a revenue-sharing formula — rather than an operating licence — the realistic ask. Revenue sharing, however, requires a provincial partner willing to open the file.

Kahnawake proved that an Indigenous government could regulate online gaming competently before most national regulators existed. It has not been able to convert that into a land-based framework for Quebec's other ten nations.

Federal legislation as a possible unlock

The most plausible route out of the impasse may run through Ottawa rather than Quebec City. Proposals before Parliament to amend the Criminal Code and the Indian Act to recognise and codify First Nations jurisdiction over gaming on their lands would, if enacted, change the legal baseline that has made provincial consent the only practical path. A federal carve-out would give Quebec First Nations something they currently lack entirely: a source of authority that does not depend on Loto-Québec agreeing to share.

The prospects remain uncertain, and successive versions of that legislation have faced significant procedural and political obstacles. But the case for it is strongest precisely in a province like Quebec, where the provincial-delegation model has produced nothing for a quarter-century while every other province has built something. Our coverage of the Kahnawake Gaming Commission's digital jurisdiction and the comparison of US and Canadian legal models covers how differently the two countries resolved the same underlying question.

For now, Quebec remains the clearest illustration of a structural feature of Canadian Indigenous gaming: absent a federal statute, a First Nation's access to gaming revenue depends less on its own capacity than on whether its province decided, sometime in the last thirty years, to negotiate. Saskatchewan did in 1995. Ontario did in 2008. Quebec has not. Comparable provincial outcomes are tracked in our reporting on the SIGA framework in Saskatchewan.
