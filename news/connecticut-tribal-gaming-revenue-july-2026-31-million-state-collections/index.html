Connecticut Tribal Gaming Revenue: State Collects $31 Million for July 2026

Monthly figures show sequential gains for both tribes, but annual comparisons diverge between Foxwoods and Mohegan Sun.

By James KingfisherPublished October 25, 20264 min read

Connecticut collected more than $31 million from tribal gaming operations in July 2026, according to monthly figures released by the state Department of Consumer Protection and reported by The Day of New London. The Connecticut tribal gaming revenue report covers both federally recognized tribes that operate in the state, the Mashantucket Pequot Tribal Nation and the Mohegan Tribe, and it offers one of the more transparent monthly looks at how a mature tribal market is performing across slots, online sports betting and online casino play.

Because the data come from the tribes' own monthly reporting to the state, they serve a dual purpose. They determine what the state collects under the compact framework, and they give analysts a recurring read on consumer demand in the Northeast, where competition from commercial casinos and online products is intense.

What the July figures show

For the Mashantucket Pequot, which operates Foxwoods Resort Casino, slot revenue came to $32.9 million in July, and the state collected nearly $8.3 million in contributions from the tribe. That works out to roughly a quarter of slot revenue, consistent with the long-standing slot contribution rate in Connecticut's compacts. (We explain the mechanics of that arrangement in our look at the 25 percent slot revenue share and the municipal aid debate.)

Month over month, Foxwoods saw revenue rise in slots, online sports betting and online casino. Year over year, however, revenue fell in every category except online casino. The Mohegan Tribe, which operates Mohegan Sun, saw the same month-over-month gains in all three categories and, unlike Foxwoods, posted year-over-year growth in slots, online sports betting and online casino. Dollar figures for Mohegan were not included in the summary we reviewed, so we do not cite them here.

Reading the month-over-month and year-over-year split

The contrast between the two measures is worth understanding before drawing conclusions. Month-over-month increases in a summer month are common in destination markets, as leisure travel and event traffic rise. Year-over-year comparisons strip out that seasonal pattern by comparing July with the prior July, and there the picture for Foxwoods was softer, while online casino remained a bright spot for both tribes.

A single month is a thin basis for trend claims. Revenue can shift with the calendar, promotional timing, weather, entertainment bookings and one-time events. Analysts typically wait for a run of months, or quarterly totals, before describing a direction. Our broader analysis of the two-speed tribal market considers how land-based floors and digital products have been diverging nationally, and the Connecticut numbers fit within that framework without proving it.

Why Connecticut's reporting matters beyond the state

Connecticut's structure is distinctive. The tribes hold exclusive rights to online casino and sports betting in the state, a model we assessed in our five-year review of the exclusive igaming model. Because the tribes report monthly and the state publishes the results, observers in other jurisdictions can compare a tribal-exclusive digital market against markets with open licensing. Those comparisons inform legislative debates elsewhere, including tribal exclusivity discussions in states weighing mobile wagering.

The state's share also carries local consequences. Contributions from tribal gaming flow into the state's budget and into municipal aid, which is why even modest swings draw attention at the Capitol and in host communities. Readers who want a fuller picture of the market, its operators and its history can start with our Connecticut tribal gaming market deep dive and the Connecticut state hub.

How the contribution is calculated

Under Connecticut's tribal-state compacts, the tribes make contributions to the state based on a percentage of gross operating revenue from slot machines. The July figures illustrate the relationship: $8.3 million on $32.9 million of slot revenue. Online products are handled separately under the state's iGaming framework, which is why the monthly report breaks out online sportsbook and online casino results alongside slots. Readers new to the underlying structure may find our explainer on tribal-state compact revenue sharing helpful, as it describes how different states set rates and what the money is used for.

It is also worth separating the state's receipts from the tribes' own results. The contribution is calculated on gaming revenue before the tribes pay operating expenses, debt service, regulatory costs and distributions to members or community programs. A tribe's profitability can therefore move differently from the amount the state collects, and the monthly report does not capture that difference.

What to watch in the coming months

Several questions will shape how the next reports read. First, whether the year-over-year softness at Foxwoods persists or proves temporary. Second, whether online casino continues to outpace land-based slots in growth. Third, how new regional competition, including expansion in neighboring states, affects both properties. Our reporting on Northeast casino saturation and exclusivity erosion examines the competitive backdrop.

The state will publish August figures in the weeks ahead, and the pattern across a full quarter will say more than any one month. For now, the July report shows a market that is large, stable and still evolving: more than $31 million to the state in one month, rising sequentially across product lines, with mixed annual comparisons that underline how carefully tribal gaming data should be read.
