California Advances a New Wave of Tribal-State Gaming Compacts

Ratification bills for the Tule River Tribe, the Twenty-Nine Palms Band and others move through Sacramento as 1999-era compacts give way to modern terms.

By Elena RuizPublished August 13, 20265 min read

California's Legislature is working through another batch of tribal-state gaming compact ratifications this session, continuing a steady modernization of agreements that in many cases date to the 1999 template first negotiated under Governor Gray Davis. Measures moving in the 2025-26 session include ratification vehicles tied to the Tule River Tribe and the Twenty-Nine Palms Band of Mission Indians, alongside companion bills covering other tribes that have signed fresh compacts or amendments with the Newsom administration.

The mechanics are unglamorous but consequential. Under the state constitution and the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act, a compact negotiated between a tribe and the governor has no legal force until the Legislature ratifies it and the U.S. Department of the Interior signs off. California has streamlined part of that process: bills such as AB 2539 provide that a compact executed after September 10, 1999 is ratified if it is identical in all material respects to a model compact already approved, unless either house rejects it within 30 days. That framework lets Sacramento clear a queue of substantially similar agreements without renegotiating each one on the floor.

From revenue sharing to fee-based terms

The bigger story is what the new compacts contain. Where the 1999-era deals leaned on revenue sharing into the state's Special Distribution Fund and Revenue Sharing Trust Fund, many of the compacts California has ratified in recent years shift toward fixed regulatory fees, longer terms, and expanded machine authorizations rather than a percentage cut of net win to the general fund. For a tribe like Tule River, whose Eagle Mountain Casino completed a relocation and a major expansion, a modern compact locks in the machine count and facility flexibility needed to finance and operate a larger resort.

For the state, the trade-off is fiscal. Newer compacts tend to route tribal payments toward the direct cost of state regulation and toward local mitigation agreements with host counties, rather than into a general revenue-sharing pool that non-gaming tribes historically drew from. That reflects a legal reality reinforced by federal courts: the Interior Department has grown skeptical of compact provisions that look like a tax on gaming or that demand concessions unrelated to the operation of a casino.

What tribes get in return is durability and headroom. A modern compact typically extends the term well into the following decade, raises or removes the cap on gaming devices, and reaffirms the exclusivity that keeps commercial card rooms and would-be competitors off the Class III turf reserved for tribes. For a tribe weighing hundreds of millions of dollars in resort investment, that combination — a long runway, room to add machines, and a protected market — is often worth more than the marginal difference in what it pays the state. Lenders and bond investors read compact terms closely, and a fresh, long-dated compact can lower the cost of the capital a tribe needs to build.

The ratification queue also reflects the sheer breadth of California's gaming landscape. Beyond the marquee agreements, the Legislature routinely processes compacts and amendments for smaller and mid-sized tribes whose facilities anchor rural economies. Each carries its own negotiated local mitigation package — payments to counties for roads, public safety, and emergency services in the communities that host a casino. Those host-community agreements have become a standard feature of the modern compact, reflecting years of friction over the off-site costs a large resort can impose and the political necessity of keeping local governments on board.

What ratification does and does not settle

Legislative ratification is not the finish line. After the governor signs a ratification bill, the compact still goes to the Secretary of the Interior, who has 45 days to approve, disapprove, or allow it to take effect by operation of law. Only when notice is published in the Federal Register does the compact become effective. Tribes generally cannot expand Class III operations under a new compact's terms until that federal step is complete.

California remains the largest tribal gaming market in the country, and the pace at which Sacramento clears compact ratifications is one of the quieter variables shaping how quickly the state's casino pipeline can grow.

The volume of activity underscores how central California is to the national picture. With dozens of gaming tribes and a market that anchors the U.S. total, even routine ratification work moves real money and real construction timelines. Readers tracking the underlying rules can consult our Legal Guide to IGRA and Class III gaming for how compacts, machine caps, and exclusivity fit together, and the California state hub for tribe-by-tribe detail.

For now, the practical takeaway is that California's compact framework continues to grind forward on two tracks at once: a legislative track clearing agreements that mirror approved models, and a negotiating track producing the next generation of terms. Both will shape whether tribes like Tule River and the Twenty-Nine Palms Band can translate paper authorizations into floor space, hotel rooms, and jobs over the next several years.
