Who Gets the Money? Host-Community Revenue Shares in Nebraska and Taunton

South Sioux City and Taunton are both counting on casino-related revenue, but the legal mechanisms behind each payment differ in ways that matter for budgets.

By James KingfisherPublished November 02, 20265 min read

When a new casino opens, the host city and county usually expect a share of the money, but the way that share is created varies sharply. Two projects in the news this year illustrate the contrast in host-community casino revenue sharing: a tribally owned racetrack casino in South Sioux City, Nebraska, where state law sends a fixed portion of gaming taxes to local governments, and the Mashpee Wampanoag Tribe's First Light casino in Taunton, Massachusetts, where the city hopes to receive funding through a revenue share agreement.

Understanding the difference helps local officials, tribal governments and investors judge how reliable a projected payment really is.

Nebraska: a statutory formula

WarHorse Gaming, a unit of Ho-Chunk Inc., the economic development corporation of the Winnebago Tribe of Nebraska, broke ground on August 27 on a roughly $100 million casino and hotel in South Sioux City. The project is expected to cover about 63,000 square feet with 600 slot machines and 16 table games, a 100-room hotel, an outdoor amphitheater for about 5,700 people and conference space. Officials cited about 230 permanent jobs, and gaming could begin in late 2027. Our WarHorse South Sioux City groundbreaking report has the project details.

The local revenue mechanism is set by statute. Under Nebraska law, 25 percent of state gaming tax revenue from racetrack casinos goes to local governments, divided between the county and the city. The estimates of what that means in dollars differ. Mayor Rod Koch has estimated roughly $2 million to $3 million a year for the city, while Ho-Chunk Inc. chief executive Lance Morgan has estimated about $2 million to $2.5 million annually for the city and county combined. Both are projections, and the actual figure will depend on gaming volume after opening.

The strength of this model is predictability of rules. Because the percentage is fixed in law, the local government does not need to negotiate with the operator for it. The weakness is that the payment depends on the state tax base, which rises and falls with play, and on the legislature's willingness to leave the formula alone.

Taunton: a negotiated agreement

The Taunton arrangement rests on a contract. The Mashpee Wampanoag Tribe operates First Light in Taunton, and local coverage indicates the city hopes the casino will generate funding through a revenue share agreement. Our report on the First Light permanent resort follows the tribe's development there.

Negotiated agreements offer flexibility. They can be tailored to local needs, such as public safety costs, road work or utilities, and can include fixed payments, percentages or in-kind services. They also carry risk: payment depends on the terms, on the counterparty's performance and, in the tribal context, on whether sovereign immunity has been waived for enforcement. The legal mechanics are covered in our explainers on intergovernmental agreements and municipal services agreements.

The Taunton figure is also exposed to competition. As we discuss in our analysis of the Massachusetts historic horse racing bill, critics have warned that new slot-like machines elsewhere in the state could hurt the tribal casino, and any decline in casino revenue would flow through to a percentage-based share.

Neither case has fully played out. South Sioux City's casino is not expected to open until late 2027, so the first local payments are still more than a year away, and the Taunton agreement's realized value will depend on the casino's results. Observers should treat both as early-stage projections rather than reported receipts.

Why the distinction matters

For a city budget officer, the difference between a statutory share and a contractual share is the difference between a line item and a promise. A statutory share is easier to forecast but outside the city's control. A contractual share can be bargained but must be enforced. In both cases the payment is derived from gross or taxable gaming activity rather than guaranteed in dollars, so conservative budgeting treats early estimates as ranges.

There is also a question of what the money is for. Host communities often face real costs when a casino opens: traffic, emergency services, infrastructure and housing pressure. Tribal governments, for their part, generally want agreements that are predictable and that recognize the economic benefits a casino brings through jobs and procurement. Well-designed agreements link payments to identifiable impacts, which makes them easier to defend publicly.

Transparency is another variable. Statutory shares are visible in state budgets and tax reports, which lets residents see what their city receives. Contractual shares with a tribal government may be disclosed more selectively, depending on the agreement and on tribal law. Local officials who publish annual summaries of casino-related receipts and costs tend to build more public trust in the arrangement, whichever mechanism sits behind it.

The two cases suggest a few practical questions for any new project: Is the local share set by statute or by contract? What revenue base does it use? Who enforces it, and where? And how sensitive is it to changes in competition or regulation? Readers looking for a broader regional picture can start with the tribal gaming directory.
