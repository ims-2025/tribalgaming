Cheyenne & Arapaho Break Ground on Lucky Star Elk City Resort

The tribes' eighth Lucky Star location trades a convenience-store gaming hall for a hotel, steakhouse and 750-machine floor.

By Marcus WhitehorsePublished August 05, 20265 min read

The Cheyenne & Arapaho Tribes broke ground in late July on a new Lucky Star Casino resort in Elk City, Oklahoma, launching what tribal leaders described as their most aggressive development effort to date and extending the Lucky Star brand to an eighth location. The project replaces a small convenience-style gaming hall with a full-scale entertainment destination positioned on the Interstate 40 corridor in western Oklahoma, a busy east-west route linking Oklahoma City to the Texas Panhandle.

Phase one of the multi-phase build calls for a 56,000-square-foot casino floor housing roughly 750 gaming machines, a 100-room hotel totaling about 65,000 square feet, a food court, a steakhouse, an indoor swimming pool and a fitness center. The design signals the tribes' intent to convert what has been a highway stopover into an overnight destination capable of holding visitors — and their spending — for longer than a single gaming session.

A regional operator scales up

The Cheyenne & Arapaho Tribes have long operated Lucky Star as a network of modest casinos across western Oklahoma, a model built on frequency and convenience rather than the destination-resort scale of the state's largest operators. The Elk City project marks a deliberate step up that ladder. By pairing a hotel with a steakhouse and amenities, the tribes are borrowing the template that turned larger Oklahoma operators into regional powers — a template documented in our profiles of Choctaw Nation gaming and Cherokee Nation Entertainment.

Oklahoma remains one of the most competitive tribal gaming markets in the country, with dozens of tribes operating well over a hundred facilities under the state's model gaming compact. That density makes location and amenity mix decisive. Elk City sits far enough west of the Oklahoma City metro's crowded casino cluster to command its own catchment along I-40, drawing from a rural service area and highway traffic rather than fighting for metro share. The full competitive landscape is mapped in the Oklahoma state hub.

Why the I-40 corridor matters

Interstate corridors have quietly become one of the more reliable engines of tribal casino growth. A property visible from a major highway captures pass-through travelers who would never seek out a reservation-interior facility, and a hotel converts that traffic into multi-day stays. Elk City's position makes it a natural waypoint for travelers moving between Oklahoma City and Amarillo, and the addition of lodging lets the tribes capture demand that a machines-only hall simply could not.

The upgrade reflects a broader pattern in Indian Country: regional operators are trading high-frequency convenience gaming for fewer, larger destinations that capture more revenue per visit.

That shift carries real economic weight for the Cheyenne & Arapaho. Gaming revenue funds tribal government services, and a larger, higher-margin flagship expands the base that supports those programs. The construction phase itself injects jobs and contracts into a rural western Oklahoma economy that has few employers of comparable scale, and the finished resort will add permanent hospitality positions well beyond the gaming floor.

The amenity mix is also a deliberate hedge against the ceiling of a machines-only operation. A steakhouse, food court, pool and fitness center give non-gaming visitors a reason to stop, and every dollar spent on food, lodging or entertainment is revenue that never touches the gaming floor's hold — the same non-gaming diversification that has insulated larger operators when gambling demand softens. For a tribe whose network has historically leaned on frequency, adding those revenue lines changes the economics of the flagship substantially.

Oklahoma's compact framework gives the tribes a stable footing for that investment. The state's model gaming compact governs Class III machine and table play under terms shared broadly across the state's tribes, and its exclusivity fee structure has underpinned more than a decade of expansion. Building against that predictable regulatory backdrop lets an operator finance a multi-year resort with reasonable confidence about the rules it will be playing under when the doors open.

Execution risk and the road ahead

Multi-phase resort projects are not without risk. Construction timelines, financing costs and the challenge of stabilizing a new hotel's occupancy all shape whether an ambitious build delivers the returns its planners project. The tribes will need to fill 100 rooms consistently in a rural market, a different discipline than running a machines-only hall where the only real variable is floor traffic.

Still, the groundbreaking fits a confident national moment for tribal gaming, which just posted record revenue nationally. For a regional operator like the Cheyenne & Arapaho, the calculus is straightforward: the convenience-casino format has a ceiling, and clearing it requires the beds, the food and the amenities that turn a stop into a stay. Elk City is the tribes' bet that western Oklahoma can support that leap.
