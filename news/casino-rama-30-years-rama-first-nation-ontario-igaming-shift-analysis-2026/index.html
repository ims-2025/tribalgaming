Casino Rama at 30: A Building in an Increasingly Online Market

Ontario's only First Nations commercial casino is spending on rooms and floor space at the exact moment provincial play keeps migrating to phones.

By Linda CardinalPublished August 19, 20264 min read

Casino Rama Resort marked its thirtieth anniversary in July 2026. The number that matters most at the property is not the size of the gaming floor but the headcount: roughly 1,100 employees, of whom 146 have worked there since the doors opened in 1996. In a Simcoe County economy where year-round full-time work outside tourism and construction is scarce, three decades of stable payroll is the property's most durable output.

The anniversary arrives in the middle of a refurbishment. Gateway Casinos, which operates the property under contract, announced the project in partnership with the Ontario Lottery and Gaming Corporation and Rama First Nation. The work covers hotel rooms, an expanded spa and fitness centre, the gaming floor and common areas, and comes with additional hiring.

An ownership structure that is regularly misread

Casino Rama is jointly owned by the Chippewas of Rama First Nation and OLG, and remains the only First Nations commercial casino in Ontario and the largest such property in Canada. Rama First Nation holds an ownership position in the property itself rather than merely hosting it on its land — a distinction that separates Rama from most host-community arrangements in Canadian gaming. OLG is a provincial Crown agency, so a share of what the floor produces returns to the province. Gateway runs day-to-day operations under contract.

Three parties with three different reasons to care whether the building performs is an unusual governance arrangement, and it has proved durable. The property also operates a 5,000-seat Entertainment Centre, one of very few rooms of that scale between Toronto and Sudbury, which gives the site a winter booking calendar in a region that otherwise loses most of its visitor spending between Thanksgiving and Victoria Day.

The market has been rebuilt around the building

Thirty years is long enough for the underlying business to change shape. Ontario opened a regulated online gambling market in April 2022 and it has grown rapidly. In the 2024-25 fiscal year, players in the province wagered more than $82.7 billion and generated $2.9 billion in gaming revenue — increases of 32 and 31 per cent respectively over the prior year — across 49 operators running 84 gaming sites. Online casino games alone accounted for $2.40 billion of that revenue, up 36 per cent year over year.

Ontario remains the exception rather than the Canadian standard. Most provinces have no equivalent framework, and online play elsewhere in the country runs through operators licensed offshore, commonly in Malta or by the Kahnawake Gaming Commission in Quebec. Alberta's regulated market opened in July 2026, a development we covered in our analysis of the Alberta iGaming launch and First Nations participation.

Channelization is the number the province watches

Ontario tracks how much provincial online play stays inside the regulated market, a figure it calls the channelization rate. iGaming Ontario reported that rate at 83.7 per cent, against a target of 85 per cent for 2025-26. In plain terms, roughly one dollar in six of Ontario's online play still occurs outside the system the province built. Closing that gap is the central argument for the regulated market's existence, and it has proved slower work than the headline growth figures suggest.

None of the online growth appears as employment in Simcoe County. An offshore operator has no hotel to staff, no kitchen, no maintenance contract, no local suppliers and no revenue-sharing arrangement with a First Nation.

What a refurbishment in 2026 actually signals

Spending on hotel rooms and gaming floor in the current environment is a statement that the operators expect people to keep arriving in person, at a moment when provincial data points the other way. That is the tension worth naming plainly. Digital revenue growth in Ontario has been substantial and sustained; land-based capital spending assumes the physical property retains a distinct function that an application cannot replicate.

It is worth being precise about what the growth figures do and do not measure. Ontario's iGaming revenue is provincial in scope and includes players who have never set foot in a casino, so it is not a straightforward substitution for land-based spend. But the direction is unambiguous, and the segment growing fastest — online casino games, up 36 per cent — is the one that competes most directly with a slot floor. A refurbishment is a bet that the two channels serve partly different occasions rather than the same one.

The employment argument is the strongest version of that case. The 1,100 jobs at Rama, the payroll landing in Ramara, Orillia and the surrounding townships, and the revenue flowing to Rama First Nation exist because the business still occupies a building people drive to. The Entertainment Centre operates on the same principle: a show night puts several thousand people on regional roads in the off season.

Whether that holds for another thirty years is genuinely open, and the question is not unique to Rama. The same calculation is being run at First Nations properties across the country as provinces open digital markets one at a time — a dynamic examined in our analysis of iGaming cannibalization of land-based First Nations casinos and in our comparison of provincial revenue-sharing frameworks.

The legal age for casino gambling in Ontario is 19.
