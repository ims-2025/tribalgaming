Winnebago Tribe Breaks Ground on Third Nebraska Casino in South Sioux City

A $100 million state-licensed project shows how tribal enterprises are building gaming businesses outside IGRA entirely.

By Marcus WhitehorsePublished September 04, 20264 min read

WarHorse Gaming broke ground on August 27 on a roughly $100 million casino and hotel in South Sioux City, Nebraska, the third property in the state for an operator wholly owned by the Winnebago Tribe of Nebraska. The project is a useful case study in a model that sits outside the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act entirely: a tribal government building a commercial gaming business under state license, on land that is not held in trust, competing directly with non-tribal operators on their own regulatory terms.

WarHorse Gaming, LLC operates as a subsidiary of Ho-Chunk, Inc., the economic development corporation of the Winnebago Tribe. Ho-Chunk, Inc. has spent nearly three decades building a portfolio that spans construction, government contracting, marketing, retail and real estate, and its gaming arm has become the most visible piece of that portfolio since Nebraska voters approved casino gaming at licensed racetracks in 2020.

What is being built

The South Sioux City development, announced in late August, is planned to include a casino floor with roughly 600 slot machines and 16 table games, a sportsbook operating primarily through wagering kiosks, a 100-room hotel, an outdoor pool complex and live horse racing. Reported job estimates for the completed property have centered on approximately 90 permanent positions, with construction expected to run around eighteen months and an opening targeted for 2027 rather than the 2026 date floated in earlier planning documents.

The live racing component is not incidental. Nebraska's framework ties casino licensing to licensed racetracks, so a pari-mutuel operation is a condition of the gaming license rather than an amenity bolted on afterward. That constraint shapes the site plan, the capital budget and the operating calendar in ways that a Class III facility on Indian lands never has to consider.

Why this is not IGRA gaming

It is worth being precise about the legal architecture, because the distinction gets blurred routinely in general-interest coverage. A casino on Indian lands operated under a tribal-state compact is regulated by the tribe's own gaming commission and the National Indian Gaming Commission, with the state's role defined and limited by the compact. A Nebraska racetrack casino owned by a tribal enterprise is regulated by the Nebraska Racing and Gaming Commission on the same terms as any other licensee, pays the same state gaming tax, and enjoys none of IGRA's structural protections. Our explainer on tribal versus commercial casinos walks through the differences in detail.

The trade is straightforward: a tribe gives up sovereign regulatory control and tax treatment in exchange for access to a market it could not otherwise enter, on land it does not need to place into trust.

That trade has become more common. Tribal enterprises now hold or pursue commercial gaming licenses in Illinois, Virginia, Pennsylvania, Nebraska and elsewhere, and the strategic logic is consistent across cases — diversification away from a single home-market property, and revenue that does not depend on the state of a compact negotiation. We examined the broader pattern in our analysis of tribal enterprises operating commercial gaming beyond Indian lands.

The numbers behind the expansion

WarHorse is expanding from a position of measurable strength in its home state. Through the first seven months of 2026, WarHorse Casino Omaha reported gross gaming revenue of approximately $70.3 million, leading Nebraska's racetrack casinos, while WarHorse Casino Lincoln was second at roughly $55.7 million over the same period. Those figures reflect a market that barely existed five years ago and that has drawn a meaningful share of play back from Council Bluffs, Iowa, where Nebraska residents had crossed the river to gamble for decades.

The company also secured a $300 million refinancing earlier in 2026 to support its Nebraska growth plans, including a previously announced expansion at the Lincoln property. Refinancing at that scale is a signal in itself: tribal enterprise borrowers have historically paid a premium for capital, and a facility of that size suggests lenders are underwriting WarHorse on the strength of its operating record rather than on tribal-credit assumptions.

South Sioux City is also a deliberate geographic play. The site sits at the Nebraska-Iowa-South Dakota junction, immediately across the Missouri River from Sioux City, Iowa, a market already served by an established commercial casino. The Winnebago Reservation lies roughly twenty miles south, and the tribe's own Class II and Class III operation on trust land has served that corridor for years. Building a state-licensed property in the same regional catchment raises a cannibalization question the enterprise will need to manage carefully, a dynamic we have examined in the context of multi-property tribal portfolios.

What to watch

Three things will determine whether the South Sioux City bet pays. The first is construction cost discipline; an eighteen-month schedule in a tight regional labor market has proven optimistic for several tribal projects this year. The second is whether the sportsbook, limited to kiosks and a retail counter, generates enough incremental traffic to justify its floor space in a state without mobile wagering. The third is the hotel — 100 rooms is a modest count, but it converts the property from a day-trip stop into an overnight destination, and occupancy performance will say a great deal about whether the tri-state corridor supports that positioning.
