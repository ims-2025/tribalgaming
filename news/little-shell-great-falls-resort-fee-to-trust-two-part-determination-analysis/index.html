Little Shell's Great Falls Resort Tests Montana's Two-Part Path

A tribe restored in 2019, with no reservation and thin federal funding, is pursuing IGRA's hardest route to new gaming land.

By Sarah BegayPublished November 18, 20264 min read

The Little Shell Tribe of Chippewa Indians of Montana is about ten months into federal environmental review of its proposed Event Center and Resort in Great Falls, a project that would give one of the country's most recently restored tribes its first gaming enterprise. The Little Shell Tribe casino proposal is also one of the clearest current examples of how a tribe without a reservation must navigate the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act's most demanding route to new gaming land: a fee-to-trust acquisition paired with a two-part Secretarial determination and a governor's concurrence.

The project is early, and no federal decision is imminent. But its structure, its scale relative to Montana's gaming market and the approvals it needs make it worth understanding now, before the environmental assessment reaches public comment.

What the tribe has proposed

The tribe has applied to have roughly 136 acres, made up of three parcels near the northwestern edge of Great Falls in Cascade County, taken into federal trust for gaming purposes. The site lies north of Valleyview Drive, between 9th Street Northwest and Stuckey Road. Planning materials describe a casino, reported at about 500 gaming machines, a 200-room hotel, an indoor waterpark, a bowling alley and arcade, an 8,200-seat arena, restaurants and bars, and associated parking and utilities. The tribe publicly announced the plan in December 2024 and has estimated about 430 long-term jobs.

A separate housing development, known as Good Medicine, would include 41 single-family homes, 18 townhomes and 46 apartments on about 18.4 acres. The tribe has said it does not plan to place the housing in trust; it is proceeding through city annexation instead, and the Great Falls planning board recommended approval of that request in May 2026.

The economic rationale is direct. Tribal Chairman Gerald Gray has said the tribe receives about $480,000 a year in federal funding for member services, which he estimated works out to roughly $65 per member annually. "So that isn't enough," he told Montana Public Radio. "So we have to look at other revenue streams."

Why the two-part determination applies

Congress restored federal recognition to the Little Shell Tribe on December 20, 2019, after decades of seeking it. The tribe does not have a reservation, and IGRA generally prohibits gaming on land taken into trust after October 17, 1988, unless one of the statute's exceptions applies. Our explainer on IGRA's Section 20 two-part determination walks through the main route: the Secretary of the Interior must find that gaming on the land would be in the best interest of the tribe and its members and would not be detrimental to the surrounding community, and the state's governor must concur.

Public materials on the Great Falls project describe that two-part path. IGRA also contains exceptions for restored tribes and initial reservations, which some newly recognized tribes have used, but the federal review now underway is framed around a Secretarial determination. If Interior issues a favorable determination, Montana's governor would have one year to concur, with an optional 180-day extension. Under current timelines, that decision would fall to Governor Greg Gianforte unless the federal process extends beyond his term.

The tribe has also said it is open to negotiating a Class III compact with the state. Montana's tribal gaming market is shaped by state law that caps machine counts and prize values, and compacts there have historically tracked those limits. Our Montana tribal gaming market explainer covers how those caps work and how other tribes in the state have structured their operations.

Where the federal review stands

The Bureau of Indian Affairs' Rocky Mountain Region is preparing an environmental assessment under the National Environmental Policy Act. It issued a notice of intent on January 19, 2026, and a 30-day public scoping period closed on February 19. In March, the BIA extended cooperating-agency status to the City of Great Falls, subject to conditions set by the agency. Cascade County did not submit a response to the BIA's initial notice, according to the county attorney's office.

The BIA has indicated it expects to complete the assessment within about a year of the notice of intent, though that timeline is not firm. Once the draft is ready, the agency will publish a notice of availability and open a public comment period. Our guide to NEPA review in tribal casino fee-to-trust applications explains what an environmental assessment covers and when a project can be elevated to a full environmental impact statement.

The tribe itself has estimated that approvals could take two to four years, an expectation consistent with recent processing times for off-reservation gaming acquisitions.

Local posture and the road ahead

Great Falls officials have engaged without endorsing. City Manager Greg Doyon has said the city retained outside legal counsel and described the project as "truly breaking new ground" for Montana, while emphasizing that the city's response to federal reviewers does not signal approval. Terra Branson, who represents the tribe on the project, has said the tribe is "committed to paying its fair share" and would negotiate service payments with the city or county.

Several questions will shape the next phase. The first is the content of the draft environmental assessment, including traffic, water and wastewater analysis for a site that would host an 8,200-seat arena. The second is how other Montana tribes view a new gaming facility in one of the state's largest cities; intertribal opposition has become a significant factor in off-reservation reviews elsewhere. The third is timing, since fee-to-trust processing has slowed across the federal system this year.

For a tribe restored to federal recognition less than seven years ago, the Great Falls proposal is an attempt to build a governmental revenue base largely from scratch. The Legal Guide covers the broader IGRA framework that will govern each of the decisions still ahead.
