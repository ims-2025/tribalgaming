Oklahoma Senate Turnover Reopens Sports Betting Question for 2027

HB 1047 fell four votes short in April. A changing Senate roster and an incoming governor could restart talks, but the vote math remains tight and tribal positions are split.

By Marcus WhitehorsePublished November 07, 20265 min read

Oklahoma sports betting could return to the legislative agenda in 2027. House Bill 1047 failed in the Senate on April 22, 2026, by a vote of 21 to 27, four votes short of the 25 needed, as we reported in our coverage of the HB 1047 Senate defeat. Following the November 3 election, reporting from LegalSportsBetting.com indicates that six of the 27 senators who voted against the bill will leave when the new session begins, and seven new senators will arrive when it opens February 1.

The arithmetic is not simple, and the tribal gaming industry is not of one mind. This article lays out what changed, what did not, and why the framework that governs Oklahoma's compacts shapes every option on the table.

The vote math

According to the same reporting, one senator who voted yes is also departing. With those changes, the bill's Senate sponsor, Sen. Bill Coleman, would start the new session with 20 returning yes votes, five short of passage. The departures help, but they do not deliver a majority on their own. The seven incoming senators are the unknown; their positions on sports betting have not been widely reported.

The executive branch is also in transition. Gov. Kevin Stitt opposed the bill, though it never reached his desk. The governor-elect, Mike Mazzei, reportedly campaigned on repairing relations with Oklahoma's tribes but has not taken a public position on sports betting. Relations between the governor's office and tribes have been strained in recent years over compact renewal disputes, so a reset in tone could matter as much as any single vote. Our Oklahoma state hub collects background on the state's major gaming tribes.

What the House-passed structure would have done

The floor version of HB 1047 set out three tiers. Retail betting at tribal casinos would begin once the Interior Department approved a tribe's compact supplement. Remote mobile betting on tribal land would follow once five tribes received federal approval. A third tier, described as competitive mobile betting outside any tribe's historic reservation, would activate once the Oklahoma Indian Gaming Association hired an administrator.

That last tier is the most contested. As reported, the bill's geographic definitions would have created a boundary problem: an app tied to a tribe in Durant would not function for a bettor physically in Ardmore, which sits on another nation's land. The House sponsor, Rep. Ken Luttrell, told NonDoc in April that he and Coleman wanted what he called border-to-border, open mobile access. He suggested the Chickasaw Nation might be the most resistant tribe. The Cherokee Nation opposed the limits, while most other gaming tribes backed them. In other words, the tribes themselves hold different views on how open the market should be.

The federal piece

Whatever passes in Oklahoma, sports betting for tribes runs through the compact system. Oklahoma uses a model compact, and any expansion of covered games has to be approved by the Interior Department and published in the Federal Register before it takes effect. That is why the bill tied each tier to federal approval. Readers unfamiliar with the structure can find the framework in our Legal Guide on IGRA and Class III gaming, and the Oklahoma-specific mechanics in our explainer on the model compact's exclusivity fee structure.

Revenue was part of the pitch. Coleman estimated $15 million to $18 million a year from an 8 percent exclusivity fee. As described, up to $7 million of the non-NBA revenue would go to a Thunder-related fund that skips competitive grant reviews, with the balance directed to athletics at the University of Oklahoma and Oklahoma State University. Those earmarks drew attention during the debate and may be revisited.

The competitive pressure

Oklahoma is surrounded by markets with statewide legal sportsbooks, including Kansas, Missouri, Colorado and Arkansas. Coleman has argued that Oklahomans already bet through unregulated channels and that the state receives nothing from it. He has also acknowledged that prediction market apps hold a competitive advantage, since they operate statewide without compact limits. Indian Gaming Association Chairman Matthew Morgan has said such apps undermine tribal gaming compacts, a concern that has driven litigation across the country. We examined the exclusivity payments at stake in our report on Oklahoma exclusivity fees and prediction markets.

The state's ballot-initiative option, discussed after the April defeat, remains a second route; our earlier piece on the ballot pivot after HB 1047 covers that path.

What to watch

Three things will determine whether 2027 differs from 2026: the positions of the seven incoming senators, whether the new governor signals openness, and whether the tribes can agree on how open mobile betting should be. A bill that satisfies the Cherokee Nation's objections may lose the support of tribes that favored the narrower structure. The session opens February 1, and the first indication will likely be the bill filings in the weeks before it.

It is also worth remembering what the failed vote did and did not decide. Nothing in April's defeat changed any tribe's existing compact rights, and tribes continue to operate casinos and pay exclusivity fees under the current model compact. The debate is about whether to add sports wagering to that arrangement, on what terms, and with whose consent. Because compacts are agreements between sovereign governments, no statute alone can impose a structure on a tribe that has not agreed to it, which is one reason the Oklahoma Indian Gaming Association's internal consensus carries so much weight with legislators weighing a new bill.
