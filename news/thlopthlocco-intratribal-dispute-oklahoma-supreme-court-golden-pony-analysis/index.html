Thlopthlocco Ruling Six Months On: Intratribal Disputes and the Casino

State courts are out, the BIA has declined to pick a side, and the forum gap the ruling exposed is a governance risk every gaming tribe should study.

By James KingfisherPublished November 23, 20264 min read

Six months after the Oklahoma Supreme Court ruled that state courts have no authority to decide who governs Thlopthlocco Tribal Town, the case remains the clearest recent illustration of a hard problem in tribal gaming: what happens to a tribal casino when an intratribal dispute leaves no outside body able, or willing, to say who is in charge. Thlopthlocco, a federally recognized Muscogee (Creek) tribal town headquartered near Okemah, operates the Golden Pony Casino along Interstate 40. The casino's operations were disrupted during the leadership fight, and reporting in late 2025 described it as closed after an attempted break-in in early November of that year.

In a decision issued May 19, 2026, the court granted a writ of prohibition barring an Okfuskee County district judge from proceeding with a suit brought by one faction against two rivals. All nine justices agreed on the result. Writing for the majority, Chief Justice Dustin Rowe held that disputes over tribal leadership, elections and governance are nonjusticiable in state court and must be resolved within the tribe.

How the Thlopthlocco dispute reached the state's highest court

According to reporting by NonDoc, KOSU and Casino.org, the dispute involves at least three groups claiming the authority of the tribal town's government. In October 2025, a faction led by Mekko Brent Brown, who had headed the business committee, filed suit in Okfuskee County seeking injunctions and a declaration of which group held lawful authority, and obtained an order the same day. Brown's filing alleged that rival factions had occupied tribal offices, contacted the tribe's bank in a way that froze its finances, and held an election his group considered unauthorized. A second faction, led by Brent Smith, has asserted that it is the lawful government. A third, aligned with Nathan Anderson, whose claim to office dates back to 2007, sought the writ from the Supreme Court.

The Muscogee (Creek) Nation's Lighthorse Police and the Bureau of Indian Affairs investigated the November 2025 incident at the casino. Neither publicly tied it to the leadership dispute.

Deciding which group is the lawful government, the court reasoned, would require interpreting the tribe's own constitution, a task that belongs to the tribe.

The majority relied on the general rule, recognized by federal courts as well, that outside courts lack jurisdiction over purely intratribal disputes. A four-justice concurrence reached the same result by a different route, applying the Bracker balancing test as framed in Oklahoma v. Castro-Huerta and concluding that state jurisdiction was preempted. Our explainer on the Bracker balancing test describes that framework.

The forum gap

The ruling is a straightforward affirmation of tribal self-government. Its practical consequence is that the factions must settle the matter themselves, and the usual outside backstops are limited. NonDoc reported that the BIA, in a November 2025 letter, declined to recognize any faction as the tribe's government, citing precedent that it said prevents it from issuing such a decision. Federal courts generally apply the same nonjusticiability rule the Oklahoma court applied. And in April 2026, the Smith-led group notified the district court that it did not recognize state jurisdiction at all.

That leaves tribal mechanisms, which can be difficult to use when the dispute is over who controls them. Many small tribes do not have a standing tribal court of their own, and borrowing another nation's court raises its own questions of consent and sovereign immunity.

Why intratribal disputes hit casinos first

Gaming enterprises are unusually exposed to leadership disputes because so much of their legal footing runs through the tribal government. Under IGRA, the tribe must hold the sole proprietary interest in its gaming operation, adopt a gaming ordinance approved by the National Indian Gaming Commission, and license the facility and key employees through its own regulatory body. Banks, vendors, payroll processors and federal regulators need to know who can sign on the tribe's behalf. When that question has no agreed answer, routine functions such as releasing payroll, renewing licenses or paying suppliers can stall. Our explainers on the tribal gaming regulatory authority and IGRA's sole proprietary interest requirement explain those links.

Thlopthlocco is not the first case. In October 2014, the Picayune Rancheria of the Chukchansi Indians' casino near Coarsegold, California, was closed after an armed confrontation between rival factions; a federal court and the NIGC acted on safety and regulatory grounds, and the property did not reopen for more than a year. The lesson tribal attorneys drew from Chukchansi was that federal and state authorities will act on public safety and gaming-regulatory compliance, but generally not on the underlying question of who leads the tribe.

Governance lessons for gaming tribes

The episode underscores several practices that tribal governance advisers commonly recommend. A tribal constitution with clear election, succession and removal procedures reduces the room for competing claims. An independent tribal court, or a standing agreement for dispute resolution with a defined forum, gives members a place to bring governance disputes that outsiders will respect. Banking resolutions and signature authorities that specify how they transfer after an election can keep the enterprise operating while a dispute is resolved. And an arm's-length gaming enterprise board and regulatory commission, insulated from day-to-day politics, can preserve continuity for employees and patrons.

None of these measures can prevent a political dispute, and none substitutes for the tribe's own decision about who governs. The Oklahoma Supreme Court's ruling is a reminder that, for good reason, outside courts will not make that decision. Our public reporting review found no confirmed reopening date for the Golden Pony Casino as of this writing.
