Hotel Rooms vs. Slots: How Three Tribal Resort Expansions Compare

Tejon, Wildhorse and Crosswinds are each building hotels; the numbers show why compacts push growth toward rooms.

By James KingfisherPublished October 23, 20264 min read

Three tribal resort expansions now underway or newly launched in 2026 and 2027 offer a way to see how operators think about the relationship between hotel rooms and slot machines. Hard Rock Casino Tejon in California, Wildhorse Resort & Casino in Oregon and Crosswinds Casino Resort in Kansas are different markets with different compact regimes, yet each is adding a substantial hotel to an operating casino. Comparing the numbers the operators have disclosed shows a pattern: hotel-led expansion is increasingly the default growth strategy for established tribal properties, and the right ratio depends on the market rather than a formula.

The disclosed numbers

At Tejon, the Tejon Indian Tribe and Hard Rock International broke ground on October 22, 2026, on a project of more than $725 million. It features a 406-room hotel, including 119 suites, and adds 900 slot machines for a total of 3,000, with an opening targeted for summer 2028. That works out to roughly one hotel room for every 7.4 slot machines on the completed floor.

The Confederated Tribes of the Umatilla Indian Reservation announced a $100 million expansion at Wildhorse in May 2025 that includes a 10-story, 214-room tower and a 15,000-square-foot convention and event center, bringing the property to more than 400 rooms. Wildhorse reported about 1,100 slot machines at the time of the announcement, which implies a much higher room-to-slot ratio than Tejon, though that comparison mixes current gaming positions with a future hotel.

The Wyandotte Nation broke ground in June 2025 on a Crosswinds expansion in Park City, Kansas, built around a 15-story, 238-room hotel with 32 suites and an 8,500-square-foot ballroom, with completion anticipated in summer 2027. The expanded casino is described as having more than 1,400 slot machines. The Nation says the expansion will create 350 new jobs.

Why the ratios differ

A simple room-to-slot comparison is imperfect, but it highlights structural differences. Wildhorse is a destination in a small regional market in Eastern Oregon, with a golf course and family entertainment complex that give guests reasons to stay. Rooms there serve a leisure and event customer, and the added convention space supports group business. Crosswinds sits in the Wichita area, and its hotel is designed around a stay-and-play proposition, a phrase the Nation's economic development division used when describing the project. Tejon draws from a larger population base in California and a brand, Hard Rock, with its own loyalty network.

In each case, the hotel does work that a larger slot floor cannot. It lengthens stays, adds revenue lines outside gaming, and gives the property something to sell to customers who are not primarily gamblers. Our analysis of hotel occupancy, ADR and RevPAR explains the metrics operators use to judge whether those hotels pay off, and our review of the 2026 hotel tower wave looks at the broader trend.

Compacts set the ceiling on the floor

Slot machine counts are not purely a business decision. In many states, tribal-state compacts cap or allocate devices, which is one reason hotel investment can be more attractive than floor expansion. Where the number of machines is constrained, non-gaming amenities become the lever for growth. We cover the mechanics in our explainer on device caps in tribal-state compacts.

Kansas and Oregon illustrate the point from different directions. The Wyandotte Nation operates in Kansas under its own compact arrangements, while Oregon's tribes operate under state compacts that also address matters such as community infrastructure. The state-by-state differences are summarized in our Kansas and Oregon market deep-dives.

When a compact limits devices, rooms become the growth lever, and the question shifts from how many machines to how many nights.

Risks of the hotel-first model

Hotel-led expansion is not without risk. Hotels carry fixed operating costs, depend on labor that is difficult to recruit in many tribal casino markets, and take years to build. A project announced in 2025 may be delivered into a different economic environment in 2027 or 2028. Operators also face the possibility that rooms lag in markets with limited year-round visitation. Our analysis of construction cost inflation and the labor squeeze covers these pressures.

The disclosed figures also have limits. Not every operator publishes cost, occupancy or financing details, and the numbers cited here reflect what the tribes and their partners have announced rather than audited results. Readers should treat the ratios as an orienting device, not a benchmark.

Even so, the direction is consistent across these three projects. Established tribal properties are using their gaming cash flow to build hotels, convention space and dining, converting casinos into resorts. How well each performs will be measurable in the next few years, once the rooms are open and occupancy figures begin to circulate.

What to measure next

As these projects open, three indicators will show whether the model works: average daily rate and occupancy at the new hotels, the share of hotel guests who also play, and the change in non-gaming revenue as a percentage of the total. Tribal operators vary in how much they disclose, and many report results only to their tribal councils and regulators. Where figures become public, such as in annual reports or lender materials, they will allow a more rigorous comparison than the announcement-stage data used here.

For now, the most defensible conclusion is narrow: three operators in three states have chosen to put hundreds of rooms and, in some cases, hundreds of millions of dollars behind the idea that a tribal casino's next stage of growth is a place to stay. The compact frameworks that govern each property help explain why.
