Gaming Enforcement's New Toolkit: Payment Processors and App Stores

When regulators cannot reach an offshore operator directly, they increasingly go after the money and the distribution channels, a shift with consequences for tribal and First Nations exclusivity.

By Sarah BegayPublished October 12, 20266 min read

The gaming enforcement toolkit regulators rely on has shifted from targeting operators to targeting the infrastructure around them. In explaining how Alberta intends to keep unlicensed online gambling out of its new regulated market, the province's minister pointed to cease-and-desist letters and pressure on payment processors, and reporting also noted possible pressure on technology platforms over gambling apps and advertising. He described the first two as methods used by several U.S. states. For tribal gaming, where exclusivity is the foundation of the economic bargain, the effectiveness of these tools is a central question.

Why direct enforcement against offshore operators falls short

Licensing regimes work only if the perimeter holds. A regulated market, whether created by a tribal-state compact, a provincial statute or an exclusivity agreement, depends on customers using licensed operators that pay taxes, fees or revenue shares and follow consumer-protection rules. An operator based outside the jurisdiction, with no license and no assets to seize locally, is difficult to reach through ordinary legal channels. A regulator can send a letter or sue, but judgments against an offshore company are hard to collect, and the company can keep serving customers while the process unfolds.

Regulators have therefore looked for chokepoints. Two stand out: the payment rails that move customer deposits and withdrawals, and the digital distribution channels, such as app stores, search results and social platforms, through which customers find and install gambling products. Both are operated by companies that are within the regulator's reach and that have their own reasons to avoid being associated with illegal activity.

The tools in practice

The first tool is the cease-and-desist letter. It is cheap, quick and creates a formal record that an operator was on notice. State regulators in the United States have used it against a range of operators, including platforms offering event contracts. Our reports on the Connecticut cease-and-desist to nine prediction markets and the Michigan halt order describe how states have used administrative orders in disputes that touch tribal exclusivity. Such letters rarely end the activity on their own, but they set the stage for litigation and for escalating steps.

The second tool is pressure on payment processors. Gambling transactions pass through banks, card networks and payment intermediaries, which are sensitive to regulatory and reputational risk. A regulator that notifies processors that a particular operator is unlicensed can make it harder for that operator to accept deposits or pay out winnings. The method does not require jurisdiction over the operator itself, only over the financial institutions serving local customers. Its limits are equally clear: operators can route around one processor by using another, or by shifting to payment methods that are harder to monitor.

The third tool is pressure on platforms. Unlicensed apps and advertising reach consumers through app stores, search engines and social media. A regulator can ask those platforms to remove unlicensed apps or restrict ads, relying on the platforms' own policies on illegal content. Results depend on the platform's willingness to cooperate and on how clearly the regulator can show a product is unlicensed.

Why this matters to tribal and First Nations gaming

Tribal gaming is built on legally protected markets. Under IGRA, Class III gaming proceeds under a tribal-state compact, and many compacts trade revenue sharing for a degree of exclusivity. When online or quasi-gambling products operate outside that framework, they erode the value of the bargain. Our reporting on sweepstakes casino bans and tribal exclusivity shows how states have acted against one such category. The Alberta example, covered in our report on the October 13 iGaming deadline, shows a province considering the same toolkit to protect a licensed market in which First Nations-owned operators participate.

The toolkit also reveals what it cannot do. It works best when the target is clearly unlicensed and the regulator has a clear legal basis. It is far less effective where the legality of the product is itself contested, as in the dispute over whether prediction-market event contracts are sports wagering subject to state and tribal law or federally regulated swaps. In that setting, payment processors and platforms are unlikely to act on a regulator's say-so alone, because the question is being litigated. The legal questions in that dispute are summarized in the Legal Guide.

What regulators and tribes should watch

Several open questions will determine how useful these tools become. One is transparency: Alberta's minister declined to describe specifics, citing a desire not to tip off operators, which makes it harder to evaluate results. Another is durability: enforcement that depends on cooperation from large financial and technology companies can change quickly if those companies change policy. A third is coordination: unlicensed operators serve customers across borders, so a patchwork of provincial and state actions leaves gaps.

For tribal governments, the lesson is that exclusivity requires active defense. Written protections in compacts and statutes matter, but so do the practical means of enforcing them. Regulators that can credibly interrupt payments and distribution give licensed operators, including tribal ones, a better chance of keeping the customers a legal market was designed to serve. Those that cannot may find that the protected market exists mainly on paper.

We have not found published data on how effective payment-processor or platform pressure has been in any of these jurisdictions, and this analysis does not claim otherwise. What the record supports is that regulators are turning to these tools and that the choice reflects a recognition that licensing alone does not secure a market.
