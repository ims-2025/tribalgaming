Pauma Band Opens Kiosk Sportsbook at Casino Pauma, Testing California Law

A small San Diego County tribe launched sports wagering in a state where no compact authorizes it. The legal basis has not been made public.

By Sarah BegayPublished November 13, 20264 min read

The Pauma Band of Luiseño Indians opened a sports wagering operation on the casino floor at Casino Pauma on Thursday evening, November 12, timing the launch to Thursday Night Football. The Pauma sportsbook is, by the tribe's own description, a historic step for a small San Diego County property. It is also a development that will draw close attention across California, where no tribal-state compact has authorized sports betting and voters rejected two sports wagering measures in 2022.

The details come from a press release dated November 12 in Pauma Valley and distributed by SCCG Management, a Las Vegas advisory firm that says it worked with the casino on planning and launch. As of publication, TribalGaming.com had not located independent reporting, a statement from state officials, or a public filing describing the legal basis for the operation. What follows reports the tribe's and its partner's account, and sets out the legal questions the launch raises.

What the Pauma sportsbook offers

According to the release, the Pauma Sportsbook runs on eight self-service kiosks on the casino floor. Guests 18 and older place wagers on major sports using cash and receive a printed ticket; winning tickets are redeemed at the casino cage. Betting is available only at Casino Pauma, and the release says mobile wagering, limited to the casino property, is planned for this winter.

Former San Diego Chargers linebacker Shawne Merriman placed what the release calls the first official wager, a bet on the Los Angeles Chargers to beat the Baltimore Ravens on Monday, November 16. Merriman later hosted a private meet-and-greet at the property.

Tribal Chairman Temet Majel framed the launch in governmental terms. "This sportsbook represents the Tribe's continued commitment to self-determination," he said in the release. General Manager Oscar Garcia said the launch "reflects the Tribe's vision, leadership, and continued commitment." SCCG founder and CEO Stephen A. Crystal said, "The Tribe's inherent sovereignty is the foundation of this achievement."

The format matters. A kiosk-and-ticket model with cage redemption is the simplest form of retail sports wagering: there is no account registration, no remote access, and no geolocation layer. The plan for on-property mobile betting resembles the approach taken by tribes in other states that have kept mobile wagering inside the property line, such as the Three Rivers Casino on-property mobile model in Oregon.

The legal questions in California

Under the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act, sports wagering is Class III gaming. A tribe may conduct Class III gaming only on Indian lands, only in a state that "permits such gaming for any purpose by any person," and only under a tribal-state compact approved or deemed approved by the Interior Department. The TribalGaming.com Legal Guide covers those three requirements in detail, and our explainer on IGRA's scope-of-gaming test explains how courts have read the "permits such gaming" language.

California's compacts authorize slot machines, banking and percentage card games, and lottery games. They do not list sports wagering. In 2022, Proposition 26, which would have allowed in-person sports betting at tribal casinos and certain racetracks, and Proposition 27, which would have allowed online sports betting, both failed. The state's tribes have since been working toward a 2028 ballot measure; the California Nations Indian Gaming Association has circulated three possible 2028 sports betting models to the industry.

Against that background, the release does not say whether Pauma relies on a compact amendment, a reading of its existing compact, a classification argument, or a theory grounded in sovereignty alone. It does not mention approval from the California Gambling Control Commission, the state Department of Justice, the National Indian Gaming Commission, or the Interior Department. Those omissions are not proof that no legal basis exists. They do mean the operation's footing cannot yet be assessed from the public record.

The open question is not whether a tribe can run a sports betting kiosk, but which legal authority the Pauma Band says covers it, and how the State of California responds.

Under IGRA, a state that believes a tribe is conducting Class III gaming in violation of a compact may sue in federal court to enjoin it. The NIGC also has enforcement authority over gaming on Indian lands. Neither path has been publicly invoked so far. The Pauma Band is no stranger to federal court in disputes with the state; it litigated against California for years over compact license fees.

Why the launch matters beyond one property

Casino Pauma is one of the smaller gaming operations in North San Diego County, a market that includes Pechanga, Pala, Rincon's Harrah's Resort Southern California, and Valley View. A small property moving first on a product that larger tribes have deliberately routed through the 2028 ballot process is notable on its own terms.

It also arrives during a transition in Sacramento. Governor-elect Xavier Becerra, who takes office in January, has not publicly stated a position on gambling expansion, though as attorney general he sided with tribes in disputes with commercial cardrooms. Any state response to Pauma's operation may fall to his incoming administration.

For other California tribes, the launch creates a strategic question. The industry's coordinated position has been that sports wagering should come through a voter-approved constitutional change that keeps it tribal and keeps out operators and prediction-market platforms that tribes view as a threat. A unilateral launch tests that consensus, whether or not other tribes publicly react.
