BC First Nations Gaming Revenue Sharing: Inside the 7% Model

British Columbia's 25-year, roughly $3 billion arrangement pays every eligible First Nation, casino or not, and leaves the distribution to First Nations institutions.

By James KingfisherPublished November 24, 20264 min read

British Columbia's approach to First Nations gaming revenue sharing gets less attention than Ontario's or Saskatchewan's, but it is one of the broadest arrangements in Canada. Through the BC First Nations Gaming Revenue Sharing Limited Partnership, the province shares 7 percent of the BC Lottery Corporation's net income with First Nations each year until 2045. The partnership estimates the total over the 25-year term at about $3 billion. The money is paid to First Nations governments, not to casino hosts, and it reaches every eligible nation in the province, whether or not a gaming facility is anywhere nearby.

Six years after the long-term agreement was signed, the BC model is useful for comparison. It shows one answer to a question every Canadian province eventually faces: how should First Nations share in gaming revenue when the province, rather than the nations, holds the legal authority to conduct and manage gaming?

How the BC First Nations gaming revenue sharing agreement works

The arrangement began with an interim agreement signed on August 2, 2019, which covered payments for the 2019/20 and 2020/21 fiscal years. The legislature amended the Gaming Control Act through Bill 36, which received Royal Assent on October 31, 2019, so that the payments could be made. The long-term agreement followed on September 16, 2020 and covers a further 23 years. According to the partnership, $194.84 million was transferred for the first two years, and annual distributions have been expected each May since 2021.

The limited partnership was created by the First Nations Gaming Commission. Its general partner is BCFN GRS GP Inc., and a board appointed by First Nations oversees it. That design is deliberate. The province hands over the money, and First Nations institutions run the distribution, rather than a provincial ministry.

The allocation formula, developed by the First Nations Gaming Commission and approved by the BC Assembly of First Nations, the First Nations Summit and the Union of BC Indian Chiefs, divides each year's pool three ways:

50 percent is divided equally among all participating First Nations; 40 percent is distributed by population, counting registered and treaty members both on and off reserve; and 10 percent is reserved for geographically remote communities.

The 50 percent equal share is the key design choice. It means a small nation with a few hundred members receives a substantial base amount every year, and the remoteness share recognizes that the same dollar buys less in a fly-in community. The formula was set up for review after five years, which leaves room for adjustment as the program matures.

Restrictions, reporting and accountability

Funds must be spent in six broad categories: health and wellness; infrastructure, safety, transportation and housing; economic and business development; education, language, culture and training; community development and environmental protection; and capacity-building, fiscal management and governance. Direct per capita distributions to individuals are not allowed. That differs from the per capita payments some U.S. tribes make under an approved revenue allocation plan.

Each participating nation files an annual spending statement, which the partnership forwards to an Independent Appointee, a third-party auditor appointed jointly by the partnership and the province. Provincial and partnership materials describe the statement's form somewhat differently, but the enforcement mechanism is the same in both. Payments to a non-compliant nation can be suspended, and a nation that remains out of compliance five months after suspension may forfeit its share for that period. U.S. readers can compare this with the five permitted uses of net gaming revenue under the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act, discussed in our Legal Guide.

Revenue sharing and ownership: the BC model in national context

The BC partnership is a pure revenue-sharing model. First Nations receive a defined share of provincial gaming profit but do not own or operate the BCLC system that produces it. Saskatchewan is different: SIGA operates First Nations casinos, and its income flows through the First Nations Trust. Ontario's arrangement through the Ontario First Nations Limited Partnership is closest to BC's, with a percentage of provincial gaming revenue shared across nations. For a side-by-side view, see our comparison of Canadian First Nations gaming revenue frameworks.

British Columbia is also seeing First Nations ownership grow alongside the revenue-sharing stream. Several nations have acquired casino properties from private operators in the past two years, a trend covered in our report on BC First Nations casino acquisitions. Those deals give individual nations equity, operating control and local jobs, which a revenue-sharing distribution cannot provide. They also concentrate the gains among the nations with the capital to buy.

Seen this way, the two approaches complement each other. The 7 percent stream gives every nation in the province a predictable floor of funding, regardless of geography, and the acquisitions create stronger economic positions for a smaller group. Our analysis of revenue sharing versus ownership sets out the trade-offs in more detail.

The pressure on the model is easy to identify. The payment is tied to BCLC's net income, so anything that reduces that figure, such as competition from unregulated online sites, changes in the provincial online market or economic cycles, flows directly into First Nations budgets. A fixed percentage protects First Nations from political renegotiation but not from market risk. With almost two decades left in the term, the scheduled formula review and the province's online gaming policy will do the most to determine whether the estimated $3 billion is actually paid out.
