Seneca Exclusivity Zone and New York's VLT Casinos: What's at Stake

Beyond the 25 percent headline, state-licensed terminals sit at the center of the exclusivity argument.

By Daniel YazziePublished October 27, 20264 min read

The Seneca Nation of Indians and New York State remain without a signed Class III gaming compact nearly three years after the last one expired. On October 2, 2026, Seneca Nation President J.C. Seneca said the two sides had negotiated an agreement in principle that would eliminate the nation's 25 percent revenue-sharing obligation. A spokesperson for Governor Kathy Hochul responded that no compact has been agreed and that discussions are continuing. Amid that dispute, one competitive factor has received less attention than the revenue-share headline: state-regulated video lottery terminal facilities operating inside the territory where the Senecas hold exclusive rights. This analysis examines the Seneca exclusivity zone and the role of video lottery terminals in the negotiation.

The expired bargain

Under the compact that expired in December 2023, the Senecas paid the state 25 percent of slot win and, in exchange, held exclusive rights to casino-style gaming in Upstate New York west of State Route 14. Exclusivity is the central consideration in this kind of agreement: the state receives a revenue share only because the tribe receives meaningful protection from competition. Our primer on exclusivity clauses and payment suspension describes why tribes tie their payments to that protection.

Since the compact lapsed, the Senecas have continued to set aside 25 percent of slot win in an escrow account rather than paying it to the state. The public record does not show a total escrow balance, so any estimate would be speculative. What is clear is the structure: money is being held pending a deal that determines who ultimately receives it.

Three VLT casinos inside the zone

The Senecas argue that state-licensed video lottery terminals violate their exclusivity. Three VLT facilities operate within the zone: Hamburg Gaming, Batavia Downs Gaming and Finger Lakes Gaming. Together they host nearly 3,000 terminals and reported combined VLT revenue of $27.4 million in August, according to trade reporting. The nation characterizes these devices as slot-like machines that infringe on its compact rights. The detailed legal reasoning has not been laid out publicly, and the state's view of how the machines fit within the exclusivity provision is not established in the sources reviewed here.

The tension is a familiar one in tribal gaming. State lottery products and racetrack-based terminals sit in a gray area between lottery and casino gaming, and tribes with exclusivity clauses often contend that such products breach their rights. We examined a related flashpoint in our coverage of historical horse racing machines. The legal question usually turns on the exact wording of the compact's exclusivity clause and how it defines covered games.

Why the VLT question affects the revenue-share math

If the state holds that VLTs are a lawful lottery product, then its position is that the exclusivity it granted remains intact and a revenue share is justified. If the tribe's position prevails, the exclusivity has been diluted and a payment reduction or elimination becomes easier to defend. The Senecas have taken the most aggressive version of that stance in the current round, seeking to remove the 25 percent obligation altogether. Governor Hochul's office has not accepted that characterization, according to the reporting.

Scale matters here. A reported $27.4 million in monthly VLT revenue across the three facilities is meaningful, but it is a fraction of what a large tribal casino operation generates annually. That is why the VLT dispute functions as leverage and principle as much as a pure revenue contest. Each concession affects the precedent for what exclusivity must cover in any new compact.

Host cities and the Legislature

The nation has said any agreement would go to the state Legislature for approval after it convenes next spring, a timeline that makes the first half of 2027 a critical window. Local governments in Seneca host communities have a direct fiscal stake, since revenue-sharing flows historically supported municipal budgets. We covered that dimension in our report on the agreement in principle and host cities and earlier in our look at the December compact deadline.

The nation also argues that the governor's team was hostile in negotiations and offered nothing of value in return for a share of revenue. Those are the nation's characterizations; the governor's office describes its discussions as productive. Observers should treat both descriptions as positions rather than findings.

What to watch

Three markers will indicate direction. The first is whether the state publicly addresses the VLT question, either in a compact term or in statements about the lottery's scope. The second is whether any text of the agreement in principle is released, since the details of exclusivity language will matter more than the headline about revenue sharing. The third is the legislative calendar: without legislative approval, the compact status quo continues, with escrowed payments and unresolved claims.
