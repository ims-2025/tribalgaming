Wilton Rancheria Adds 90 Acres to Trust Around Sky River Casino

The fee-to-trust milestone gives one of California's newest gaming tribes room to build out a full resort campus around Elk Grove.

By Elena RuizPublished July 30, 20264 min read

The Wilton Rancheria has placed roughly 90 acres of land surrounding its Sky River Casino into federal trust, a quiet but consequential step that gives one of California's youngest gaming tribes the legal footing to grow a single casino into a full resort campus. The Wilton Rancheria fee-to-trust action, confirmed in late July, converts privately held parcels near the tribe's flagship property in Elk Grove into "Indian lands" for jurisdictional purposes, the category that governs where gaming and other tribal government functions can lawfully occur.

For a tribe that only regained federal recognition in 2009 and opened its first casino in 2022, the announcement is less about a single ribbon-cutting than about securing a runway. Sky River opened as a day-one success, but the surrounding land it sits on has to travel through the federal trust process before the tribe can treat it as a sovereign campus rather than a collection of ordinary Sacramento County parcels.

Why the trust status matters

Under the framework most readers can trace through our Legal Guide to IGRA and Class III gaming, a tribe generally must conduct gaming on land held in trust by the United States. Fee land — property a tribe owns outright, like any private buyer — does not by itself carry the jurisdictional status that supports a casino, a hotel governed by tribal ordinances, or the tribe's own regulatory authority. Moving the acreage into trust folds it into the reservation land base and places it under tribal and federal jurisdiction rather than state and county control.

That distinction shapes everything from zoning to taxation to the reach of the tribe's gaming commission. A hotel tower, parking structures, event space, or back-of-house infrastructure built on trust land answers to the tribe's building codes and its compact with California, not to Elk Grove's municipal permitting. For the Wilton Rancheria, consolidating the land around Sky River under one jurisdictional umbrella removes the patchwork risk of operating a modern resort across parcels with different legal footing.

Trust status is the difference between owning land near a casino and owning land that can legally become part of one.

A California pattern

The Wilton action lands amid a broader wave of California tribes using the trust process to anchor expansions and new builds. The same mechanism is at the center of higher-profile fights elsewhere in the state, including the contested projects tracked in our coverage of the Scotts Valley Band's Vallejo casino, where opponents have challenged the very question of whether newly acquired land qualifies. Wilton's path has been comparatively smooth because Sky River is already an operating, on-reservation-adjacent facility rather than a greenfield project in a new market — a distinction that tends to lower the temperature of the federal review.

Still, the legal terrain is not frictionless. The Supreme Court's 2009 decision in Carcieri v. Salazar narrowed which tribes the Interior Department may take land into trust for, a ruling whose long shadow we unpack in our Carcieri analysis. Tribes recognized or restored after 1934 have at times faced additional scrutiny over whether they fall within the statute's reach. Wilton's restoration and subsequent land actions have proceeded, but the case underscores why each trust acquisition is treated as a discrete legal event rather than a formality.

What comes next in Elk Grove

With the surrounding acreage secured, the tribe has signaled it intends to build out the amenities that turn a regional gaming floor into a destination resort — hotel rooms, meeting and event capacity, and expanded dining. Sky River entered a crowded but affluent Sacramento-region market and has performed strongly enough to justify the reinvestment; the constraint has been land status, not demand. The trust decision removes that constraint.

The move also cements Elk Grove's place on the map of California's fast-maturing gaming corridor, a market whose scale and concentration we examine in the California state hub. For local governments, the shift is double-edged: trust land narrows the municipal tax and permitting base, but a larger resort typically expands the payroll, visitation, and the negotiated service payments that tribes commonly extend to host communities.

The trust decision also reframes the tribe's relationship with Elk Grove and Sacramento County. Since Sky River opened, the Wilton Rancheria has leaned on negotiated agreements with local governments to smooth traffic, public-safety, and infrastructure concerns — the kind of intergovernmental deal-making that has become standard practice for tribes operating in populous, non-rural markets. Consolidating the campus under trust status does not erase those relationships; if anything, it makes them more important, because the tribe and the city will now be coordinating as neighboring sovereigns over a larger, more complex footprint. Chairman Jesus Tarango has consistently framed the tribe's Elk Grove presence as a partnership rather than an intrusion, and the expansion will test how durable that framing is as the resort's scale grows.

None of this happens overnight. Fee-to-trust conversions clear the legal path, but design, financing, and construction still stretch across years. What the Wilton Rancheria has now that it lacked a month ago is certainty — the assurance that when it builds, it will be building on land that answers to the tribe. In tribal gaming, that certainty is the asset everything else is built on.
