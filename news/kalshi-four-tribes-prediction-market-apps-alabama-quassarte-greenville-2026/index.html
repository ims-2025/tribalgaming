Four More Tribes Partner With Kalshi, Deepening Split in Indian Country

Alabama-Quassarte, Greenville Rancheria, Kletsel Dehe and Alturas will own their apps while Kalshi runs the exchange, even as other tribes litigate against it.

By Elena RuizPublished October 13, 20265 min read

Four more tribal nations announced this month that they will launch prediction-market apps powered by Kalshi, a development that sharpens an already visible divide over whether sports-event contracts belong inside or outside Indian gaming law. The decision by the Alabama-Quassarte Tribal Town of Oklahoma, the Greenville Rancheria, the Kletsel Dehe Wintun Nation and the Alturas Indian Rancheria, both of the latter two in California alongside Greenville, follows the Tunica-Biloxi Tribe's September announcement and means that tribes partner with Kalshi in a growing, if still minority, set of cases.

The timing is awkward for the tribal gaming organizations that are fighting Kalshi. Other tribes are pursuing the company in federal court and before federal regulators, arguing that sports-event contracts are Class III gaming that requires a tribal-state compact. The new partners are taking the opposite practical route, building businesses on the exchange rather than litigating against it.

How the deals are structured

According to reporting on the announcement, each tribe will own its app and control its brand and customer relationships, while Kalshi operates the underlying federally regulated exchange. The apps carry distinct names: PokkeTrades for Alabama-Quassarte, Sovereign Edge for the Kletsel Dehe Wintun Nation through its Economic Development Authority, an app named Greenville for the Greenville Rancheria, and AIR Markets for Alturas. The Tunica-Biloxi venture, announced earlier, is SaltTrade Derivatives, which we covered in our report on the first tribal prediction-market app.

The ownership split is the important design choice. By holding the brand and the customer relationship, the tribes position the apps as tribal enterprises rather than white-label products, while leaving regulatory responsibility for the exchange itself with Kalshi under federal commodities law. Whether that arrangement produces meaningful revenue is not yet known, and the announcements did not publish financial projections.

The argument from geography

Tribal leaders explaining the decision have emphasized limits on land and location. Alabama-Quassarte Chief Wilson Yargee said geography has constrained gaming revenue for many tribes and that prediction markets offer a way to build revenue beyond those limits, and he asked other tribes to respect the nation's decision on its own land. Michael Stopp of Alabama-Quassarte described the venture the same way. Eric Wright of the Kletsel Economic Development Authority said a digital venture could fund services for tribal citizens without the land and infrastructure a casino requires.

That reasoning speaks to a real structural problem in Indian gaming. A casino's value depends heavily on proximity to population, and many tribes hold remote or small land bases. We examine that dynamic in our analysis of the urban-rural performance gap. Industry analyst Ryan Butler of Covers characterized the split as reflecting long-standing differences between large gaming nations and smaller, land-poor ones, and noted that these deals alone are unlikely to shift the broader legal fight.

The legal objection has not gone away

The California Nations Indian Gaming Association responded that the partnerships do not resolve the legal objections to the contracts. CNIGA said it respects each nation's right to pursue economic opportunities but that other tribes' sovereign rights should not suffer because of a few tribes' decisions. The underlying position of tribal gaming organizations is that sports-event contracts are Class III gaming under the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act and therefore subject to compacts, which is the question at issue in the litigation. The Legal Guide explains how IGRA classifies games and why that classification controls who may offer them.

Kalshi's position is that its sports-event contracts fall under federal financial regulation rather than state or tribal gaming law. Federal appeals courts have not aligned. A unanimous Ninth Circuit panel sided in September with two California tribes seeking to stop Kalshi from offering sports wagers on their land without authorization, and a separate Ninth Circuit ruling on Sept. 1 cleared Nevada to enforce its gambling laws against the company. Meanwhile, 145 tribes and tribal organizations have urged the Supreme Court to resolve conflicting Third and Ninth Circuit rulings in Flaherty v. KalshiEX. Our earlier piece on the divide over Kalshi partnerships and the litigation scorecard trace how these threads fit together.

What it means for the broader debate

Tribal sovereignty includes the right of each nation to choose its own economic path, and supporters of the partnerships frame the decisions that way. Opponents counter that a prediction-market business built on contracts tied to sporting events could undermine the exclusivity that other tribes negotiated and paid for in their compacts. Both claims can be sincerely held, and neither is resolved by the announcements themselves.

Two practical questions will determine how much weight the partnerships carry. First, whether the apps generate revenue at a scale that matters to small nations, which will only be visible over time. Second, whether the courts ultimately treat the underlying contracts as federally regulated swaps or as gaming. A ruling that sports-event contracts are Class III gaming could complicate the position of tribes operating on the exchange, while a ruling for Kalshi would strengthen the case that these ventures stand on firm ground.
