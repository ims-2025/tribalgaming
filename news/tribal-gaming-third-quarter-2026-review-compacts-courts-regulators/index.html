Tribal Gaming Q3 2026 Review: Compacts, Courts and Regulators

Longer compacts, narrower revenue sharing and tougher standing rules: what six weeks of developments say about the direction of tribal gaming policy.

By Sarah BegayPublished October 21, 20264 min read

Looking back over the last six weeks of tribal gaming developments, a pattern emerges that is easy to miss when each story is read on its own. Compacts are being ratified and extended, and in places renegotiated with less revenue sharing. Federal courts are narrowing the avenues through which one tribe can challenge another. Regulators are operating with reduced leadership, and the legal contest over prediction markets has split Indian Country. This quarterly review draws those threads together from the reporting we have published since mid-September.

Compacts: longer terms, more machines, less revenue sharing

The clearest policy signal came from California, where Governor Gavin Newsom signed legislation in late September ratifying gaming agreements with six tribes, including Agua Caliente, the Chumash and Pechanga. The amended Agua Caliente compact extends through 2061, and Pechanga is set to be authorized for 1,000 additional gaming machines. We compared the terms in our report on the six California compacts. The long terms matter for planning: tribes can finance multi-year resort builds against a compact that runs decades, a point explored in our analysis of compact term length and capital planning.

In New York, the Seneca Nation announced on October 5 that a new gaming compact had been “negotiated in principle” with Governor Kathy Hochul. The nation’s description of the talks drew a sharply different characterization from the governor’s side, and local officials have raised concerns about what a compact without the traditional revenue-sharing payments would mean for host cities, as we reported in our coverage of the agreement in principle. Revenue sharing is the hinge on which many state-tribal bargains turn; our revenue sharing explainer sets out the basics.

Michigan offered the counterpoint. Tribal payments into the state’s strategic fund fell 71 percent as exclusivity disputes mounted, a figure that has put pressure on a state economic development agency. Our report on the payment decline and the Michigan state hub provide detail. The lesson is that revenue-sharing arrangements depend on exclusivity, and when exclusivity is perceived to erode, payments can change quickly.

Courts: standing and deference

Two September rulings in Washington, D.C., point in the same direction. In the Coquille case, Judge Amit Mehta upheld Interior’s decision to take land in Medford, Oregon, into trust for gaming under the restored-lands exception. In the Oklahoma case, Judge Timothy Kelly dismissed a challenge by the Cherokee, Chickasaw, Choctaw and Citizen Potawatomi Nations to the 2020 compacts of the Comanche Nation and Otoe-Missouria Tribe, holding that the plaintiffs lacked standing. In both cases, tribes that argued they would lose revenue to a competitor did not prevail. The appeal windows for both are discussed in our court calendar.

In California, a judge on October 2 rejected a bid to reopen the Scotts Valley Band’s Vallejo casino, describing roughly $10 million in losses as self-inflicted, according to reporting by Casino.org. See our report on the preliminary injunction denial. In Minnesota, the state Public Utilities Commission ruled in the Upper Sioux Community’s favor in a dispute over solar power at Prairie’s Edge Casino, though the utility has indicated it will likely appeal; see our report on the ruling.

Regulators: a commission short a chair

The National Indian Gaming Commission has had no chairperson since January, and as of the latest reporting the president had not nominated a successor. Because functions such as approving tribal gaming ordinances and certifying management agreements fall to the chair, the vacancy has practical effects. The Iowa Tribe of Oklahoma, for instance, has been unable to transfer operations of its new Harrah’s casino to the operator and is managing the property itself. We have covered the consequences in our report on stalled approvals.

Prediction markets: a divided Indian Country

The most contested policy question remains prediction markets. In early October the Commodity Futures Trading Commission sent two proposed regulatory changes to the White House for review, while four tribal nations announced partnerships with Kalshi even as other tribes challenge the company in court. The Seminole Tribe separately sued DraftKings over its prediction products on October 8. Our analysis of the divide and our primer on how exclusivity works explain what is at stake. Florida readers can also consult the Seminole Tribe directory page.

Reading the quarter

Taken together, the quarter shows a sector that is expanding physically and extending its legal footing, while the terms of its bargains with states, and the tools its competitors can use against it, are shifting. Whether the Seneca and Michigan experiences become the template for other states, or remain outliers, is the open question heading into 2027.

Three cross-currents deserve emphasis for readers tracking the sector. First, capital planning and compact design are converging: tribes that win longer terms or larger machine counts, as in the California package, gain the certainty needed to finance resort expansions, while tribes negotiating without revenue-sharing obligations, as the Seneca Nation says it is doing, may reduce what flows to host communities even as they gain flexibility. Second, the courts have so far been unreceptive to competitive-harm arguments between tribes, which suggests that disputes over market share are more likely to be resolved through negotiation and legislation than through litigation. Third, the regulatory environment is uneven: a federal commission operating without a chair coexists with state agencies and tribal gaming commissions that continue to carry the day-to-day oversight burden. Our explainer on the layers of tribal casino regulation describes how those responsibilities divide.

None of these trends is settled. Appeals are possible in both September federal cases, the Seneca compact still requires formal steps before it takes effect, and the prediction-market questions are before multiple courts and federal agencies at once. We will update this review as the fourth quarter unfolds.
