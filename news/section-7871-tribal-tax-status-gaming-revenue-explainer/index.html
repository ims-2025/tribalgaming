Section 7871 and Tribal Tax Status: What It Means for Gaming Revenue

A 1983 federal law gives tribal governments state-like treatment in parts of the tax code. Its reach into gaming is narrower than many assume.

By Daniel YazziePublished November 04, 20264 min read

Tribal gaming revenue is generally not subject to federal income tax at the tribal government level, because federal tax law has long treated tribes as sovereign governments rather than taxable entities. A separate provision, Section 7871 of the Internal Revenue Code, addresses a different question: when tribal governments are treated like states for specific tax purposes. Because the two ideas are often blended, this explainer separates them and describes how Section 7871 relates to tribal gaming.

This is a general overview, not tax advice. Tribal governments and their advisers should consult counsel on specific transactions.

What Section 7871 does

Congress enacted the Indian Tribal Governmental Tax Status Act in 1982, and Section 7871 took effect shortly afterward. The provision directs that an Indian tribal government is treated as a state for certain federal tax purposes. Those purposes include the deductibility of charitable contributions made to the tribe for public purposes, exemption from certain federal excise taxes, and eligibility to issue tax-exempt bonds under rules that apply to states and local governments.

The bond provision is the one most relevant to the economics of tribal enterprises, and it carries an important restriction. Tax-exempt bonds under Section 7871 are generally limited to financing essential governmental functions. Congress specified that these do not include functions customarily carried on by commercial enterprises. As a result, tribes have typically financed casino construction through taxable debt and private placements rather than tax-exempt tribal governmental bonds, while using tax-exempt financing for projects such as schools, clinics, roads and water systems that fit the governmental function test.

Gaming revenue and the broader tax picture

The tax treatment of casino revenue is separate from Section 7871. The Internal Revenue Service has long taken the position that a tribe is not itself a taxable entity on income from its governmental activities, and income earned by a tribal government, including gaming income, is not subject to federal income tax at the tribal level. Tribally chartered corporations organized under federal law may be treated differently from the tribe, which is one reason enterprise structure matters. We discuss that in our Section 17 corporation explainer.

Individual tribal members are a different story. Per capita payments distributed from gaming revenue are generally taxable income to the recipient under federal law, and IGRA requires tribes to adopt a revenue allocation plan approved by the Secretary of the Interior before distributing per capita payments. The tribe is expected to notify members of the tax liability. Casinos also carry reporting and withholding duties on certain player winnings, covered in our winnings and W-2G guide. For a fuller treatment, see the overview of how tribal gaming revenue is taxed.

Why the distinction matters for capital planning

Operators weighing a major expansion face a choice among funding sources: internal cash flow, bank loans, taxable bonds, and in some cases partnerships. Since tax-exempt governmental bonds are not generally available for the commercial portion of a casino resort, tribal borrowers price their debt closer to corporate credit. The market for that debt depends on investor comfort with sovereign immunity waivers, collateral structures and regulatory approvals. Our analysis of borrowing costs and bond market access examines how those factors play out.

Section 7871 does, however, support the non-gaming side of tribal government. Revenue from gaming funds tribal services under IGRA's permitted uses, including government operations, general welfare, economic development and support for local governments. When a tribe issues tax-exempt debt for a clinic or a water system, it is applying the governmental-function rules that Section 7871 sets out, even if casino revenue ultimately services the debt. Charitable giving by individuals and businesses to tribal programs can also qualify for deduction under the provision.

The Treasury Department has maintained a Tribal Advisory Committee on tax matters, and tax questions that affect tribal governments continue to arise in legislation and guidance. Readers tracking financial structure may also find the site's Legal Guide a useful reference for the underlying statutes.

Common misunderstandings

Three misunderstandings recur. The first is that Section 7871 is the source of the tax exemption for gaming income. It is not; the federal treatment of tribal governments as non-taxable on governmental income rests on longstanding principles, and Section 7871 addresses specific state-like treatment in defined areas. The second is that tax-exempt financing is routinely available for casinos. As noted, the governmental-function limits generally steer commercial gaming projects toward taxable markets.

The third is that tribal members are exempt from tax on distributions. Tribal governments are not taxed on the revenue, but members receiving per capita payments generally owe federal income tax on them, and tribal enterprises that pay wages withhold employment taxes in the ordinary way. State tax questions add another layer and depend on the tribe's location, compact and the status of the recipient.

Because tax rules change through legislation and guidance, tribal finance officers typically work with specialized counsel when structuring bond issues, charitable programs or distributions. The core principle remains useful: the tax code treats the tribal government as a government, and the questions that matter are which specific rule applies to which activity.
