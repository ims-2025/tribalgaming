The Lame-Duck Ledger: Which Tribal Bills Die When Congress Ends

A permitting package is advancing over tribal objections, while gaming and tax bills that never got a hearing head for the January reset.

By James KingfisherPublished November 12, 20265 min read

When the 119th Congress adjourns for the last time, every bill that has not been enacted expires with it. The new Congress convenes on January 3, 2027, and anything left unfinished must be reintroduced and begin the committee process again. For tribal governments, that deadline turns the current lame-duck session into a sorting exercise. A small number of measures could still move, while a longer list of bills affecting tribal gaming, taxation and land is likely to lapse without a hearing.

This analysis reviews the tribal-relevant legislation in play as Congress returns after the midterm elections, drawing on bill-status summaries published by the Cherokee One Feather on November 10 and on reporting about the Senate's permitting package. It does not revisit the prediction-market fight in detail; our post-midterm strategy analysis covers that front.

The permitting package: the bill that is moving

The measure with the most momentum is S. 5653, the Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act, a broad permitting package intended to speed federal reviews for pipelines, energy projects and data centers. On November 9 the Senate voted 73 to 25 to advance it, according to ICT, the same day roughly 800 people rallied at the Capitol against provisions that tribal organizations say weaken consultation.

Tribal objections center on Title III, which would change how federal agencies apply Section 106 of the National Historic Preservation Act. As ICT described it, the change would allow agencies to decline to consult tribes on some projects affecting cultural and religious sites, and the bill would also shorten review timelines and narrow tribes' avenues for legal challenge. National Congress of American Indians President Mark Macarro said in October that the proposed changes "violate treaty and trust obligations." Eleven tribal advocacy groups said revisions released November 8 addressed only a small number of their concerns.

The package is not a gaming bill, but its effects would reach tribal economies. Section 106 consultation and National Environmental Policy Act review are the processes tribes rely on when other parties' projects affect their homelands, and they are also processes tribes navigate when developing casino and resort projects that need federal approvals such as fee-to-trust acquisitions. Shorter timelines or tighter litigation rules could cut both ways for a tribe that is at once a project developer and a guardian of cultural sites. The federal approval framework for gaming projects is summarized in our Legal Guide.

Gaming and tax bills likely to expire

The Tribal Gaming Regulatory Compliance Act, S. 2564, sponsored by Sen. Martin Heinrich of New Mexico, would bring gaming by the Ysleta del Sur Pueblo and the Alabama-Coushatta Tribe of Texas under the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act rather than the 1987 Restoration Act that currently governs them. It was referred to the Senate Committee on Indian Affairs on July 31, 2025, and, according to the One Feather's November 10 summary, no hearing had been scheduled. We examined what the bill would mean for the two Texas tribes in our earlier coverage of S. 2564. With Texas lawmakers prefiling casino and sports betting resolutions for the 2027 session, the issue now has a parallel state track.

The Tribal Tax and Investment Reform Act, S. 2022, sponsored by Sens. Catherine Cortez Masto and Lisa Murkowski, would treat tribal governments in the same manner as state governments for certain federal tax purposes. It was referred to the Senate Finance Committee in June 2025 with no hearing scheduled. Tax parity has been a longstanding priority for tribal governments, whose gaming revenues fund public services, and the bill's expiration would send the issue back to the starting line.

H.R. 5257, introduced by Rep. Tom Cole of Oklahoma, would reaffirm the trust status of land taken into trust for federally recognized tribes. It was referred to the House Natural Resources Committee in September 2025 without a hearing. Because gaming eligibility under IGRA depends on the status of the land, measures that reinforce trust acquisitions are watched closely by tribes with pending or recently completed fee-to-trust decisions, including several of the projects tracked in our tribal casino directory.

The land bill closest to the finish

Not every measure is stalled. H.R. 226, the Eastern Band of Cherokee Historic Lands Reacquisition Act, would place 76 acres in eastern Tennessee into trust for the Eastern Band of Cherokee Indians. It passed the House by voice vote in February 2025 and received a Senate Committee on Indian Affairs hearing on September 23, 2026. It is a historic and cultural lands bill rather than a gaming measure, but it illustrates the path a narrow, broadly supported tribal bill can still take in a lame-duck session: committee action, followed by passage by unanimous consent or as part of a larger package.

What already failed

The session also carries the memory of a bill tribal gaming interests helped stop. In September the Senate fell short, 49 to 50, on a cloture vote on the revised CLARITY Act, the digital-asset market-structure bill that the Indian Gaming Association opposed over its potential effect on prediction markets and on the authority of the Commodity Futures Trading Commission. That bill is also subject to the end-of-Congress rule, though its sponsors could reintroduce it in the new Congress. We covered the revised text and the tribal response in our analysis of the CLARITY Act vote.

Expiration is a reset, not a verdict. Advocacy relationships, testimony and coalition work carry into the new Congress even when bill numbers do not.

What the reset means

For tribal governments, the expiration of bills is not purely a loss. Reintroduction allows sponsors to revise text, gather cosponsors and seek hearings under committee leadership that may change in the new Congress. The practical work between now and January is narrower: following the permitting package to its final form, securing land bills that are close to passage, and preparing reintroduction strategies for gaming and tax measures that never received a hearing.
