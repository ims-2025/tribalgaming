Connecticut Online Casino Tax Hits 20%: What the First Month Shows

Mohegan and Mashantucket Pequot iGaming crossed into the higher tax tier during a record month. The timing cushions the change, but it does not yet test it.

By Marcus WhitehorsePublished November 22, 20264 min read

Connecticut's online casino market posted its best month on record in October 2026, the first month in which the state's two tribal iGaming operators paid the higher 20% tax rate set by the 2021 agreements that legalized online gambling. According to market data compiled by Poker Industry PRO, online casino revenue reached $65.7 million, up 18% from October 2025 and well above the previous high of $59.7 million set in March 2026. Handle topped $2 billion for the first time.

The timing makes the month an early test of a bargain the Mohegan Tribe and the Mashantucket Pequot Tribal Nation struck with Gov. Ned Lamont in 2021: exclusive online casino rights for the tribes in exchange for a tax schedule that steps up after five years. The Connecticut online casino tax rate rose from 18% to 20% beginning with the October reporting period, and the first payments at the new rate were due November 15.

How the Connecticut online casino tax step-up works

Connecticut authorized online casino gaming and sports betting in 2021 through legislation and amendments to the tribes' gaming compacts, and online casino play went live on October 12, 2021. Under the framework, iGaming revenue is taxed at 18% for the first five years and at 20% for at least the following five. Sports wagering is taxed at 13.75%. The two tribes hold the state's only online casino licenses, operated through commercial partners: DraftKings with the Mashantucket Pequot Tribal Nation's Foxwoods Resort Casino, and FanDuel with the Mohegan Tribe's Mohegan Sun.

The two-point increase is modest in percentage terms but meaningful in dollars. At October's revenue level, the difference between 18% and 20% amounts to roughly $1.3 million a month in additional state collections, or on the order of $15 million a year if revenue holds near current levels. For context, operators paid the state about $108 million in online gaming payments in 2025, according to Department of Consumer Protection data.

Market share in October was almost evenly split. DraftKings and Foxwoods took 50.4% of revenue and FanDuel and Mohegan Sun took 49.6%, only the second time in the past 12 months that the DraftKings-Foxwoods platform led. That near-parity is a defining feature of the Connecticut model, which our five-year analysis of the tribal-exclusive iGaming model describes as one of the few closed online markets in the country.

What the first month at 20% does and does not show

A single record month does not settle whether the higher rate will change the economics for the tribes. Several factors make October an imperfect benchmark. Online casino revenue in most states has trended upward over time, and Connecticut's year-over-year growth of 18% is consistent with continued maturation rather than any effect of the tax change. Promotional spending, which operators can adjust in response to tax costs, is not visible in the topline figure. And because revenue is split nearly evenly between two operators with no new entrants possible, competitive pressure on margins works differently than in open markets such as Michigan.

The rate change shifts roughly two cents of every revenue dollar from the tribal operators and their partners to the state; the record month suggests the market can absorb it, but it does not yet show how.

What the month does show is that the step-up arrived during a period of strength rather than contraction. That matters for the tribes because their net share of online revenue is what remains after taxes and after the revenue split with their platform partners. A rising topline cushions the higher rate; a flat or declining market would have made the step-up more consequential.

It also matters for the policy argument in other states. Tribal-exclusive or tribal-led online gaming frameworks have been proposed or adopted in a handful of jurisdictions, and legislators frequently cite Connecticut's numbers when debating tax rates. A market that sets a record in its first month at 20% will be used by advocates of higher rates elsewhere, while tribal operators are likely to emphasize that Connecticut's rate was negotiated as part of a broader exchange that included exclusivity.

Online revenue in the context of Connecticut's tribal gaming economy

Online gaming has become a significant complement to the two tribes' land-based operations, which remain subject to the long-standing 25% contribution on slot revenue under the original compacts. That separate payment stream, and the municipal aid it funds, is examined in our report on the 25% slot revenue share and the municipal aid debate. Taken together, the slot contribution and the online gaming tax make the two tribes among the state's most significant sources of gaming revenue.

The broader structure of the market, including the tribes' resort operations, the Connecticut Lottery's role in sports betting and the competitive pressures from neighboring states, is laid out in our Connecticut tribal gaming deep-dive, and property information is available on the Connecticut state hub. Readers comparing tribal iGaming arrangements across states may also find our recent look at Michigan tribal online gaming payments useful, since Michigan's open market offers a sharp contrast with Connecticut's two-operator design.

The more telling data will come over the next several months. If revenue growth continues at a double-digit pace, the step-up to 20% will look like a manageable adjustment built into a deal both sides anticipated. If growth slows, the higher rate will absorb a larger share of operator margins, and the terms of Connecticut's tribal online casino bargain will draw closer attention as the compact amendments move through their second five-year period.
