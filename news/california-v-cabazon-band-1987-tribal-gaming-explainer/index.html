California v. Cabazon Band: The 1987 Ruling That Built Tribal Gaming

How a dispute over a high-stakes bingo parlor became the foundation of a multibillion-dollar industry and the law that regulates it.

By Daniel YazziePublished June 17, 20264 min read

Modern tribal gaming, a sector that now generates tens of billions of dollars a year, traces its legal foundation to a dispute over a bingo hall run by a tribe of a few dozen members. In California v. Cabazon Band of Mission Indians, decided by the U.S. Supreme Court in 1987, the justices held that California could not shut down high-stakes gaming on reservation land. The ruling reshaped federal Indian law and set in motion the framework that governs tribal casinos to this day.

The facts were modest. The Cabazon Band, along with the neighboring Morongo Band, operated a high-stakes bingo parlor and a card club open to the public, patronized largely by non-Indians visiting the reservations. California, which permitted some forms of gambling but capped bingo prizes and restricted card play, moved to apply its gaming laws to the tribal operations. The tribes resisted, arguing that the state had no authority to regulate activity on Indian land.

The criminal-versus-civil distinction

The case turned on a question of statutory interpretation under Public Law 280, a federal law that had granted certain states, including California, broad criminal jurisdiction over reservations. The state argued that its gambling rules fell within that grant. The Court disagreed, drawing a now-famous line between laws that are criminal and prohibitory and those that are civil and regulatory.

The logic ran like this: because California itself permitted gambling in various forms, from a state lottery to charitable bingo, its laws did not flatly prohibit gaming as a matter of public policy. Instead, they regulated how gaming could occur. Laws that merely regulate, the Court held, are civil in nature and fall outside the criminal jurisdiction Public Law 280 conferred. Since the state allowed gambling generally, it could not selectively enforce its regulatory limits against tribes on reservation land.

Because California permitted gambling in some forms, the Court held its rules were regulatory rather than prohibitory, and so could not be imposed on the reservations.

The Court also weighed the federal and tribal interests at stake, and found them substantial. It noted that the federal government had actively encouraged tribal self-determination and economic development, and that the gaming operations were a significant source of revenue and employment for the tribes and their members. Against those interests, California's asserted concern, that organized crime might infiltrate unregulated tribal gaming, was found insufficient to justify overriding tribal authority, particularly given the absence of evidence that such infiltration had occurred. This balancing of interests, weighing tribal self-government and federal policy against the state's regulatory aims, became a template courts would return to in later disputes over the reach of state law onto reservation land.

By a 6-3 margin, the justices upheld the tribes' right to conduct bingo and card games free of state interference, reaffirming the principle that tribal sovereignty shields reservation activity from state control absent a clear act of Congress. The decision did not invent tribal gaming, which already existed, but it removed the legal cloud over it and signaled that states could not unilaterally close it down.

From a court ruling to a federal statute

Cabazon created opportunity and uncertainty in equal measure. Tribes across the country saw a path to economic development that the highest court had effectively endorsed; states worried about gaming they could not regulate; and the absence of any federal regulatory structure left a vacuum. Congress responded the following year by passing the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act of 1988, the statute that still governs the field.

IGRA was a direct answer to the questions Cabazon raised. It created a national framework, established the National Indian Gaming Commission, and sorted gaming into classes, the distinctions at the heart of our explainer on Class II versus Class III. Crucially, it required tribes to negotiate compacts with states before offering the casino-style Class III gaming that defines the largest tribal resorts, a compromise between the sovereignty Cabazon affirmed and the regulatory role states sought. Our Legal Guide walks through how that structure operates today.

Why the case still matters

The compact requirement IGRA introduced proved to be its own source of conflict, culminating in the Supreme Court's 1996 decision in Seminole Tribe v. Florida, which limited how tribes could force reluctant states to the table, a story we cover in our explainer on Seminole Tribe v. Florida. The tension Cabazon exposed, between tribal authority and state interest, runs through nearly every gaming dispute since, from sports-betting exclusivity fights to debates over off-reservation facilities.

It is a striking lineage. A bingo parlor operated by a tribe of a few dozen members in the California desert produced a ruling that, together with the statute it prompted, underwrites an industry of hundreds of casinos operated by well over two hundred tribes. For readers exploring where it all took root, our California state hub maps the operators in the state where the case began. Cabazon endures not because of the size of what was at stake in 1987, but because of the principle it secured: that gaming on tribal land is, first and foremost, a matter of tribal sovereignty.
