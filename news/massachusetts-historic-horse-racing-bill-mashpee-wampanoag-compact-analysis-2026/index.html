Massachusetts Horse Racing Machines Test the Mashpee Wampanoag Compact

The House added historic horse racing terminals to an economic development bill; the state's only tribal casino says that is slot machine expansion by another name.

By Elena RuizPublished November 02, 20265 min read

A Massachusetts House plan to authorize historic horse racing machines at two former racetrack sites has become a test of how far a state can expand gambling outside a tribal-state compact before it provokes a sovereignty dispute. The Mashpee Wampanoag Tribe, the state's sole tribal gaming operator, argues that the Massachusetts historic horse racing proposal would introduce slot-like terminals without the licensing, suitability review and consultation that govern tribal and commercial casinos alike.

The provision has not been resolved as of the latest reporting available to us, and readers should check the current status of the legislation before relying on this analysis for any decision.

How the proposal reached the floor

On July 8, 2026, the Massachusetts House approved the historic horse racing language as one of several late amendments to a $561 million economic development bill, designated H.5562 in the tribe's statements. Reporting indicates the venues in view are Suffolk Downs in East Boston and the former Raynham Park in southeastern Massachusetts. Neither hosts live racing, though part of Suffolk Downs still offers simulcast wagering. Developer Richard Fields, who is pushing the legislation, has agreed to acquire Raynham Park if the bill is enacted, and a report he commissioned from Spectrum Gaming Group estimates about $225 million in combined capital investment for the two sites.

The bill then moved to the Senate, where the version of the economic development bill does not address historic horse racing, according to August reporting by the Taunton Daily Gazette. That split between chambers is why the outcome remains uncertain.

The tribe's case

In a July 10 statement, Mashpee Wampanoag Chairman Brian Weeden said the House advanced language that expands gambling without honoring the Commonwealth's commitments, and urged the Senate to remove it. Tribal counsel Rebekah Salguero said the language raises serious legal and policy concerns and should not be handled through a late amendment process.

The tribe's argument has a legal and a practical edge. Legally, it says the machines are electronic gaming terminals that resemble slot machines and use past horse races as the basis for wagers, and that the bill labels them pari-mutuel wagering to avoid casino-style regulation. It contends the authorization would sit outside both the tribal-state compact and the Massachusetts Expanded Gaming Act, and it points to licensing, suitability reviews, background checks and responsible gaming protections that would not apply in the same way. The tribe also cites its rights in Region C, the southeastern gaming region covered by its compact. The statement does not, on its face, claim a formal exclusivity clause, so the precise legal footing of any challenge would depend on the compact's text.

Practically, the concern is competition. Critics quoted in local coverage called the machines de facto slot machines and warned that they could harm the tribe's Taunton casino, where the city hopes to receive funding through a revenue share agreement. Our report on the First Light permanent resort covers the tribe's build-out in Taunton.

A familiar flashpoint, with local twists

Disputes over historic horse racing are not new. Our analysis of HHR machines and tribal exclusivity examines how the technology, which resolves wagers using a pool of previously run races, has been used in several states to add slot-like gaming at racetracks, often prompting objections from tribes and commercial casinos alike. In Massachusetts, the operators of Encore Boston Harbor, MGM Springfield and Plainridge Park urged House leaders to reject the amendment on similar grounds, so the tribe is not alone in opposition.

What distinguishes the tribal position is the government-to-government dimension. The tribe's statement framed the issue as a matter of tribal sovereignty and government-to-government respect. Whether or not a court would find a compact breach, the framing signals that the tribe sees consultation, not just competition, as at stake. For a primer on how exclusivity provisions typically work and what remedies they may carry, see our explainer on how tribal gaming exclusivity works.

The timing also reflects how the bill traveled. Because the language was attached late to a broad economic development measure, it was debated with limited standalone hearing time, which is the process concern the tribe's counsel raised. Supporters of the machines, for their part, point to the capital investment projected for the two sites and to the jobs that development could bring, arguments that carry weight in an economic development bill. Those competing claims are likely to frame any Senate negotiation.

What happens next

Three paths are plausible. The Senate could strip the language, leaving the status quo. The chambers could reconcile with some version of the authorization, perhaps with a revenue or regulatory structure meant to answer objections. Or the language could be enacted and then challenged, either politically or in court, by the tribe or others. Which path unfolds will turn on legislative priorities in a bill that carries many other economic development provisions.

For tribes elsewhere, the episode is a reminder that exclusivity is often defended in the legislature as much as in the courthouse. Compact terms matter, but so does the political process through which gambling expansion rides along with unrelated bills.
