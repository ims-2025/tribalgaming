Oklahoma Tribal Gaming Exclusivity Fees Hit Record $238.24M in FY2026

State compliance data show the model compact paying more than ever, with four nations carrying three-quarters of the load.

By Marcus WhitehorsePublished November 23, 20264 min read

Oklahoma tribal gaming exclusivity fees reached an all-time high of $238.24 million in fiscal year 2026, according to monthly receipts published by the state's Office of Management and Enterprise Services (OMES) and summarized in the state's FY2026 gaming compliance reporting. The total, covering July 2025 through June 2026, is roughly $17.1 million, or 7.8 percent, above the $221.08 million the state collected in FY2025, a year that had itself been a record.

The payments are made by the tribes that operate under Oklahoma's model tribal-state compact, the framework voters approved through State Question 712 in 2004. In exchange for a share of revenue, compacted tribes hold exclusive rights to Class III electronic games and covered table games in the state. Our Oklahoma state hub lists the operators and properties behind those numbers.

Where the Oklahoma exclusivity fees go

State reporting shows that the Education Reform Revolving Fund, commonly called the 1017 Fund, received $209.4 million in FY2026, and the General Revenue Fund received $28.6 million. The statutory formula sends the first $250,000 each year to the Department of Mental Health and Substance Abuse Services for gambling-addiction programs, then splits the remainder 88 percent to the 1017 Fund and 12 percent to general revenue.

That allocation is why the fee totals draw attention at the Capitol every budget cycle. The 1017 Fund supports common education, so growth in tribal gaming translates directly into dollars for school budgets. We examined that pipeline in detail in our earlier report on how exclusivity fees feed the 1017 education fund.

The fee schedule itself has not changed since the model compact took effect. Tribes pay 4 percent of the first $10 million of annual adjusted gross revenue from electronic covered games, 5 percent of the next $10 million, and 6 percent above $20 million. Table games are assessed at 10 percent of monthly net win. Because the rate climbs with revenue, larger operators pay a higher effective rate than smaller ones, which magnifies the concentration visible in the data.

A market built on a handful of large operators

According to figures compiled by the Oklahoma Policy Institute from state compliance reporting, 34 tribes operated an average of about 49,485 Class III machines across 138 facilities in FY2026, generating roughly $3.93 billion in Class III and table-game revenue. Four nations accounted for about three-quarters of the fees: the Chickasaw Nation paid approximately $87 million, the Choctaw Nation about $53.2 million, the Cherokee Nation about $22 million, and the Muscogee (Creek) Nation about $15.7 million. Thirteen tribes paid less than $1 million each.

The record total reflects growth at the top of the market at least as much as breadth across it. Thirteen of the 34 compacted tribes paid under $1 million each.

That two-tier structure is a familiar feature of Oklahoma's market, which we described in our deep dive on the model compact and its operators. Large destination resorts along the Texas border and the Interstate 35 and 44 corridors capture regional and out-of-state traffic, while smaller tribes operate travel-center casinos and rural properties with far narrower catchments. A higher statewide total therefore does not mean every compacted tribe had a stronger year.

What the early FY2027 figures show

OMES has also begun posting receipts for FY2027. The first three months of the fiscal year show $20.38 million in July, $19.82 million in August and $23.01 million in September 2026, or about $63.2 million for the quarter. Monthly receipts fluctuate with the calendar and with the timing of tribal remittances, so a single quarter is an imperfect guide, but the early pace is consistent with a market that remains near its recent highs.

The figures arrive during an unusually busy period for Oklahoma gaming policy. The state's sports betting debate is expected to resume in the 2027 legislative session, the long-running dispute over the compacts Gov. Kevin Stitt signed with several tribes in 2020 has continued in court, and tribal leaders have raised concerns about prediction-market platforms offering sports event contracts to Oklahoma residents. None of those issues touches the fee formula directly. Sports wagering is not a covered game under the model compact, and any addition would require new authorization and compact supplements.

What the FY2026 total does provide is a baseline. Lawmakers who want to estimate the value of a sports betting framework, and tribes weighing what they would seek in exchange for adding it, now have a record year of land-based exclusivity payments to measure against. For context on how Oklahoma's revenue-sharing compares with other states, see our state comparison tools and our Legal Guide to IGRA and Class III compacts.

Reading the numbers carefully

Two caveats apply. First, exclusivity fees are calculated on covered games only, so they understate total tribal gaming activity in Oklahoma, which also includes Class II games that are not subject to state fees. Second, the fee figures measure what the state receives, not what tribes retain; IGRA requires that net gaming revenue fund tribal government operations, member services and economic development, and those uses are set by each tribe rather than the state.

Even with those limits, the trend is clear. Oklahoma's model compact, now more than two decades old and automatically renewed through 2035 under a 2020 federal court ruling, continues to deliver rising payments to the state, and the bulk of that growth continues to come from the state's largest tribal operators.
