Drop, Count and Cage: How a Tribal Casino Accounts for Every Dollar

Dual custody, separated duties and independent verification. The redundancy is the control.

By Linda CardinalPublished September 17, 20264 min read

Every dollar that moves through a tribal casino passes through a controlled chain of custody before it appears in a revenue report. That chain — from the bill validator on a slot machine to the count room to the vault to the financial statement — is governed by minimum internal control standards, documented in written procedures, and verified by independent auditors. Understanding drop and count operations is the most direct way to understand how tribal gaming regulation actually functions day to day, because this is where regulatory theory meets physical cash.

What "drop" means

The drop is the removal of cash and cash equivalents from gaming devices and tables. On a slot machine, currency inserted into the bill validator collects in a locked stacker inside a secured cabinet. On a table game, cash exchanged for chips is pushed through a slot in the table into a locked drop box beneath it. Neither container is accessible to the employees working the floor.

Drop occurs on a published schedule — daily for most slot floors, per shift or per day for tables, depending on volume and the property's approved internal controls. A drop team removes the full stackers and boxes and replaces them with empty ones, moving the contents to the count room under continuous surveillance coverage and, at most properties, with a member of the tribal gaming commission's regulatory staff observing or on call.

The critical control is that the drop team never counts. Its job is transport under observation. Separation of the people who move money from the people who count it is the foundational principle of the entire system.

Inside the count room

The count room is among the most tightly controlled spaces in any casino. It has dedicated surveillance coverage with no blind spots, controlled access requiring multiple authorized persons, and typically a requirement that the room cannot be occupied by fewer than three employees while a count is in progress. Employees frequently wear pocketless uniforms. Entry and exit are logged.

The soft count handles currency from bill validators and table drop boxes. Money is counted by machine, verified, and reconciled against the meter readings the slot system reports for each machine. A variance between the physical count and the system meter triggers documented investigation — not at the end of the month, but as part of closing the count. The hard count, where coin-operated machines remain, handles coin; on a modern ticket-in ticket-out floor it has largely disappeared.

Count results are recorded on documents signed by every member of the count team. Those documents feed the daily revenue report, which is the basis for every downstream number: gross gaming revenue, the win figures reported to the tribal gaming regulatory authority, revenue-sharing calculations under a compact, and the annual financial statements. Our explainer on handle, coin-in and hold covers how those figures are derived and what they measure.

The count room is where a casino's reported revenue is created. Every control in the building exists to make that number verifiable.

The cage and the vault

Counted funds move to the vault, which functions as the casino's central bank. From there the main cage — the customer-facing window where patrons redeem tickets, cash checks and obtain chips — draws its working bankroll. Cage operations run on an imprest system: each cashier begins a shift with a fixed, counted bankroll and must end the shift able to account for every dollar, with all transactions documented.

The cage is also where federal anti-money-laundering obligations become operational. Tribal casinos are financial institutions under the Bank Secrecy Act, and cage staff are responsible for identifying reportable currency transactions, aggregating them correctly across a gaming day, and escalating activity that warrants a suspicious activity report. That compliance function sits on top of the accounting function and is audited separately.

Who verifies all of this

Three layers of oversight apply. The tribal gaming regulatory authority — the tribe's own regulator, independent of the gaming enterprise — writes and enforces the property's internal controls, staffs regulatory positions on the floor and in surveillance, and investigates variances. Its structure and authority are described in our explainer on tribal gaming commissions and minimum internal control standards.

Above that sits the annual independent audit required of every tribal gaming operation, conducted by a licensed CPA firm and submitted to the National Indian Gaming Commission. Auditors test the drop and count process directly — observing counts, tracing documents, and testing whether documented procedures were actually followed. The requirements are set out in our explainer on the annual audit under 25 C.F.R. Part 571.

The third layer is the compact. Where a tribal-state agreement provides for revenue sharing, the state has a direct financial interest in the accuracy of the win figure, and compacts typically grant audit rights or require submission of audited statements. That provision is why drop and count accuracy is not only an internal matter but a term of a government-to-government agreement — a point developed further in our legal guide to compacts and regulation.

Why the process is built this way

The design assumes that no individual should ever be in a position to divert funds without a second person noticing and a record existing. Dual custody, separation of duties, continuous surveillance, signed documentation and independent verification are redundant by intent. The redundancy is the control.

For tribal governments, the stakes are higher than in commercial gaming. Gaming revenue funds health clinics, schools, elder services and public safety. A control failure is not only a financial loss; it is a loss of governmental capacity, and it damages the regulatory credibility that tribal sovereignty in this industry rests on. That is the reason tribal regulators tend to enforce count-room procedure with a rigor that surprises people encountering it for the first time.
