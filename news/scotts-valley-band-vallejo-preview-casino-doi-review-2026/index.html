Scotts Valley Band Opens Vallejo Preview Casino Amid Federal Review

A modular interim hall lets the Pomo tribe begin operations while Interior reweighs the trust and gaming determination for its Solano County site.

By Sarah BegayPublished June 13, 20265 min read

The Scotts Valley Band of Pomo Indians is preparing to open a temporary preview casino in Vallejo, a modular interim facility that would let the tribe begin gaming operations even as the U.S. Department of the Interior reconsiders whether the tribe's Solano County parcel is eligible for gaming under the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act. The move is the latest turn in a years-long effort by the landless Pomo band to anchor a permanent home and a roughly $700 million resort near the junction of Interstate 80 and Highway 37.

Vallejo officials have approved a municipal services agreement tied to the interim facility. Under the terms reported by the city, the tribe would pay Vallejo about $402,000 a year to offset public-safety and administrative costs, a one-time activation fee of roughly $100,000, and at least $100,000 a year directed to local nonprofits. The agreement is structured to give the city revenue and oversight commitments while the underlying federal questions remain unresolved.

How the Vallejo project reached legal limbo

Interior placed the Vallejo parcel into trust in January 2025 and, in the same decision package, found the land eligible for gaming. Less than three months later the department reversed course, saying it needed to weigh additional evidence submitted by neighboring tribes that had objected to the determination. That whiplash set off litigation, and in October 2025 U.S. District Judge Trevor McFadden ruled that Interior had violated the Scotts Valley Band's due-process rights when it rescinded the gaming determination without adequate notice. The judge declined to simply reinstate the approval, however, allowing the department to continue its reconsideration so long as it gave the tribe proper notice and an opportunity to respond.

The result is a project caught between a favorable procedural ruling and an unfinished administrative review. The tribe holds trust land, but the gaming eligibility that makes that land economically meaningful is once again under departmental scrutiny. For a band that has spent decades without a reservation land base, the interim hall represents a way to begin generating revenue and demonstrating operational capacity while the paperwork is litigated and re-litigated. Why the eligibility question matters

The stakes turn on the structure of IGRA itself. The statute generally bars gaming on lands acquired in trust after October 17, 1988, unless a project fits one of several narrow exceptions, including the "restored lands" pathway that landless and recently restored tribes often invoke. Whether the Vallejo site qualifies is precisely the kind of determination Interior is now revisiting, and it is the hinge on which the entire development swings. A preview casino lets a tribe begin operations and build a record of compliance, but it does not resolve the federal eligibility question that determines whether a permanent resort can ever be built.

Opposition from nearby tribes is a recurring feature of off-reservation proposals, and it is a major reason Interior decisions in this space are so frequently contested. Competing operators argue that new projects divert patrons and dilute exclusivity, while project sponsors counter that landless tribes deserve a viable economic base. The Vallejo dispute sits squarely within that tension, and its outcome will be watched closely across California's crowded tribal gaming market, where dozens of tribes operate within driving distance of one another.

What comes next

For now, the preview casino allows the Scotts Valley Band to move from litigation to operation, at least provisionally. The permanent resort remains contingent on Interior completing its reconsideration and reaffirming gaming eligibility, a process that could stretch well into the year and may itself invite further legal challenge. The municipal agreement with Vallejo gives the city a financial stake in the project's success and a framework for managing traffic, policing, and community impacts in the meantime.

Context matters here. The Scotts Valley Band is a federally recognized tribe that was terminated in the 1960s and later restored, leaving it without a meaningful reservation land base for decades. That history is central to its legal claim, because the restored-lands pathway under IGRA was written precisely for tribes in this position. It also explains the urgency: unlike established operators with settled reservations, a landless tribe has no fallback site on which to build, so the Vallejo parcel carries the weight of the band's entire economic strategy.

The episode underscores how fragile off-reservation gaming approvals have become. A favorable court ruling on due process did not deliver certainty; it delivered a remand. Tribes pursuing trust acquisitions for gaming increasingly face a landscape in which an initial approval can be revisited, vacated, or reconsidered, and in which the difference between a thriving resort and a stalled parcel can hinge on an administrative finding rather than a courtroom verdict. The Scotts Valley Band's interim hall is a pragmatic response to that uncertainty: begin where you can, and keep building the legal record for the larger project still to come.
