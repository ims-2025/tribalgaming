Jamul Casino Resort's Hotel Tower Remakes San Diego Tribal Gaming

A tribe with one of California's smallest usable land bases answered the constraint by building upward.

By Sarah BegayPublished August 25, 20265 min read

The Jamul Indian Village of California has completed the hotel tower that converts its San Diego County property from a casino into a full destination resort, closing out a development arc that began when the tribe opened a gaming floor on a small trust parcel roughly 20 miles east of downtown San Diego. The rebranded Jamul Casino Resort now pairs its existing multi-level gaming floor with a 16-story hotel of roughly 200 rooms, including a block of suites, a rooftop pool deck, a full-service spa and salon, and additional food-and-beverage and retail space.

The project matters less for its room count than for what it represents. Jamul Indian Village is among the smallest federally recognized tribes operating a Class III casino in California, and its reservation footprint is famously constrained — a compact hillside parcel that pushed the original casino into a vertical, structurally complex design. Building a 16-story tower on that same footprint is an engineering answer to a land-base problem shared by a meaningful share of the state's gaming tribes.

Why the hotel tower changes Jamul's economics

A day-trip casino and a resort casino are different businesses. Without rooms, a property's revenue is effectively capped by drive-time radius and by the length of an average visit: guests arrive after work, play for a few hours, and leave. Adding hotel inventory extends dwell time, lifts theoretical win per visit, and opens the property to group, convention, and leisure segments that were previously unreachable. It also hands the marketing team a non-cash comp currency, letting the property reward play with room nights rather than free play — a meaningful margin protection.

That shift is the single most common capital strategy in tribal gaming right now. Industry tracking for 2026 anticipated roughly six new tribal casino hotels and eight hotel expansions coming online across Indian Country, adding on the order of 1,500 rooms at a combined project cost exceeding $1 billion. The Jamul Casino Resort tower is one of the more architecturally distinctive entries in that cohort, and it lands in San Diego County, among the most competitive tribal gaming clusters in the United States.

The county hosts a dense set of tribal properties, several of which already operate large hotel inventories. Jamul's development corporation has positioned the tower toward the boutique and upper-upscale end rather than trying to out-scale its neighbors on room count, targeting a AAA Four Diamond designation and leaning on proximity to the city as its differentiator. A small land base, a vertical solution

Jamul Indian Village's path to gaming was contested for years. The tribe's trust land is modest, hemmed in by terrain and by a two-lane state route, and the original casino project drew sustained opposition from neighboring residents alongside litigation over traffic, environmental review, and the gaming eligibility of the parcel itself. The property ultimately opened in 2016 after the tribe and its development partners worked through the federal and state approval process.

The result reads more like an urban casino than a resort on open reservation land: parking structures rather than surface lots, a stacked gaming floor, and now a tower rather than a low-rise wing. That template — build up, not out — is increasingly relevant for tribes whose restored or newly acquired parcels are measured in acres rather than square miles. It is also expensive. Vertical construction carries a materially higher cost per key than the horizontal hotel wings common at Oklahoma and Washington properties, which means the underwriting depends on a higher average daily rate and stronger midweek occupancy than a rural property would need to clear.

The economics of a small-parcel tribal resort are unforgiving: with no room to expand outward, every additional dollar of revenue has to come from higher yield per square foot rather than more square footage.

Jobs, diversification, and the sovereignty argument

The tribe has said the expanded resort supports more than 1,400 permanent jobs across the region — a figure covering hotel operations, food and beverage, spa, facilities, and the security and surveillance staffing a resort footprint requires. For a tribe with a small enrolled citizenship, gaming employment is overwhelmingly non-tribal, which is typical across Indian Country and helps explain why tribal gaming's regional economic footprint consistently exceeds what enrollment figures alone would suggest.

Under the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act, net gaming revenue must be directed to a defined set of purposes: funding tribal government operations, providing for the general welfare of the tribe and its members, promoting tribal economic development, donating to charitable organizations, and helping fund operations of local government agencies. Tribes that reach the resort stage typically use incremental non-gaming revenue to reduce dependence on the slot floor itself — the same logic driving the broader non-gaming diversification push across the industry. What to watch next

Three questions will determine whether the tower pencils out. First, midweek occupancy: boutique properties near major metros often fill on weekends and struggle Tuesday through Thursday, and the answer usually lies in group business the property has not historically pursued. Second, rate integrity — whether the hotel is sold as a revenue center in its own right or discounted into a comp vehicle for the casino floor. Third, whether the added amenity base actually lengthens visits or merely redistributes existing local play across more outlets.

None of those questions is unique to Jamul. They apply to every property in the current tribal hotel tower wave, and the answers will surface in operating results long before they surface in press releases. What is already clear is that a tribe with one of the smallest usable land bases in California gaming has built one of its taller buildings — and that the constraint, rather than defeating the project, defined it.
