When Voters Decide: Ballot Measures Are Reshaping Tribal Gaming Policy

Compacting happens in a negotiating room. Increasingly, the decision that matters most happens at the ballot box — under rules that favor neither side by default.

By James KingfisherPublished September 21, 20267 min read

Tribal gaming policy is usually made in two places: a negotiating room where a tribe and a governor's office work through a compact, and a federal agency that reviews the result. Increasingly, it is being made in a third — the ballot box. Ballot measures have become a recurring instrument for authorizing, restricting and occasionally reversing tribal gaming rights, and the pattern deserves more attention than it typically receives, because the ballot operates under rules that differ fundamentally from both compacting and litigation.

Three distinct categories of ballot measure now touch Indian gaming, and conflating them produces bad analysis. The first authorizes tribal gaming through state constitutional change. The second restricts or revokes it. The third takes place entirely within a tribal nation, where citizens vote on whether their own government should enter gaming at all.

Authorization: The California Template

California established the model. After years of disputes over what the state's constitution permitted, voters approved Proposition 5 in 1998, a statutory initiative authorizing tribal Class III gaming. The California Supreme Court struck it down the following year as inconsistent with the state constitution's prohibition on Nevada-style casinos. Tribes returned in March 2000 with Proposition 1A — a constitutional amendment rather than a statute — which passed and supplied the durable authorization that underpins the state's compacting framework to this day.

The lesson embedded in that sequence still governs: in states where gaming limits are constitutional rather than statutory, only a constitutional amendment produces a settled authorization. A statutory initiative in the same posture is an expensive way to generate litigation. The same structural question is live in North Dakota, where the constitutional route has been discussed as the path for any expansion of online sports betting, and it shapes the strategic calculus in several other states. The California state hub tracks how the post-Proposition 1A framework developed.

Restriction: The Arkansas Case

The reverse use is newer and, for tribal enterprises, more unsettling. In November 2024, Arkansas voters approved Issue 2, enacted as Amendment 104, which repealed the state's authority to issue a casino license in Pope County and revoked the license the Arkansas Racing Commission had already awarded to Cherokee Nation Entertainment months earlier. The tribe's constitutional challenge was dismissed in full by a federal district court in August 2025.

Amendment 104 also added a prospective requirement: countywide voter approval for any future casino license. That provision is the more consequential half of the measure. It converts every future licensing decision in the state into a local election, which is a different kind of project risk than a regulatory approval — slower, more expensive to contest, and far more responsive to organized opposition spending. Several states have local-option requirements of this kind; what Arkansas demonstrated is that one can be installed retroactively against a license already issued.

The ballot's defining feature, from a project-risk standpoint, is that it is not subject to the constraints that discipline legislatures and agencies. There is no negotiation, no administrative record, and no duty to consult.

That is precisely why the instrument is attractive to whichever side is out of power in a given state. A tribe facing a legislature that will not move a sports betting bill can go to the voters. A commercial operator facing a tribal exclusivity regime it cannot dislodge in the capitol can do the same. California's November 2022 cycle produced both at once — Proposition 26, the tribal-backed retail sports betting measure, and Proposition 27, the commercial online measure — and voters defeated both decisively, in a campaign that set national records for initiative spending.

Tribal Referenda: The Category That Gets Overlooked

The third category involves no state electorate at all. In June 2026, citizens of the Lumbee Tribe of North Carolina rejected a proposed amendment to the tribal constitution that would have opened a path to gaming — a decision made entirely within the tribe's own governing process, and one that industry coverage frequently misfiles as a state-level development. It is not. It is a sovereign nation's electorate deciding what its government may do, which is the most direct expression of self-determination in the entire sequence. Our coverage of the Lumbee gaming amendment vote details how that process unfolded.

Tribal referenda carry their own dynamics. Turnout is smaller and the electorate is directly affected by the outcome, which tends to surface concerns — per-capita distribution, land use, cultural objections, the governance capacity required to run a gaming enterprise — that rarely appear in a statewide campaign. They are also, unlike state measures, entirely insulated from outside campaign money in any formal sense.

What to Watch

The most consequential near-term test is Oklahoma, where sports betting is headed toward a November 2026 ballot question following the defeat of HB 1047 in the state Senate. Oklahoma's tribal gaming market is the largest and most mature in the country by number of operators, and its compact framework is the product of a 2004 ballot measure — meaning the state has already run this play once, successfully, from the tribal side. Our analysis of the Oklahoma ballot pivot examines the exclusivity questions at stake.

For tribal gaming leadership, the practical takeaway is that ballot risk should be underwritten as a distinct category, separate from legislative and regulatory risk, with its own timeline and its own cost estimate. A campaign is not a lobbying budget. It is a defined, expensive, publicly contested event whose outcome is difficult to reverse and largely immune to the legal doctrines that protect tribal interests elsewhere. The Legal Guide covers how those doctrines operate in the compact context — and, by implication, where they stop.
