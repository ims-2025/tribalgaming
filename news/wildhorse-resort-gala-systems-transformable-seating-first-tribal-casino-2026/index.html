Wildhorse Resort Adds Transformable Seating, a First for a Tribal Casino

The Umatilla tribes' resort is installing a reconfigurable seating system in its new event hall, a signal of how far tribal casinos are moving into live entertainment.

By Daniel YazziePublished October 31, 20265 min read

Wildhorse Resort & Casino, owned and operated by the Confederated Tribes of the Umatilla Indian Reservation near Pendleton, Oregon, has announced a partnership to bring transformable seating to its new multi-purpose event hall. According to reporting by the East Oregonian on October 23, the resort will become the first tribal casino to use the technology, and one of three venues in the United States with it.

What Wildhorse is installing

The partnership with Gala Systems, a Canadian manufacturer, was announced on October 15. The company will supply its Gala Venue transformable seating system, which uses Spiralift technology in place of hydraulics. The East Oregonian reports 38 rows of seating that can be arranged in at least seven configurations, and says a change can be made in as little as 15 minutes. The center of the hall remains fixed to meet federal accessibility standards, and doors lock during configuration changes. Total seating capacity was not stated in the coverage, and we have not independently confirmed it.

The seating is part of the resort's broader $100 million expansion, which also includes a new 10-story hotel tower, additional dining, and rentable meeting spaces. The event hall is described as both a convention center and a concert hall, and the project is scheduled for completion in April 2027. Wildhorse CEO Gary E. George is quoted in the coverage on the venue's role in the resort's plans. We covered the larger project earlier in our report on the Wildhorse $100 million expansion and its entertainment partnership.

Why a flexible hall matters for a remote resort

Wildhorse sits roughly three hours from the nearest metropolitan area, and the resort's destination concept dates to 1993. For a property in that position, the economics of live entertainment are different from those of an urban casino. A concert in a big city draws from a deep local audience, while a rural venue has to draw from a thinner population or persuade visitors to travel. A hall that can host a concert one night, a banquet or conference the next, and a sporting or community event after that spreads fixed costs across many types of bookings and reduces the number of nights the room sits dark.

The East Oregonian reports that the resort projects a 400 percent annual increase in live entertainment and performance options. Projections of that kind should be read as targets rather than outcomes, but the direction is consistent with an industry-wide shift. As we described in our analysis of the tribal casino entertainment venue buildout, operators increasingly treat entertainment as a driver of hotel stays, dining, and gaming visits rather than as a stand-alone profit center.

Meetings, groups, and the rest of the property

The same flexibility serves the meetings business. Reconfigurable seating allows a room to move between theater-style, banquet, and arena-style layouts, which is useful for regional associations, tribal gatherings, and corporate events. Our coverage of convention and meeting space as a group-business strategy explains why many resorts see that segment as a way to fill midweek rooms when leisure demand is softer. A new hotel tower paired with a flexible event hall is a coherent package: the tower supplies rooms, and the hall supplies reasons to book them.

Wildhorse also illustrates the proportion that tribal resorts are now placing on non-gaming investment. The ratio of hotel rooms to slot machines in expansions like this one has become a recurring analytical question, which we examined in our piece on hotel-to-slot ratios in tribal resort expansions. The Umatilla project is a useful example of an operator building amenities that draw visitors for reasons other than gaming alone.

What to watch through 2027

Several details will determine whether the investment pays off. The first is scheduling: a venue with seven configurations only creates value if the resort can book it consistently, which depends on talent buyers, event promoters, and local demand. The second is cost control. Specialized seating systems carry installation and maintenance costs, and a venue that changes layouts frequently needs staff trained to do so safely. The third is the opening timeline itself. Construction schedules at tribal resorts are affected by labor, materials, and financing, as we described in our explainer on why opening dates slip.

It is also worth being precise about the novelty claim. The East Oregonian reports that Wildhorse is the first tribal casino with this specific technology and one of three U.S. venues overall. That is a narrow distinction about one seating system, not a statement that other tribal casinos lack flexible event spaces. Many already operate multipurpose rooms, and several are building new ones.

For the Umatilla tribes, the broader significance is that gaming revenue is funding infrastructure that serves the regional economy and not only the casino floor. A completed hall would give the Pendleton area a venue it does not currently have at that scale, and an April 2027 opening would place it in the 2027 wave of tribal openings.
