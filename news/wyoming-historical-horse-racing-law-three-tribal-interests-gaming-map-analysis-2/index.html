Wyoming Historical Horse Racing Law and the Three-Tribe Gaming Map

Reservation casinos under IGRA and a state-licensed racing network under Chickasaw ownership show how one small market hosts two legal regimes.

By Daniel YazziePublished October 30, 20264 min read

Wyoming has one of the smallest tribal gaming footprints in the country, yet its gaming economy now involves three different tribal interests operating under two different legal regimes. The recent acquisition of a Wyoming horse racing network by the Chickasaw Nation's Global Gaming Solutions makes that Wyoming historical horse racing law and tribal presence worth a closer look. Understanding the state's rules explains why a tribe based in Oklahoma can own gaming assets in Wyoming without a compact, and why the two resident tribes operate under entirely different terms.

Two regimes, side by side

The first regime is federal. The Northern Arapaho Tribe and the Eastern Shoshone Tribe share the Wind River Indian Reservation near Riverton and operate casinos there under the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act, with the oversight structure that implies. Those casinos sit on sovereign tribal land, and the games they offer are defined by IGRA classification and their governing agreements. Our Wyoming market deep dive describes that side of the market.

The second regime is the state's. Wyoming authorizes pari-mutuel wagering on horse racing, and in recent years it has permitted historical horse racing machines. These machines look like slot machines but are built on pools of previously run races. Under Wyoming law the machines are tied to live horse racing: an operator must maintain a live track to operate them. A state gaming official recently described that as the very purpose for which the machines were authorized. Facilities of this kind sit on state land and fall under state rules, administered by the Wyoming Gaming Commission, which approved the Chickasaw transaction at a special meeting in mid-October.

Why the tie to live racing matters

Linking machines to live racing does three things. It gives lawmakers a rationale for limiting machine gaming, because the product is formally a racing product. It creates a continuing obligation for operators to fund a track and a racing calendar, which supports an agricultural and sporting tradition. And it makes the track itself a regulated asset: the live-event and simulcast permits that the commission approved in the transfer are the permissions on which the machine business depends.

That last point deserves attention. When a buyer acquires a Wyoming operation, the valuable thing is not only the real estate or the machines but the continued right to run live racing, since without it the machine floor cannot lawfully operate. The racing at Sweetwater Downs in Rock Springs is therefore central to the business rather than a side attraction. Reporting on the deal indicates that more than 1,500 historical horse racing machines and about 13 Horse Palace locations came with it.

In Wyoming, the racetrack is the license. The machines follow the racing, not the other way around.

Why tribes view historical horse racing warily elsewhere

In states with Class III compacts that grant tribes exclusivity, historical horse racing can be contentious, because tribal operators argue that machines resembling slots erode the exclusivity for which they pay. We explored that dynamic in our analysis of historical horse racing as a tribal exclusivity flashpoint and in our report on California's Santa Anita debate. Wyoming differs in that its tribal casinos are limited in number and location, and the state has no broad exclusivity regime comparable to those in California or Oklahoma.

The irony of the Chickasaw purchase is that it puts a tribal owner on the other side of that debate. A tribe that depends on exclusivity at home is now running machine-style gaming under state authority elsewhere. There is no inconsistency in law, since the two activities occur in different jurisdictions, but the arrangement illustrates how tribal enterprises balance positions as sovereigns and as commercial investors. For background on the distinction between Indian-lands gaming and state-licensed activity, see our Legal Guide.

The competitive geography

Geography shapes the economics. The Horse Palace Swan Ranch facility in Cheyenne, a roughly $25 million, 30,000-square-foot property that opened in June 2024, sits along southbound Interstate 25 about four miles from the Colorado border. Evanston, home to another Horse Palace that opened in February 2026, lies in the far southwest corner of the state. Both draw on customers from neighboring states, and neither competes directly with the Wind River casinos, which are roughly in the central part of the state near Riverton. Our interstate corridor analysis looks at similar border-driven strategies elsewhere.

The separation between markets may be what makes the arrangement stable. The resident tribes serve the reservation area and its surroundings, while the racing network serves border and highway traffic. That does not guarantee future harmony, particularly if the new owner expands or if Wyoming lawmakers revisit the machine framework, but it does mean the three tribal interests are not currently in direct conflict.

What could change

Three variables bear watching. Legislative changes to the machine authorization, including limits on machine counts or locations, would alter the economics for any operator. Changes in Colorado's gaming environment could affect Cheyenne's cross-border draw. And the new owner's investment decisions, including whether it adds locations or upgrades the track, will signal how much capital a tribal owner is willing to commit to a state-regulated product in a small market.

For now, Wyoming offers a case study in how a tribal presence in gaming can mean very different things depending on where the activity sits. The reservation casinos exist because of sovereignty and federal law, while the racing network exists because the state licensed it. A Chickasaw owner of the latter is a reminder that tribal gaming is not a single legal category but a set of overlapping ones.
