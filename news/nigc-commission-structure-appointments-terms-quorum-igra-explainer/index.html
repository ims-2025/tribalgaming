How the NIGC Is Built: Commission Appointments, Terms and Quorum Under IGRA

The federal regulator of Indian gaming is a three-member body whose composition is set by statute. Here is how it is meant to work and why the details matter.

By Daniel YazziePublished October 20, 20266 min read

The National Indian Gaming Commission is the federal agency charged with overseeing gaming on Indian lands, but its design is less well known than its enforcement actions. Congress created the agency in the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act of 1988 and wrote the structure of its governing commission into the statute. Knowing how that commission is assembled helps explain how the agency makes decisions, and why periods of vacancy attract so much attention across Indian Country.

A three-member commission

IGRA establishes the NIGC as an independent agency within the Department of the Interior. It is governed by a commission of three full-time members: a Chairman and two associate commissioners. The Chairman is appointed by the President with the advice and consent of the Senate. The two associate commissioners are appointed by the Secretary of the Interior, and the statute requires that the Secretary consult with Indian tribes before making those appointments.

The statute also sets eligibility rules. Two of the three commissioners must be enrolled members of an Indian tribe, and no more than two members may be of the same political party. The Chairman serves a term of three years, and the associate commissioners serve staggered three-year terms, a design intended to provide continuity as individual terms expire. Commissioners are required to be persons of good character and cannot hold financial interests in gaming activities during their service, constraints that reflect Congress's concern about integrity in the regulation of a cash-intensive industry.

The Commission also elects a Vice Chairman, who can act in the Chairman's role under the statute's procedures. That detail becomes important when considering quorum rules.

What the quorum rule means

IGRA provides that two members of the Commission, at least one of whom is the Chairman or Vice Chairman, constitute a quorum. This rule has practical consequences. A body with only one sitting commissioner cannot take action that requires a Commission vote, even if the agency's staff continues to operate. Our analysis of the NIGC leadership vacuum and quorum questions looks at how this has played out when seats are unfilled.

It is important to separate what depends on a Commission vote from what does not. Day-to-day work such as audits, technical assistance, training and field compliance reviews is carried out by staff and does not require a quorum. By contrast, certain formal decisions, including particular enforcement actions and approvals set by statute or regulation, may require action by the Chairman or the full Commission. The precise division depends on the regulations and delegations in place, and readers should consult the agency's published rules for specifics rather than assume a single answer applies to every action.

What the Commission actually does

The Commission's statutory authority covers several areas. It reviews and approves tribal gaming ordinances and management contracts, subject to the standards in IGRA. It can issue notices of violation, impose civil fines and order temporary closure of gaming facilities in specified circumstances. It conducts audits and background reviews, and it sets minimum standards, including the requirement that tribes submit annual independent audits. Our explainer on how the NIGC regulates tribal gaming covers these tools in more detail.

The Chairman holds certain powers on his or her own, such as approving tribal gaming ordinances and management contracts and issuing some enforcement actions, while others require a vote of the Commission. This split is why the identity and availability of the Chairman matter so much to the agency's functioning, and why a Chairman vacancy has effects that a vacancy in an associate seat may not.

Funding and independence

The agency is largely funded through fees assessed on tribal gaming operations, which Congress caps by statute at a percentage of gross gaming revenue. The mechanics are described in our explainer on how the NIGC is funded. This structure creates a distinctive relationship: tribes finance a regulator that is nonetheless federal, and whose authority is bounded by the statute and by tribal primary regulatory responsibility.

The independence of the Commission is limited by design. The Chairman may be removed by the President only for neglect of duty or malfeasance in office, a protection intended to insulate the agency from political pressure, while the associate commissioners are subject to removal by the Secretary of the Interior under the statute. Those provisions, and how they interact with modern separation-of-powers doctrine, are matters for lawyers and courts rather than for this overview.

Why structure matters to Indian Country

Tribes interact with the NIGC on matters that carry financial and operational weight, from ordinance approvals to management contract reviews to compliance findings. Predictable decision-making depends on a functioning commission, which is why appointments and confirmations draw close attention from tribal governments and inter-tribal organizations. At the same time, because the tribe is the primary regulator under IGRA, the day-to-day quality of oversight depends heavily on tribal gaming regulatory authorities, with the NIGC serving as a federal backstop.

Readers who want the primary sources can start with the statute, 25 U.S.C. § 2701 and following, and the agency's regulations in Title 25 of the Code of Federal Regulations. Our Legal Guide to IGRA and Class III gaming provides orientation on both. The takeaway is simple: IGRA built a three-person commission with a specific quorum rule, and much of what happens at the NIGC follows from that design.
