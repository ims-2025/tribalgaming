Mdewakanton Descendants Ask Supreme Court to Revisit Casino Revenue Claim

A decades-old fight over land and gaming proceeds tied to two of Minnesota's most successful casinos lands on the Supreme Court's docket.

By Marcus WhitehorsePublished July 20, 20264 min read

A group of individuals claiming descent from the historic Mdewakanton Dakota community has petitioned the U.S. Supreme Court to review a federal appeals court decision that rejected their claim to a share of land and gaming revenue connected to two of Minnesota's most successful tribal casinos. The petition, filed this summer, keeps alive a dispute that has moved through the federal courts for more than a decade and touches on some of the most sensitive questions in Indian country: who belongs to a tribal community, and who is entitled to the economic benefits that flow from a tribe's gaming enterprises.

The claimants argue they are lineal descendants of Mdewakanton people who remained loyal to the United States during the nineteenth century and were later provided for through federal appropriations used to purchase Minnesota lands. Those lands, they contend, are connected to territory now associated with the Shakopee Mdewakanton Sioux Community's Mystic Lake operation near Prior Lake and the Prairie Island Indian Community's casino downriver. Lower courts have consistently held that the claimants lack a legal entitlement to the property or its revenue, and that questions of tribal membership belong to the tribes themselves.

What the petition asks

The Supreme Court petition asks the justices to consider whether the lower courts correctly applied precedent governing federal trust obligations and the finality of earlier rulings in the same long-running matter. Filing a petition for a writ of certiorari does not guarantee review; the Court agrees to hear only a small fraction of the thousands of petitions it receives each term, and there is no indication the justices will take this one. If the Court declines, the appeals court ruling stands and the current tribal governments' control over their lands and gaming proceeds is undisturbed.

Both the Shakopee Mdewakanton and Prairie Island communities are federally recognized tribes with their own constitutions, enrollment criteria, and governing bodies. Under long-settled federal law, a tribe's authority to determine its own membership is a core attribute of sovereignty, and courts are generally reluctant to intervene in enrollment disputes. That principle has been central to the tribes' successful defense in the earlier stages of this litigation.

Questions of tribal membership and the internal distribution of gaming proceeds sit at the heart of tribal self-governance, which courts have historically been reluctant to disturb.

Why the stakes are high in Minnesota

Minnesota is home to eleven tribal nations, and gaming has become a foundation of tribal government budgets across the state, funding health care, education, housing, and economic diversification. Mystic Lake Casino Hotel is among the largest gaming properties in the Upper Midwest and anchors a broader hospitality and entertainment footprint that the Shakopee Mdewakanton community has built over the past three decades, including the Mystic Lake Amphitheater and related diversification projects. A ruling that unsettled established title or revenue arrangements would carry consequences far beyond the two communities named in the suit.

The case also unfolds against a backdrop of broader debate over the future of gaming policy in the state, where tribes and racetrack interests have repeatedly deadlocked over sports betting. Minnesota tribes have maintained a unified position that any expansion of legal wagering should run through tribal operators, and they have been protective of the exclusivity that underpins the current market. Litigation that questions the distribution of existing casino revenue adds another layer of complexity to an already crowded policy environment.

The road ahead

For now, the practical effect of the petition is limited. The tribes continue to operate their casinos, distribute proceeds according to their own governing documents, and administer their membership rolls without interruption. The claimants, for their part, have signaled that the Supreme Court represents a final avenue after years of unfavorable rulings in the district and appeals courts.

Observers of tribal gaming law will watch whether the Court requests a response from the government or the tribes, an early signal that at least one justice has flagged the petition for closer attention. Absent such a request, the petition is likely to be denied in the ordinary course. The dispute is also a study in how procedural posture shapes outcomes. Federal courts have repeatedly emphasized doctrines of finality and issue preclusion in this litigation, holding that claims resolved in earlier rounds cannot be endlessly relitigated under new theories. Those doctrines exist precisely to bring closure to protracted cases, and they have weighed heavily against the descendant claimants at every stage. A petition to the Supreme Court does not reset that history; it asks the justices to find that the lower courts misread controlling precedent, a demanding standard that few petitions meet. Legal scholars watching the matter caution against reading the filing as a sign that the underlying claims have gained new strength.

Whatever the outcome, the case is a reminder that the wealth generated by tribal gaming has not resolved every historical grievance, and that the courts remain a venue where competing claims to that wealth are contested. The tribes involved have consistently framed the litigation as an attempt to relitigate settled questions of sovereignty and membership rather than a genuine dispute over federal obligations, and each successive ruling in their favor has reinforced the principle that a tribe's control over its members and its resources is not lightly displaced.
