Five Tribes Press Congress for Prediction-Market Oversight

In a state with no legal sports betting, event-contract platforms are capturing handle tribal casinos cannot lawfully touch.

By Marcus WhitehorsePublished September 11, 20265 min read

Oklahoma's largest tribal governments have escalated their campaign against online prediction markets, asking Congress and federal regulators to confirm that event-contract platforms offering sports outcomes must answer to the same gaming laws that govern every licensed casino in the state. The Inter-Tribal Council of the Five Civilized Tribes — the standing body of the Cherokee, Chickasaw, Choctaw, Muscogee and Seminole Nations of Oklahoma — adopted a resolution calling for federal oversight of prediction markets, arguing that the platforms have moved into territory long governed by tribal gaming law while carrying none of the corresponding obligations.

The push matters more in Oklahoma than almost anywhere else. The state has no legal sports betting of any kind. Legislation to authorize it has repeatedly stalled, most recently when House Bill 1047 failed to clear the Senate earlier this year. That means every sports-related wager placed from an Oklahoma address on a federally regulated exchange is a wager the state's compacted tribes cannot lawfully accept themselves — a structural asymmetry that tribal leaders describe as the core of the problem.

Why prediction markets threaten tribal gaming exclusivity

Under the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act of 1988, Class III gaming on Indian lands requires a tribal-state compact, and in most states that compact is the instrument through which tribes secured substantial exclusivity in exchange for revenue sharing. Oklahoma's model compact is a textbook case: compacted tribes pay a monthly exclusivity fee to the state, and in fiscal 2025 those payments delivered roughly $194.3 million to the state's 1017 Education Reform Revolving Fund and about $26.5 million to general revenue.

Prediction markets sit outside that architecture entirely. Platforms offering sports event contracts operate under the Commodity Exchange Act and the oversight of the Commodity Futures Trading Commission, not under IGRA, not under a state gaming licence, and not under a tribal gaming regulatory authority. They pay no exclusivity fee, submit to no tribal background investigation, and hold no facility licence. Choctaw Nation Chief Gary Batton has said the platforms "undermine tribal sovereignty" and the compacts that have structured tribal gaming for decades.

Tribal leaders are careful to frame the objection as one of parity rather than prohibition. The resolution does not ask Congress to ban event contracts. It asks that they be required to comply with existing tribal, federal and state gaming law, and that federal action not be permitted to erode compacts negotiated in good faith. That framing is deliberate: it places the burden on federal regulators to explain why a product that functions economically like a sports wager should be regulated as a commodity derivative.

The Oklahoma calculation

Oklahoma's tribal gaming sector is the largest in the country by number of facilities, with more than 140 tribal gaming properties spread across the state and operators including Cherokee Nation Entertainment, Chickasaw Nation's Global Gaming, and Choctaw Nation of Oklahoma's casino division. Readers tracking that landscape can review the Oklahoma state hub for operator-level detail.

The exposure here is unusual. In states where tribes hold mobile sports betting rights, prediction markets compete for a share of handle that tribal sportsbooks already capture. In Oklahoma, they compete for handle that has no legal domestic outlet at all. Tribal officials argue this is the worst version of the problem: the demand is being monetised, the state and tribes see none of it, and the activity carries no player-protection framework negotiated at the compact table — no self-exclusion interoperability with tribal properties, no tribally licensed responsible-gaming program, no local problem-gambling funding.

That argument has practical political weight as Oklahoma moves toward a possible ballot question on sports betting. Every month prediction markets operate unchallenged makes it harder to argue that a regulated, compact-based framework is the only route to a legal market — and easier for opponents to claim the market already exists. Our analysis of the Oklahoma ballot path covers how exclusivity is being framed in that debate.

What federal remedies actually exist

The tribes are pursuing several tracks at once, and none of them is quick.

The first is litigation. Tribes in California, New Mexico, Michigan, Wisconsin and Washington have filed or supported suits arguing that offering sports event contracts to users physically located on Indian lands violates IGRA. Results have been mixed, with some courts declining to enjoin the platforms and others ordering geographic restrictions. The litigation record is genuinely unsettled, and appellate rulings are pending in more than one circuit. Our running coverage of prediction markets and IGRA exclusivity tracks those cases.

The second is rulemaking. The CFTC has authority to determine whether particular event contracts are contrary to the public interest, and tribal organisations have pressed for that determination in comment filings and consultation requests. That process is slow and heavily contested by the exchanges.

The third — the one the Five Tribes resolution targets most directly — is legislation. Several bills introduced this Congress would clarify the boundary between commodity event contracts and gaming, and tribal advocates have sought explicit savings language protecting IGRA and existing compacts in any federal framework. For readers new to how these authorities interact, the Legal Guide sets out the IGRA structure and the compacting process.

A coordinated posture, not a single filing

What distinguishes the Oklahoma action is its source. The Inter-Tribal Council of the Five Civilized Tribes is a long-standing intergovernmental body whose resolutions carry political weight in Washington precisely because they represent five separate sovereign governments speaking with one voice. Its involvement signals that prediction markets have moved from a litigation matter handled by individual tribes' general counsel to a policy priority at the level of principal chiefs and governors.

Whether that translates into federal action is a separate question. Congress has shown little appetite for resolving the gaming-versus-derivatives boundary, and the CFTC has not moved decisively. But the tribes' strategic aim appears narrower than a final resolution: to ensure that whenever federal policymakers do act, the record already contains a clear, unified tribal position that compacts and IGRA exclusivity are not collateral damage to be absorbed quietly. On that measure, the resolution has already done some work.
