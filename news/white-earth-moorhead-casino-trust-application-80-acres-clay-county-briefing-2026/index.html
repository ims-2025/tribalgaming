White Earth Narrows Moorhead Casino Trust Bid to 80 Acres

At an October 13 briefing, tribal officials told Clay County commissioners the Moorhead casino application has shrunk from 300 acres to 80, with a filing targeted for January 2027.

By Sarah BegayPublished October 19, 20264 min read

The White Earth Nation is moving its proposed Moorhead casino into a formal federal process, but on a smaller footprint than first envisioned. Tribal officials briefed Clay County commissioners on Tuesday, October 13, saying the nation plans to ask the Bureau of Indian Affairs to place 80 acres east of Moorhead into trust, down from the roughly 300-acre site it had earlier considered. The White Earth Moorhead casino fee-to-trust application has not yet been submitted, and officials said they expect to file by January 2027.

If federal reviews proceed without delay, tribal officials told the county, the land could be accepted into trust and gaming eligibility finalized in 2028. That timeline is an estimate rather than a commitment, and it depends on approvals that sit outside the tribe's control.

A smaller parcel, a more conventional request

The site lies near Interstate 94 and Minnesota Highway 336 on the eastern edge of Moorhead, directly across the Red River from Fargo, North Dakota. The tribe now intends to seek trust status for two 40-acre parcels within the larger assembled property. Jacob Syverson, the nation's acquisition manager, told commissioners that the reduction followed guidance from the Bureau of Indian Affairs, a practical signal that the agency favors tightly scoped requests for off-reservation gaming applications.

Scaling back the acreage does not change the legal character of the project. The site is roughly 100 miles from the White Earth Reservation, so it falls under the off-reservation framework of the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act rather than the simpler path available for land already held in trust. The Interior Secretary must find that gaming on the parcel would benefit the tribe and its members and would not be detrimental to the surrounding community, and the governor of Minnesota must concur. Our explainer on governor concurrence and the two-part determination details why that sequence is the most demanding in federal Indian gaming law.

Syverson also clarified a point that had circulated in earlier reporting. He said the tribal council has approved the site, but that the application itself still needs formal authorization and has not been filed. In other words, the project has a destination, a schedule and internal support, but not yet an application on a federal desk.

What the county heard

Several White Earth representatives joined the presentation, including Nate Mathews of the White Earth Tribal Utilities Commission and Chairman Michael Fairbanks. They described the process the application would trigger: environmental review, consultation with local governments and nearby communities, and separate approvals from the Interior Secretary and Minnesota's governor. Clay County District 4 Commissioner Kevin Campbell was among the local officials present.

The centerpiece of the outreach was an offer of a cooperative aid agreement with Clay County and the City of Moorhead. Under the concept described to commissioners, the tribe would negotiate commitments covering public safety, roads and utilities, the categories of service that host communities most often cite when a new casino is proposed. Such agreements are common in off-reservation projects because they convert local concerns into enforceable obligations before a federal decision is made. The reporting on the briefing did not state the city's position, and no agreement has been announced.

How the briefing fits the project's recent history

The October presentation is the latest step in a project that has changed direction more than once this year. In August, new tribal leadership paused the $176 million destination resort to review financing, infrastructure needs and the potential effect on the nation's existing casinos. In September the nation convened a member information meeting that reoriented the effort around a federal application, as we reported in our coverage of the member meeting on the trust bid. The county briefing shows that the reorientation has now produced concrete parameters: a defined acreage, a target filing date and an outreach plan.

The shift also changes who controls the clock. A privately financed development timeline is set by the owner; a fee-to-trust application runs on the agency's. Our analysis of fee-to-trust processing timelines shows that environmental review and consultation, rather than the initial filing, usually determine how long an application takes. A 2028 outcome would be consistent with a smooth process, not a conservative one.

What remains uncertain

Several questions will determine whether the project advances. The first is whether the tribe files by January and whether the filing includes the cooperative aid terms in written form. The second is how the state responds; a governor's concurrence is discretionary, and Minnesota already hosts a mature tribal gaming market, described in our Minnesota state hub. The third is how competing interests, including other tribes with gaming operations in the region, respond during the comment periods that accompany the federal review.

The financial case also remains to be restated. The nation has not published updated cost or revenue projections for a project built around 80 acres, and the earlier concerns about debt service and cannibalization of White Earth's existing casinos have not been publicly resolved. Those details will matter to members, who carry the downside if expansion strains enterprise revenue that funds tribal government services.

For now, the briefing marks a narrowing and a clarification. White Earth has chosen the slower, more defensible route for an off-reservation casino, and it has begun the local diplomacy that route requires.
