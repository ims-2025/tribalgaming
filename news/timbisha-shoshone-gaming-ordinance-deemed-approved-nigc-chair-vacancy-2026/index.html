Timbisha Shoshone Gaming Ordinance Deemed Approved Amid NIGC Vacancy

With no Chair in place, the 90-day clock ran out and the law approved the Death Valley tribe's ordinance on its own.

By Sarah BegayPublished November 26, 20264 min read

The Timbisha Shoshone Tribe's amended tribal gaming ordinance has been deemed approved under the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act, according to a November 17, 2026 letter from the National Indian Gaming Commission. The approval did not come from a signature. It came from the calendar: the NIGC Chair position is vacant, no Chair acted within IGRA's 90-day review window, and the statute treats an ordinance left unacted upon as approved.

The letter, addressed to Timbisha Shoshone Tribal Chairperson Margaret Cortez and signed by the NIGC's acting general counsel, Rea Cisneros, is the latest example of the agency's leadership gap shaping outcomes for tribes through operation of law rather than affirmative decisions.

What happened, step by step

The Timbisha Shoshone Tribal Council adopted the amended ordinance by Resolution 2026-08 on August 13, 2026, after making revisions the NIGC had requested. The amended ordinance replaces the tribe's prior gaming ordinance, No. 01-2003, which had been in place for more than two decades. The tribe submitted the amendment for NIGC review on August 18.

Under IGRA, the NIGC Chair has 90 days from submission to approve or disapprove a tribal gaming ordinance. The agency's regulations at 25 C.F.R. § 522.10 provide that if the Chair fails to act within that period, the ordinance is considered approved, but only to the extent it is consistent with IGRA and NIGC regulations. The November 17 letter states that, because the Chair position is vacant, no action occurred within the 90 days and the ordinance is therefore deemed approved. The letter adds the general counsel's view that the ordinance is consistent with IGRA and the commission's rules.

Why the vacancy matters

The NIGC is designed as a three-member commission, with a Chair appointed by the President and confirmed by the Senate. Many of IGRA's key approvals, including ordinances and management contracts, are statutorily assigned to the Chair. When the Chair seat is empty, those approvals cannot be granted affirmatively, and the agency has relied on the statute's deemed-approval provisions to keep ordinance reviews from stalling outright. We examined the broader consequences of that gap in our report on the NIGC Chair vacancy.

Operation-of-law outcomes are not unique to the NIGC. In October, a change to the Stillaguamish Tribe's Class III compact with Washington took effect after the Interior Department let IGRA's separate 45-day compact review period lapse, as covered in our report on the Stillaguamish deemed approval. The two mechanisms are distinct, one governing compacts at Interior and the other governing ordinances at the NIGC, but both carry the same qualifier: approval extends only as far as the document is consistent with IGRA. What sets the Timbisha letter apart is the reason the clock ran out. Interior chose not to act; the NIGC, without a Chair, could not.

A deemed approval is legally effective, but it is narrower than a signed one: it covers the ordinance only to the extent it complies with IGRA and NIGC regulations.

That qualifier is the practical difference. An affirmatively approved ordinance carries the Chair's determination that the whole document complies. A deemed-approved ordinance leaves open, at least in theory, the question of whether particular provisions are consistent with federal law. The general counsel's accompanying opinion is designed to narrow that uncertainty, and in the Timbisha letter the opinion is favorable. For most tribes, that combination functions as a workable approval, although lenders, vendors and management partners sometimes look closely at the distinction.

What the approval does and does not do

An approved ordinance is a threshold requirement for conducting Class II or Class III gaming under IGRA. It is not, by itself, authorization to open a casino. Any gaming facility would also need a tribal facility license, eligible Indian lands, and, for Class III gaming in California, a tribal-state compact approved by the Secretary of the Interior. The Timbisha letter addresses the ordinance only, and it does not signal any specific facility plans.

The Timbisha Shoshone Tribe's homeland lies in and around Death Valley, and the tribe secured a land base there through the Timbisha Shoshone Homeland Act of 2000 after decades without a reservation. Updating a gaming ordinance that dated to 2003 is the type of housekeeping many tribes undertake to align their codes with current NIGC regulations, background-check requirements and model provisions, whether or not a project is imminent.

The larger pattern

As long as the Chair seat remains vacant, more tribal submissions are likely to reach the same outcome. That is not necessarily a problem for tribes whose ordinances are well drafted, and the deemed-approval mechanism was written into IGRA precisely so that federal inaction would not block tribal governance. But it does shift the federal role from active review toward after-the-fact legal opinions, and it leaves the commission's policy direction less visible.

A confirmed Chair would restore affirmative approvals and the agency's full enforcement toolkit. Until then, the 90-day clock is doing much of the work.
