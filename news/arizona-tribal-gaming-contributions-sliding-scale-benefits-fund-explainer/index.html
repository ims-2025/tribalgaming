Arizona Tribal Gaming Contributions: The Sliding Scale Explained

A 1-to-8 percent graduated rate on five large tribes, 0.75 percent on the rest, and a published quarterly accounting of every dollar.

By Sarah BegayPublished October 19, 20264 min read

Arizona's tribal gaming contributions follow one of the most clearly defined revenue-sharing formulas in the country. Under the state's tribal-state gaming compacts, tribes with Class III casinos pay a percentage of their Class III net win to the state and to local governments, and the Arizona Department of Gaming publishes the results every quarter. Understanding the Arizona tribal gaming contributions framework is the quickest way to see how a rate structure, rather than a flat fee or a lump sum, can tie a state's receipts to the performance of individual casinos.

The most recent figures cited by industry reporting show how large the stream has become. For the third quarter of fiscal year 2025, tribes contributed $30,845,042 to the Arizona Benefits Fund, about 7.9 percent more than the same quarter a year earlier. Across all years since fiscal 2004, the Department of Gaming reports cumulative contributions to the state and its local governments of more than $2.34 billion, drawn from 26 Class III casinos.

The sliding scale and the flat rate

The formula was established by the voter-approved Proposition 202 framework of 2002 and carried into the compacts that tribes signed afterward. It does not apply a single rate to everyone. Instead, it separates the state's tribes into two groups.

Five named tribes, the Gila River Indian Community, the Salt River Pima-Maricopa Indian Community, the Ak-Chin Indian Community, the Tohono O'odham Nation and the Pascua Yaqui Tribe, pay on a graduated scale applied to year-to-date Class III net win. The first $25 million is charged at 1 percent, the next $50 million at 3 percent, the next $25 million at 6 percent, and any amount above $100 million at 8 percent. Because the scale resets each fiscal year, a large casino pays its highest marginal rate on revenue earned late in the year.

Every other Arizona tribe with a Class III casino pays a flat 0.75 percent of Class III net win. The design reflects a policy judgment that the largest and most profitable operators, many located in the Phoenix and Tucson metropolitan areas, can support a heavier obligation, while smaller rural properties should keep nearly all of their revenue to fund tribal government and economic development. The profile of Gila River Gaming Enterprises illustrates the scale of the operators that sit at the top of the scale.

Net win, the base for the calculation, is the amount wagered minus the amount paid out as winnings on Class III games. It is therefore a measure of what the casino retains from gaming, not of total resort revenue, and it excludes hotel, food and beverage, and entertainment income. That detail matters when comparing Arizona with states that assess fees against a broader base.

Where the money goes

The compact divides each contribution in two. Twelve percent goes directly to cities, towns and counties of the tribe's choosing, for community services and public safety. The remaining 88 percent is deposited quarterly into the Arizona Benefits Fund, which pays for the Department of Gaming's operations and its problem gambling programs and then supports several designated public purposes.

The third-quarter fiscal 2025 distribution shows the proportions in practice. Roughly half of the fund's receipts, $15.4 million, went to the Instructional Improvement Fund for education. Another $7.7 million went to trauma and emergency services. The Department of Gaming's operating costs took $2.8 million, the Wildlife Conservation Fund and the Tourism Fund received $2.2 million each, and $617,000 funded problem gambling education, treatment and prevention.

The Arizona model earmarks nearly every dollar by statute. Education and emergency medical care receive about three-quarters of the Benefits Fund, which gives the program a durable public constituency.

Why the Arizona model matters nationally

Arizona's structure answers a question that arises in every compact negotiation: what must a state give in return for a share of tribal gaming revenue? Under the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act, a state generally may not tax tribal gaming, and payments to a state are lawful only when tied to meaningful concessions such as exclusivity, expanded games or other benefits. The Legal Guide covers that requirement in detail. In Arizona, the contribution framework came with expanded gaming authorization and a defined market in which tribes hold the right to operate Class III casinos.

The model also appears in the state's recent expansion of tribal event wagering. As the analysis of five years of Arizona event wagering notes, the hybrid arrangement layers new products on top of the same compact architecture, and the contribution base grows only to the extent that the agreements assign new activity to it.

Three features stand out for those comparing states. The graduated rate ties the state's take to the largest casinos' growth without requiring renegotiation. The earmarked distribution makes the revenue visible to the public, since the quarterly reports list each recipient fund. And the local-government set-aside gives host communities a direct stake. Compare that with the more discretionary treatment of compact revenue in Connecticut, where the allocation of the state's share has been a recurring budget dispute.

Anyone tracking the Arizona market can follow the quarterly reports from the Department of Gaming and the broader context in the Arizona state hub and the market deep dive. As new casinos open, including the Pascua Yaqui Tribe's third property in Tucson, the contribution figures will show how much additional net win the formula captures.
