Geographic Exclusivity Zones in Tribal-State Compacts, Explained

Many compacts draw lines on a map. Those lines set the price of revenue sharing, and online wagering is now testing them.

By Elena RuizPublished November 24, 20264 min read

Most discussion of tribal gaming exclusivity treats it as a statewide question: does the tribe have the only right to offer slot machines, banked table games or sports betting in the state? Many of the most consequential tribal-state compacts are more specific than that. They draw lines on a map. Geographic exclusivity zones, meaning defined counties, regions or corridors where a tribe's gaming rights are protected from competition, are a central part of the bargain in New York, Massachusetts and, in a different form, Florida. Seen together, they show how tribes and states use geography to set the value of a compact's revenue-sharing payments.

The basic logic comes from federal law. Under the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act, a state generally cannot tax tribal gaming. Revenue-sharing payments are permitted when the state gives the tribe a meaningful concession in return, and exclusivity is the usual one. A geographic zone is a way of specifying exactly what the tribe is buying. Our revenue-sharing explainer and the Legal Guide cover the legal background.

New York: three tribes, three exclusivity zones

New York is the clearest example of a state divided by tribal geographic exclusivity. The Seneca Nation's 2002 compact gave it exclusive rights to casino gaming in a western New York zone, roughly everything west of State Route 14. The St. Regis Mohawk Tribe holds a zone covering eight northern counties: Clinton, Essex, Franklin, Hamilton, Jefferson, Lewis, St. Lawrence and Warren. The Oneida Indian Nation's 2013 settlement with the state set out a protected zone in central New York around Turning Stone.

The zones were tested in 2013, when New York prepared to authorize commercial casinos upstate. In the settlements negotiated that year, the state agreed to keep commercial casino siting out of the tribal zones, and the tribes resumed or confirmed revenue-sharing payments. The St. Regis Mohawk Tribe, for example, had stopped payments in 2010 on the grounds that the state had violated its exclusivity. It agreed to pay about $30 million in past payments and to resume paying 25 percent of net gaming revenue.

The zones have continued to produce disputes. The Seneca Nation's multi-year payments dispute with the state, which ended with the Nation paying the state hundreds of millions of dollars in 2022, turned in part on what its exclusivity was worth. Competition from state-authorized video lottery terminals inside or near the Seneca zone remains a point of contention, as our analysis of VLT competition in western New York describes. With the Seneca compact's December 2026 expiration approaching, the zone's boundaries and the price of protecting it are central to the successor compact talks.

Massachusetts and Florida: regions and carve-outs

Massachusetts took a regional approach in its 2011 Expanded Gaming Act, which divided the state into three casino regions. Region C, covering the southeastern part of the state, gave the Mashpee Wampanoag Tribe a priority path to a tribal casino ahead of commercial applicants. Our Region C explainer describes how that priority has shaped more than a decade of planning in the region.

Florida's approach works in reverse. The Seminole Tribe's 2021 compact gives it statewide exclusivity on banked card games, craps, roulette and hub-and-spoke sports wagering. The exclusivity is defined by carve-outs, since the pari-mutuel facilities in Miami-Dade and Broward counties keep their state-licensed slot machines. In return, the Tribe committed to a minimum of $2.5 billion in revenue-sharing payments over the first five years. The geography is still part of the deal, but it is written as exceptions to a statewide right rather than as a zone. See our Seminole compact analysis and the Florida state hub for details.

What geographic exclusivity zones mean for compact negotiations

Exclusivity zones make the terms of a compact easier to see. A statewide exclusivity clause can be vague about what counts as competition. A zone with listed counties is easier to enforce and easier to price. That clarity has a cost: it gives both sides a measurable line to argue over. When a state authorizes VLTs in a county next to a zone, or when online wagering lets players in the zone bet with an outside operator, the tribe can argue that the state has broken the agreement, and the state can argue that it has not.

Digital gaming adds a further complication. A zone drawn in 2002 or 2013 was meant to protect brick-and-mortar casinos. Mobile sports betting, iGaming and prediction-market contracts do not stay inside county lines. Several pending negotiations are trying to settle whether exclusivity zones apply to online products, whether a tribe's online rights reach beyond its zone, and whether out-of-state operators reaching players inside a zone affect the revenue-sharing obligation.

For states, geographic exclusivity remains one of the few concessions that can support significant revenue sharing under federal review. For tribes, a well-drafted zone is a durable asset. It can last through changes in governors and legislatures, and it gives tribal governments a clear basis for enforcement. The next set of compacts in New York and elsewhere will show whether these map-based protections can be adapted to gaming that increasingly happens on phones rather than casino floors.
