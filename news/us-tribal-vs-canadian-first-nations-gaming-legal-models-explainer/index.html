US Tribal Gaming vs. Canadian First Nations Gaming: Two Legal Models

Similar casinos, different foundations — federal sovereignty and compacts versus provincial Crown authority.

By James KingfisherPublished July 29, 20266 min read

Indigenous gaming operates on both sides of the Canada–United States border, and to a casual observer the casinos can look interchangeable: comparable floors, similar amenities, revenue directed toward community benefit. But the legal architecture underneath is fundamentally different. In the United States, tribal gaming rests on federal law and negotiated tribal-state compacts. In Canada, First Nations gaming flows through provincial authority under the Criminal Code. Understanding these two models is essential to making sense of why the industries look similar on the surface yet behave so differently in practice.

The distinction is not academic. It shapes who owns a casino, who regulates it, how revenue is shared, and how much control an Indigenous nation exercises over its own gaming enterprise. For anyone comparing operators, tracking cross-border trends, or evaluating investment, the legal foundation is the starting point.

The U.S. model: federal law and compacts

Tribal gaming in the United States is governed by the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act of 1988, or IGRA, a federal statute enacted in the wake of a landmark Supreme Court decision affirming tribal authority to conduct gaming. That decision, examined in our explainer on California v. Cabazon Band, established that tribes as sovereign governments could offer gaming largely free of state regulatory control, prompting Congress to build a federal framework around that sovereignty.

IGRA sorts gaming into three classes. Class I covers traditional and social games and falls under exclusive tribal jurisdiction. Class II covers bingo and certain non-banked card games, regulated by tribes with federal oversight from the National Indian Gaming Commission. Class III—the Las Vegas–style casino gaming that generates most tribal revenue—requires a compact negotiated between the tribe and the state. That compact is the defining instrument of the U.S. system: it sets the terms of exclusivity, revenue sharing, regulatory responsibility, and the scope of permitted games. The mechanics of these agreements are detailed in our legal guide to IGRA and Class III gaming.

Two features define the U.S. model. First, gaming is grounded in tribal sovereignty—the tribe is a government, not merely a licensee. Second, net gaming revenue must be directed to enumerated governmental purposes: funding tribal operations, member welfare, economic development, and charitable giving. Gaming is legally a tool of self-governance, not a private commercial venture.

In the United States, a tribe operates gaming as a sovereign government under federal law. In Canada, a First Nation operates under provincial licence within a Crown-controlled framework. That single difference cascades through everything else.

The Canadian model: provincial authority under the Criminal Code

Canada has no IGRA and no compact system. Gaming is regulated through the Criminal Code, which grants the provinces authority to conduct and manage gaming within their borders. As a result, First Nations casinos operate under provincial licences and agreements rather than through federally structured, government-to-government compacts. The province—not the First Nation—typically holds ultimate regulatory authority, and the terms of Indigenous participation are set by provincial policy and negotiation.

This produces a patchwork that varies considerably by province. Saskatchewan built one of the earliest and most developed First Nations gaming systems, operated through a dedicated Indigenous gaming authority whose growth we track in our coverage of the Saskatchewan First Nations gaming expansion. Ontario channels a defined share of provincial gaming proceeds to First Nations through a formal revenue-sharing arrangement. Other provinces have their own structures, and some are only now considering whether to authorize First Nations venues in major urban markets. The result is that a First Nation's degree of ownership and control depends heavily on which province it sits in.

How the models diverge in practice

The consequences of these two frameworks show up across the industry. On ownership, U.S. tribes generally own and operate their casinos directly as sovereign enterprises, while Canadian First Nations frequently operate within a Crown framework that reserves significant control to the province. On regulation, the U.S. system layers tribal, federal, and state roles defined by compact, whereas Canada centers regulatory authority in provincial gaming bodies. On revenue, IGRA mandates that proceeds serve governmental purposes, while Canadian arrangements are governed by provincial revenue-sharing formulas that differ from one jurisdiction to the next. The variety of Canadian approaches is compared in our review of First Nations gaming revenue frameworks across provinces.

These differences also explain divergent trends. In Canada, a notable recent development has been First Nations acquiring existing commercial casinos outright, effectively purchasing their way into ownership positions the licensing model did not originally grant them. In the United States, the frontier issues are different—compact negotiations, off-reservation land decisions, and the reach of federal Indian gaming law over new products such as sports-event exchanges. The underlying legal design channels each country's industry toward different pressure points.

Why the distinction matters

For all their surface similarity, U.S. tribal gaming and Canadian First Nations gaming are built on different premises. The U.S. system begins from tribal sovereignty and constructs a federal framework to accommodate it. The Canadian system begins from provincial Crown authority and negotiates Indigenous participation within it. Neither is simply a more or less advanced version of the other; they are distinct legal traditions producing distinct outcomes.

Grasping that distinction is the key to interpreting news on either side of the border. A compact dispute in the United States and a provincial licensing decision in Canada are not variations on a theme—they arise from separate legal orders. Readers who keep the two models straight will find the cross-border gaming landscape far easier to navigate, and far less prone to the false assumption that what applies in one country applies in the other.
