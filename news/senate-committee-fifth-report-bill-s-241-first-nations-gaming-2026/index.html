Senate Committee Urges Passage of Bill S-241 on First Nations Gaming

The Standing Senate Committee on Indigenous Peoples backs the opt-in bill without changes, citing revenue retention and jurisdiction.

By Marcus WhitehorsePublished October 16, 20264 min read

The Standing Senate Committee on Indigenous Peoples has recommended that Canada's Senate pass Bill S-241 without amendment, a step that moves the long-running effort to recognize First Nations jurisdiction over gaming closer to a final vote in the upper chamber. The committee's Fifth Report, chaired by Senator Michèle Audette, was tabled on May 27, 2026, and states that the committee agrees with all witnesses that Bill S-241 should be enacted in its current form.

The recommendation matters for anyone following Bill S-241 and First Nations gaming in Canada, because the bill would change who has authority to run lotteries on First Nation lands. It is the successor to Bill S-268, which was studied in 2024 but died when Parliament was prorogued before the committee could report.

What the bill would do

Bill S-241 is titled An Act to amend the Criminal Code and the Indian Act. According to the committee's report, it would allow the governing body of a First Nation to conduct and manage lottery schemes from or within its lands and to license individuals and organizations to do so. It would also give band councils by-law-making authority on the same subject. The regime is opt-in: a First Nation that prefers to remain in a provincially led gaming system could do so.

One difference from the earlier S-268 stands out. The committee notes that S-241, unlike S-268, could extend to online gaming. That is significant in a market where provinces such as Ontario and Alberta have launched regulated online markets and where First Nations have pressed for a share of, or a role in, those frameworks. Our analysis of Bill S-268 and First Nations gaming jurisdiction sets out the earlier bill's structure, and our earlier report on S-241 in the Senate covers its introduction.

The committee's process

The Senate referred the bill to the committee on March 12, 2026. The committee studied it in a single meeting in April 2026, relying in part on its 2024 study of S-268. That compressed schedule reflects how much of the evidentiary record carried over from the earlier bill. It also means the report is best read as an endorsement of an existing policy direction rather than the product of a new, extensive hearing process.

The report records testimony that Canada's present arrangement leaves provinces with substantial control over how much gaming revenue First Nations keep. In Alberta, as the report describes it, host First Nations give 30 percent of slot revenue to the province and 10 percent to non-host First Nations. The committee concludes that S-241 would let First Nations retain more casino revenue if it is enacted. For a comparison of how different provinces structure these flows, see our review of Canadian First Nations gaming revenue frameworks.

Trade-offs the report acknowledges

The report does not present the bill as cost-free. It acknowledges that full retention of revenue by casino-operating First Nations could reduce the resources available to other First Nations in the same province, which could be difficult for rural or remote communities that do not host a casino. That tension runs through every provincial revenue-sharing model, and it is one reason the opt-in design matters: communities can weigh retention against the benefits of pooled arrangements.

The report also highlights how gaming revenue is used. It cites Tsuut'ina Nation, Rama First Nation, Whitecap Dakota First Nation, Casino Rama's capital spending in Orillia and Barrie, and the Federation of Sovereign Indigenous Nations' spending on gambling-related problems. We examine those examples in our companion analysis of revenue sharing versus ownership.

Why the opt-in design is central

Much of the committee's endorsement rests on the opt-in structure. Because First Nations could choose whether to exercise the authority, the bill does not displace the provincial systems that currently supply revenue, regulation and technology to many communities. A First Nation satisfied with its provincial agreement could remain in it, while one seeking greater control could pursue licensing and by-law authority. The report treats that flexibility as a strength, and it is also the feature most likely to shape how provinces respond, since each would need to decide how to coordinate with First Nations that elect to act independently.

What happens next

A committee report recommending passage is a procedural milestone, not enactment. The bill must still clear remaining stages in the Senate and then proceed through the House of Commons before receiving Royal Assent. This article reflects the committee's May 27 report and does not track later parliamentary stages; the current status of S-241 is available from Parliament's LEGISinfo database.

For operators and provincial regulators, the practical questions are well defined. Provinces will want to know how First Nation licensing and by-laws would interact with existing conduct-and-manage agreements, including Saskatchewan's SIGA model. Tribes and First Nations in the United States, who operate under a federal statute rather than the Criminal Code, will watch closely; our explainer on US tribal and Canadian First Nations gaming legal models outlines why the two systems diverge. The committee's message, in any case, is unambiguous: pass the bill as written.
