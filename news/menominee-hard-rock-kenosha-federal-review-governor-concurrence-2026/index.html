Hard Rock Kenosha Clears Federal Review, Leaving One Signature

After two decades of federal stops and starts, the Menominee Indian Tribe's off-reservation project has reached the step where every Kenosha proposal has previously died.

By Marcus WhitehorsePublished August 19, 20264 min read

The Menominee Indian Tribe of Wisconsin's proposed Hard Rock Hotel & Casino Kenosha has cleared federal environmental review, the strongest procedural position the long-running project has occupied in more than twenty years of attempts. The Bureau of Indian Affairs released its Draft Environmental Assessment on March 13, 2026, concluding that construction and operation of the $360 million resort would produce insignificant impacts across air quality, water resources, traffic, public services and biological resources. Federal officials confirmed in July that the review had been completed.

What remains is the step that has historically decided the Kenosha question: concurrence from the Governor of Wisconsin. Because the site sits on off-reservation land far from the Menominee Reservation, the project depends on the two-part determination pathway under Section 20 of the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act, which requires the Secretary of the Interior to find that gaming would benefit the tribe and not harm the surrounding community, and then requires the state's governor to agree. The concurrence requirement gives a single elected official an effective veto over a project that has otherwise satisfied federal review, and it is where the Menominee proposal has stalled before.

What the environmental assessment describes

The published assessment sets out a 346,000-square-foot resort on a 59-acre site of four parcels west of Interstate 94, at 122nd Avenue and 60th Street. Under the preferred alternative, the property would include up to 95,000 square feet of casino space with 1,500 Class III slot machines and 55 table games seating roughly 330 players, a 150-room hotel tower capped at 75 feet to comply with Kenosha Regional Airport height restrictions, a 33,000-square-foot Hard Rock Live venue seating approximately 2,000, several restaurants, and 2,400 surface parking spaces.

The tribe and Hard Rock International project more than 1,000 permanent jobs and roughly 2.4 million annual visitors, a figure that reflects the site's position between the Milwaukee and Chicago metropolitan markets rather than the population of Kenosha County itself. Local approvals are already in place: both the City of Kenosha and Kenosha County have executed the intergovernmental agreements supporting the development.

Why the concurrence step matters more than the review

The Kenosha file is a useful reminder that federal environmental clearance and gaming eligibility are separate questions from state political consent. Off-reservation proposals routinely survive the technical review only to encounter a governor weighing the objections of other tribes, competing operators, or host-community opponents. Wisconsin's tribal gaming landscape is unusually dense, and any new southeastern Wisconsin property would sit within the effective market reach of several existing tribal casinos — a dynamic explored further in our coverage of intertribal opposition to off-reservation casinos.

There is direct precedent for that outcome in this file. The Menominee Nation's earlier Kenosha proposal, sited at the former Dairyland Greyhound Park, received a favorable federal determination from the Department of the Interior in 2013 only to be rejected at the state level in early 2015. The tribe restarted the process on a different site with a different partner structure, and the current 59-acre parcel west of Interstate 94 reflects that reset. The environmental clearance now in hand is procedurally stronger than what preceded the 2015 rejection, but it does not change the identity of the decision-maker at the final step.

Neither Hard Rock nor the Menominee Nation has released a construction schedule or opening timeline, and the tribe has been consistent that no timeline will be published until both the federal decision and the governor's concurrence are secured. Site preparation, utility work and vertical construction all sit behind that sequencing.

The wider Wisconsin picture

The Kenosha decision arrives while Wisconsin tribal gaming is unusually active on several fronts. The Ho-Chunk Nation's Beloit casino is preparing for a September 2026 opening that will make it the state's second-largest gaming floor, and the state's compact framework has been under discussion in parallel with mobile sports betting rollouts. Readers tracking the state as a whole can consult our Wisconsin market deep dive, and those following the border competition between southern Wisconsin and northern Illinois will find context in our analysis of the Beloit and Rockford border market.

The economics of the site are what have kept the proposal alive across three decades of setbacks. Kenosha sits on the Illinois line, roughly an hour from downtown Chicago and forty minutes from Milwaukee, on an interstate corridor carrying substantial cross-border traffic. Illinois has since licensed commercial casinos of its own closer to the state line, which compresses but does not eliminate the catchment advantage. The 2.4 million annual visitor projection depends on capturing traffic that currently passes the site rather than on organic growth in Kenosha County.

For a project first proposed in the early 2000s, the significance of clearing environmental review is real but bounded. It removes the technical objection that opponents have leaned on for years and shifts the decision entirely into the political arena. Whether that is progress depends on a judgment no environmental assessment can supply.
