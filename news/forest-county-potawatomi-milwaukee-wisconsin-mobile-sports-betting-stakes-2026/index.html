Wisconsin Mobile Sports Betting: What's at Stake for Potawatomi Milwaukee

The state's only big-city tribal casino joined the defense of Act 247. Its urban market makes the case's outcome unusually consequential.

By Marcus WhitehorsePublished November 24, 20264 min read

The Forest County Potawatomi Community is now formally part of the court fight over Wisconsin mobile sports betting, and its Milwaukee casino has the most at stake of any tribal property in the state. On November 9, Waukesha County Circuit Judge J. Arthur Melvin III allowed eight Wisconsin tribes, including the Forest County Potawatomi, to intervene in Brown County Taxpayers Association v. Evers. The lawsuit challenges 2025 Wisconsin Act 247, signed in April 2026, which authorizes online sports wagering through tribal-state compacts. Neither side opposed the motion.

For Potawatomi Casino Hotel, which marks its 35th year in 2026, the case will decide whether the tribe can add statewide mobile wagering to an on-site sportsbook that has operated within Wisconsin's in-person limits so far. The property's location is a large part of why the outcome matters.

Why Wisconsin mobile sports betting matters most in Milwaukee

Potawatomi Casino Hotel stands on trust land in Milwaukee's Menominee Valley, at 1721 West Canal Street, more than 200 miles from the tribe's Forest County homelands in the state's north. It is the only major tribal casino inside Wisconsin's largest metropolitan area. That gives it access to a population base that rural tribal properties cannot match. It also means much of its potential sports-betting customer base already lives within reach of out-of-state apps, offshore sites and, more recently, prediction-market platforms.

Under the current framework, Wisconsin sports wagering has been limited to tribal casino premises. Jeff Crawford, the Forest County Potawatomi's attorney general, has spoken publicly in support of expansion. Speaking to Spectrum News 1 in January, he described the gap between in-casino betting and the mobile access bettors in neighboring states already have. Act 247 was the legislature's answer: mobile wagering anywhere in the state, offered only by tribes, under compact amendments, with bets treated as placed on tribal land where the servers are located. Our Wisconsin tribal gaming market deep dive covers how that structure fits into the state's eleven-tribe gaming landscape.

For an urban operator, mobile access could matter more than a new hotel tower. A sportsbook that only takes bets from people standing on the casino floor competes for a small share of the metro market. A tribe-run app could reach most of it.

The lawsuit and the litigation calendar

The case was filed August 5 by the Wisconsin Institute for Law & Liberty on behalf of the Brown County Taxpayers Association and other plaintiffs. They make two main arguments: that Act 247 conflicts with Wisconsin's 1993 constitutional amendment restricting gambling, and that it improperly grants an economic benefit based on tribal membership. The second claim, an equal-protection-style argument, has appeared in challenges to tribal exclusivity in other states. Courts have generally responded that compacts are agreements between governments, not preferences for individuals.

The intervening tribes are the Bad River Band, Forest County Potawatomi Community, Ho-Chunk Nation, Menominee Indian Tribe, Oneida Nation, Red Cliff Band, Sokaogon Chippewa Community and Stockbridge-Munsee Community.

Tribal attorneys have indicated they will seek dismissal, emphasizing that compacts are government-to-government agreements. Under the current schedule, an amended complaint is due December 11, and oral argument is set for April 20, 2027. A trial-court ruling is not expected until spring at the earliest, and any decision is likely to be appealed. Our earlier report on the tribes' motion to intervene explains the procedural background. For the federal side, including how compact amendments are reviewed by the Department of the Interior, see the Legal Guide.

Even if the tribes win in Waukesha County, mobile betting cannot start until each tribe's compact amendment is negotiated with the state and approved, or allowed to take effect, by Interior. That work will be done with an incoming governor who takes office in January 2027. Every Wisconsin tribe that wants to offer mobile wagering will need its own amendment, so the timing will differ from tribe to tribe.

What Potawatomi is doing while it waits

The Milwaukee property has not paused its non-gaming development while the case proceeds. Its 2026 calendar included a 35th-anniversary promotion running from January through March and an expanding entertainment schedule. The tribe also announced Āya, a Mediterranean restaurant in Milwaukee's Third Ward, which places its hospitality brand outside the casino itself. OnMilwaukee has also reported a sportsbook sponsorship arrangement between Potawatomi Casino Hotel and the Green Bay Packers, which builds the tribe's betting brand ahead of any mobile launch.

These moves are consistent with a broader trend in tribal gaming: operators facing regulatory uncertainty on digital products put money into food, entertainment and brand recognition that will hold value whatever the outcome. If mobile wagering arrives, a well-known local brand will matter in a crowded app market. If the court case delays it, those investments still bring visitors to Canal Street.

Three things will shape the next six months: the amended complaint due December 11, the new administration's approach to compact talks in January, and the April 20 oral argument. All eight intervening tribes have a stake in each, and the Forest County Potawatomi, with the state's only big-city tribal casino, has the largest market to gain or lose.
