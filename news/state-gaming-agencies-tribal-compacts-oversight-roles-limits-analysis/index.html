State Gaming Agencies in Tribal Compacts: Oversight Roles and Their Limits

Tribal casinos are regulated first by tribes, with federal and state layers added by statute and compact. The state layer is the least uniform, and the most contested.

By James KingfisherPublished October 20, 20266 min read

Anyone trying to understand who actually watches a tribal casino floor runs into a recurring question: what does the state do? The short answer is that the answer depends on the compact. Under the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act of 1988, tribes are the primary regulators of gaming on their lands, the National Indian Gaming Commission oversees compliance with federal law, and states enter the picture only for Class III gaming, where IGRA requires a tribal-state compact. Understanding the role of the state gaming agency means reading both the statute and the negotiated text, which is why the state layer is the least uniform in Indian gaming. Our overview of the tribal, federal and state regulatory layers provides the broader map.

What IGRA allows a compact to say about regulation

IGRA lists the subjects a Class III compact may address. Among them are the application of criminal and civil laws and regulations directly related to and necessary for licensing and regulating gaming, the allocation of criminal and civil jurisdiction between the state and the tribe, and state assessments of the amounts necessary to defray the costs of regulating the activity. The statute also allows compacts to cover standards for operating the gaming activity and the maintenance of the facility, including licensing, and any other subjects directly related to the operation of gaming activities.

That list is permissive, not prescriptive. IGRA does not tell a state to create a gaming agency, and it does not define what that agency does. The statute sets a boundary rather than a blueprint: a state may negotiate regulatory roles that are directly related to gaming, but it may not use the compact process to impose a tax, fee or charge on the tribe other than the permitted regulatory assessments. That limit has been central to a long run of disputes over what states can demand in exchange for a compact, as covered in our analysis of state regulatory cost assessments.

The roles state agencies commonly hold

Compacts typically designate a state gaming agency, which varies by state. In California, compacts generally name the California Gambling Control Commission as a licensing and oversight counterpart. In Arizona the Department of Gaming is the state entity. In Washington the Washington State Gaming Commission plays that role, and in Michigan the Michigan Gaming Control Board does. The names differ, and so do the responsibilities, but several functions recur across compacts.

One is background investigation and licensing support, particularly for management contractors, gaming vendors and certain key employees. Another is access to inspect and audit, usually structured as scheduled or for-cause inspections of gaming operations and records, with notice and confidentiality provisions. A third is technical standards, in which the state agency reviews or approves the games and systems, often alongside an independent testing laboratory. A fourth is receiving reports, such as notice of significant incidents or violations. Finally, many compacts give the state agency a role in dispute resolution, including notice and cure periods before a matter escalates to arbitration or court.

What state agencies generally do not control is day-to-day operations. The tribal gaming regulatory authority, commonly called the TGRA, licenses employees, monitors the floor and enforces the tribal gaming ordinance. In most compacts the state's function is to verify that the tribal system meets the agreed standards, not to substitute for it. Our explainer on tribal gaming regulatory authorities details that first line of regulation.

Where the limits show up

The boundary between oversight and intrusion shows up in several recurring friction points. The first is cost. Compacts that allow state recovery of regulatory costs often specify how those costs are calculated, capped or audited. Disagreements over whether an assessment is a genuine cost recovery or a disguised revenue measure are a common source of negotiation tension.

The second is information access. State agencies generally want broad rights to review records and surveillance, while tribes emphasize sovereignty and confidentiality of proprietary and tribal government information. The compromise is usually procedural, with defined access rights, notice periods and confidentiality protections rather than unrestricted access.

The third is enforcement. A state agency typically cannot fine a tribal casino unilaterally. Instead, compacts channel disputes into notice, cure and arbitration or litigation processes. The extent to which a compact allows an agency to seek relief in court is itself negotiated, and tribal sovereign immunity limits what can be pursued absent a waiver. The mechanics of dispute provisions are discussed in our explainer on compact dispute resolution and arbitration.

Why the state layer matters going forward

As compacts add new subjects, from sports wagering to mobile and online play in some states, the state agency's role tends to expand. New products raise technical, geolocation and consumer protection questions that fall within a state's traditional regulatory expertise, and compacts increasingly spell out how the state agency and the tribal regulator coordinate on them. Where a compact is renegotiated, the allocation of those duties is often among the items most closely examined. For tribes in states with many compacts, such as California, the state agency also becomes a repository of consistent practice across tribes; see our California state hub for tribe-by-tribe information.

The practical takeaway is that no single description fits every state. Readers who want to know what a particular agency can do should read the compact itself, which is public once approved and published in the Federal Register. Our Legal Guide to IGRA and Class III gaming explains how to find and read those documents. The compact, not the general rule, is what binds the parties.
