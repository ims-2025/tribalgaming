Federal Court Denies Picayune Injunction, Dismisses Suit Against North Fork

The October 29 order leaves the Madera casino open, but the Picayune Rancheria has already appealed.

By Marcus WhitehorsePublished November 10, 20266 min read

A federal court has denied the Picayune Rancheria of the Chukchansi Indians' request for a preliminary injunction against the North Fork Rancheria of Mono Indians' new casino in Madera County, California, and dismissed Picayune's claims on tribal sovereign immunity grounds. The order, issued October 29 after a hearing on October 9, came a day after the casino opened its doors. According to a statement from counsel for North Fork, the U.S. District Court for the Eastern District of California ruled that Picayune had not shown a likelihood of success on the merits or irreparable harm, and that the balance of equities and the public interest did not favor an injunction.

The same statement says the court granted North Fork's motion to dismiss based on its tribal sovereign immunity and dismissed Picayune's claims without leave to amend. Picayune has already appealed. Because the account of the ruling comes from the law firm that represents North Fork, readers should treat its characterization as one side's summary until the order and any appellate filings are reviewed in full.

What the court decided

Two rulings are bundled in the reported order, and they do different work. The denial of a preliminary injunction is a decision about interim relief: whether a court should halt a project before the case is finally decided. The dismissal is a decision about whether the case can proceed at all. The two interact. A court that concludes a defendant is immune from suit has little reason to preserve the status quo for a case it cannot hear.

On the injunction, the reported findings track the familiar four-part test that federal courts apply: likelihood of success, likelihood of irreparable harm, the balance of equities, and the public interest. Reporting indicates the court found Picayune fell short on each. We explain how that standard operates, and why it is difficult to satisfy, in our explainer on the preliminary injunction standard in tribal gaming litigation.

Background to the dispute

North Fork's project is a roughly $700 million casino resort near Highway 99 in Madera County. The Secretary of the Interior took the land into trust for the tribe in 2013, and the National Indian Gaming Commission approved the casino's management contract in 2024, according to North Fork's counsel. Picayune, which operates Chukchansi Gold Resort & Casino to the north, challenged both the trust decision and the contract approval over a dispute that has run for more than a decade across several federal and state courts.

We have followed the run-up closely. Our earlier coverage of the Picayune federal lawsuit ahead of the October opening described the request for an injunction, and our report on the October 28 opening with the ruling pending noted that the court had yet to act. The October 29 order resolves the question that those articles left open, at least at the district court level.

Why sovereign immunity decided the case

Tribal sovereign immunity protects federally recognized tribes from lawsuits unless Congress has abrogated it or the tribe has waived it. The Supreme Court reaffirmed the doctrine's breadth in its 2014 decision in Michigan v. Bay Mills Indian Community, a ruling we discuss in our Bay Mills explainer. When a plaintiff sues a tribe directly, the immunity question typically comes first, and in the absence of a waiver it usually ends the case.

That is why disputes between competing tribes are structurally difficult for the challenger. The Indian Gaming Regulatory Act gives tribes and states routes to enforce compacts and certain provisions, but it does not give one tribe a general right to sue another over a rival's casino. A challenger therefore tends to aim at federal agencies, as Picayune did in contesting the trust decision and contract approval, rather than at the tribe itself. When the tribe is a necessary party who cannot be joined, courts can end the suit, a dynamic detailed in our analysis of the Maverick Gaming outcome.

What happens next

Picayune's appeal moves the dispute to the Ninth Circuit. An appeal of an order denying a preliminary injunction is available as of right, and the appellate court reviews the district court's decision for abuse of discretion on the injunction factors while reviewing questions of law, including immunity, more closely. The timeline for briefing and argument has not been published. Parties sometimes ask for expedited treatment in such cases, and an appellate panel could also decline to disturb the order and let the casino continue operating while the underlying case proceeds.

The casino, which North Fork's counsel says employs more than 1,300 people, can remain open in the meantime. The tribe has roughly 3,000 citizens, and the project is expected to fund tribal government operations and services. For the surrounding market, the opening adds a major new competitor in the Central Valley, where Picayune's property and other tribal casinos draw from overlapping customer bases. Our California state hub lists the operators in the region.

The broader lesson for California tribes and developers is that litigation risk does not necessarily end when construction does. A casino can open with appeals pending, and a ruling for the operator at one stage does not foreclose review at the next. TribalGaming.com will report on the Ninth Circuit's docket as it develops.
