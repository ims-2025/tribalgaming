SIGA posts record $167M net income on $418M revenue for 2025-26

The First Nations-owned operator's 30th year delivered its strongest financials yet, with $167 million in net income routed largely back to Saskatchewan communities.

By Linda CardinalPublished July 11, 20265 min read

The Saskatchewan Indian Gaming Authority closed its 2025-26 fiscal year with the strongest financial results in its three-decade history, reporting a record $167 million in net income on a record $418 million in gross revenue. The figures, released in late June, mark the fourth consecutive year of record profits for the First Nations-owned operator and cap a milestone year that also happened to be SIGA's 30th in operation.

Revenue rose roughly 10.6 percent over the prior year across SIGA's seven casinos, which operate on First Nations land throughout the province. The organization is owned by 74 shareholder First Nations, and the way its profits are distributed is central to why the year's numbers matter well beyond the gaming floor.

Where the money goes

Under the province's long-standing framework, SIGA's net income is divided along fixed lines. Half of the $167 million flows to the First Nations Trust, which distributes funds to all First Nations in Saskatchewan regardless of whether they host a casino. A further 25 percent — about $41.75 million this year — goes to the provincial General Revenue Fund, which supports health care, education and other public services. The remaining quarter is directed to Community Development Corporations tied to the communities where SIGA's casinos operate.

SIGA officials emphasized that shareholder First Nations decide how their share of the trust is spent, with priorities that typically include housing, infrastructure, education and social programs. Since the authority's inception in the mid-1990s, more than $1.9 billion has been returned to First Nations communities, community development corporations and the province combined — a cumulative figure that helps explain why SIGA is frequently cited as a model of the First Nations-owned gaming operator.

That distribution formula is deliberately broad-based. Because the First Nations Trust benefits every First Nation in Saskatchewan rather than only those that host a casino, the model spreads gaming proceeds across communities that would otherwise see none of the revenue. It is a design choice that turns a handful of casino sites into a province-wide funding stream, and it is part of why SIGA's results are treated as a matter of public interest rather than a private balance sheet.

SIGA's structure keeps ownership, regulation and benefit within First Nations hands while still sharing a defined portion of profits with the broader province — a balance many jurisdictions are still trying to strike.

A record year against a shifting market

The record was driven primarily by SIGA's land-based casinos, but the authority also pointed to a strong year for regulated online play in the province, a segment that has grown as Saskatchewan builds out its digital gaming offerings. That combination of steady bricks-and-mortar performance and emerging online revenue is increasingly the pattern for successful Indigenous operators across Canada.

It also arrives at a moment of real competitive pressure. As more provinces move toward open online gambling markets, land-based First Nations casinos face questions about how digital expansion will affect their core business. SIGA's results suggest that, at least for now, a disciplined operator with a clear revenue-sharing mandate can grow both channels at once.

Employment is the other side of the ledger. SIGA is one of the largest employers of Indigenous people in Saskatchewan, and its casinos anchor local economies in communities where alternative job opportunities can be scarce. A record revenue year translates into payroll, training and career pathways as directly as it translates into trust distributions — a point the authority has stressed throughout its 30th-anniversary messaging. Why it resonates beyond Saskatchewan

SIGA is often held up in policy discussions as evidence that gaming revenue, when governed by a transparent distribution formula and a broad ownership base, can function as a durable public-finance tool rather than a windfall for a single community. The fourth straight record year strengthens that case. With $1.9 billion returned since inception and half of each year's net income flowing to a trust that serves every First Nation in the province, the authority's 30th year underscores how deeply Indigenous gaming has become woven into Saskatchewan's fiscal fabric — and how much is riding on operators like it as the Canadian market moves online.

For observers across the border, SIGA's steadiness is instructive at a moment when many jurisdictions are still debating how much of gaming's proceeds should reach Indigenous communities and through what mechanism. The Saskatchewan answer — broad ownership, a fixed distribution formula and three decades of consistency — is not the only workable model, but the fourth consecutive record year makes it one of the most tested.
