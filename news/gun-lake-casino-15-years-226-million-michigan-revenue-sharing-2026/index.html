Gun Lake Casino Marks 15 Years, $226.9M in Michigan Revenue Sharing

A milestone check ceremony offers a window into how tribal-state revenue sharing actually flows to schools, services, and local budgets.

By Marcus WhitehorsePublished July 30, 20264 min read

Fifteen years after it opened its doors in Wayland, Michigan, the Gun Lake Casino has now channeled more than $226.9 million in revenue-sharing payments to the State of Michigan, county and township governments, local school districts, emergency services, and area charities. The figure, marked at a mid-July check ceremony, is more than a milestone number — it is a concrete look at how the abstract machinery of a tribal-state compact translates into line items on local budgets.

The casino is owned by the Match-E-Be-Nash-She-Wish Band of Pottawatomi Indians, widely known as the Gun Lake Tribe, which opened the property in February 2011 and has steadily expanded it through multiple phases since. The revenue-sharing total captures a decade and a half of payments tied to the tribe's exclusive right to offer certain classes of gaming in its region of the state.

How the money actually moves

Revenue sharing is one of the most misunderstood features of tribal gaming, and Gun Lake is a useful case study. As we explain in our revenue-sharing explainer, tribes are not taxed on gaming revenue the way commercial operators are. Instead, a tribal-state compact may provide that a tribe will share a percentage of certain electronic gaming revenue with the state in exchange for meaningful exclusivity — the state's promise not to authorize competing commercial gaming that would erode the tribe's market.

In Michigan, those payments are split. A portion flows to the state, and a distinct share is directed to a local revenue-sharing board that distributes funds to the governments and institutions nearest the casino — the townships, the county, school districts, and public-safety agencies that absorb the traffic, policing, and infrastructure demands of a regional entertainment destination. That local channel is why a check ceremony in Allegan County is as much a part of the story as any deposit into the state treasury.

Exclusivity is the product a state sells in a compact; revenue sharing is the price a tribe pays for it.

The exclusivity bargain, 15 years on

The durability of Gun Lake's payments reflects the durability of the underlying bargain. Because Michigan has not opened its market to widespread commercial casino competition outside Detroit, the exclusivity the tribe purchased has retained its value, and the tribe has continued to pay. That alignment is not guaranteed everywhere. In states that later authorized commercial casinos, sports betting, or online gaming, tribes have argued that the exclusivity they paid for was diminished — and revenue-sharing obligations have sometimes shrunk or lapsed as a result.

Michigan's broader tribal gaming market, which we map in the Michigan state hub, has navigated that tension comparatively well, in part because the state's tribes secured a seat at the table when online sports betting and iGaming were legalized rather than being displaced by them. The result is a market where compact-based revenue sharing has coexisted with new digital channels instead of being cannibalized by them — a dynamic we examine further in our Michigan market deep dive.

What the number represents

It is worth being precise about what $226.9 million is and is not. It is not the tribe's profit, nor the total economic footprint of the casino, which includes payroll, local purchasing, and the multiplier effects that ripple through the regional economy — the kind of impact quantified across the industry in our 2025 economic impact report. It is, specifically, the sum the tribe has voluntarily shared with governments other than its own under the terms of its compact.

That framing matters because tribal gaming's critics and champions often talk past each other on exactly this point. Tribes are governments, and gaming revenue funds tribal services — housing, health care, education, and infrastructure — first. Revenue sharing is what flows outward to state and local partners on top of that. Fifteen years of Gun Lake payments is a reminder that the two are not in competition; a healthy casino funds both the tribe's own priorities and its neighbors' budgets.

The milestone also lands at a moment when the value of exclusivity is being renegotiated across the country. As states weigh online casino legalization and prediction-market operators probe the boundaries of what counts as a regulated sports bet, tribes are watching closely to see whether the exclusivity they paid for will hold its value or quietly erode. Gun Lake's track record cuts against the anxious narrative: here is a compact that has delivered, year after year, without the market disruptions that have soured the bargain elsewhere. That stability is itself a data point in the national debate over whether tribal-state revenue sharing is a sustainable model or a relic of a pre-digital gaming era. For Michigan at least, fifteen years of payments suggest the model still works when the state honors its side of the deal.

For Michigan's smaller communities, the practical effect is tangible: fire equipment, road maintenance, classroom funding, and public-safety staffing underwritten in part by a casino that did not exist a generation ago. For the Gun Lake Tribe, the milestone is evidence that the exclusivity bargain it struck has held — and a marker of how far a single well-run property can carry a tribal government's ambitions.
