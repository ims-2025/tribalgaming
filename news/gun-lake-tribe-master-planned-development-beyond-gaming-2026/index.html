Gun Lake Tribe Builds a 1,200-Acre Economy Beyond Its Casino

A Michigan gaming tribe is assembling miles of highway frontage to build an economy that can outlast the casino that funds it.

By Marcus WhitehorsePublished July 25, 20264 min read

The Match-E-Be-Nash-She-Wish Band of Pottawatomi, better known as the Gun Lake Tribe, is moving beyond the casino floor. Fresh off a $300 million hotel and gaming expansion at Gun Lake Casino in Wayland, Michigan, the tribe has begun assembling a large master-planned district on land north of the property — a roughly 1,200-acre footprint with about 2.75 miles of highway frontage that could eventually hold retail, health care, housing and light manufacturing. It is one of the clearest examples yet of a mid-sized gaming tribe using casino cash flow to build a diversified, self-sustaining local economy.

The scale is deliberate. Rather than adding another gaming amenity, the Gun Lake Tribe is treating its US-131 corridor holdings as a decades-long real-estate platform — the kind of patient, mixed-use development that turns a single revenue engine into a regional economic hub.

From a single casino to a regional economy

Gun Lake Casino opened in 2011 as a modest Class III operation and has grown steadily into one of southwest Michigan's busiest properties. The 2025 expansion added a high-rise hotel tower, an expanded gaming floor, a spa and a multi-pool water attraction, cementing the site as a destination resort rather than a day-trip slots hall. That maturation is exactly what makes diversification possible: a stabilized gaming business throws off the predictable cash needed to underwrite non-gaming bets that may take years to pay off.

The tribe's diversification instinct is not unusual for Michigan's gaming nations, several of which have pushed aggressively into hospitality, real estate and operating companies to reduce their reliance on a single casino. What distinguishes the Gun Lake plan is the sheer land position — controlling miles of frontage lets the tribe shape an entire corridor rather than a single parcel, and it gives the nation leverage over the pace and mix of what gets built.

Why tribes are building past the casino

For tribal governments, gaming revenue funds services that other governments raise through taxes: health clinics, housing, education, elder care and public safety. But a single casino is a concentrated risk. Regional saturation, a recession, a new competitor across the state line, or a shift in player behavior toward digital products can all compress margins quickly. Non-gaming development spreads that risk and, crucially, creates assets and jobs that are not tethered to the gaming compact or to state exclusivity.

Diversification is no longer a defensive afterthought for successful gaming tribes — it is the core strategy for converting a finite gaming advantage into permanent economic infrastructure.

The categories the Gun Lake Tribe has floated — retail, health care, housing and manufacturing — track the broader wave of tribal economic diversification now reshaping Indian Country. Housing addresses a chronic shortage on and around many reservations; health care builds sovereignty over services; manufacturing and logistics create wage jobs that do not depend on casino foot traffic. Each also keeps dollars circulating locally rather than leaking to off-reservation vendors.

A long horizon, and real execution risk

Master-planned districts of this size take a decade or more to fill, and the Gun Lake project will unfold in phases as infrastructure — roads, water, sewer and power — is extended across the corridor. Financing large non-gaming projects can be harder than financing a casino, because lenders are more familiar with gaming cash flows than with speculative mixed-use real estate on trust or fee land. Tribes also navigate a distinct legal landscape around land status, taxation and jurisdiction that can complicate partnerships with private developers.

Land status adds another layer. Development on fee land is taxed and regulated like any private project, while land held in trust carries different jurisdictional and tax treatment that can attract or deter private partners. Deciding which parcels to place into trust, and when, is itself a strategic question that shapes what can be built where. Tribes with large, contiguous holdings like the Gun Lake corridor have room to sequence those decisions deliberately rather than react parcel by parcel, an advantage smaller land bases rarely enjoy.

There is also a workforce dividend. A casino employs dealers, hospitality staff and security; a mixed-use district adds construction trades, retail and medical jobs, and eventually manufacturing and logistics roles with different skill profiles and career paths. For a tribe investing in its own citizens' futures, that diversity of employment can be as valuable as the revenue, broadening the kinds of careers available close to home and reducing the pressure on members to leave the community for work.

Still, the direction of travel is unmistakable. The most successful gaming tribes increasingly measure themselves not by the size of their gaming floor but by how much of their government they can fund without it. If the corridor fills out as envisioned, the Gun Lake Tribe will have done something more durable than build a bigger casino: it will have used gaming to seed an economy that can outlast it.
