Nebraska Voters Reject Online Sports Betting: Tribal Ownership Lens

Both measures failed on November 3, leaving Nebraska sports wagering in person only. The result lands hardest on a tribally owned operator that helped lead the campaign.

By Elena RuizPublished November 07, 20265 min read

Nebraska voters rejected both online sports betting ballot measures on November 3, according to unofficial results reported by Nebraska and national outlets. Initiative Measure 440, the companion constitutional amendment, failed roughly 55 percent to 45 percent. Initiative Measure 441, which would have directed the Nebraska Racing and Gaming Commission to write rules for online sportsbooks, failed about 54 percent to 46 percent. Results remain subject to certification by the Nebraska Secretary of State.

The outcome matters for tribal gaming readers because one of the campaign's principal sponsors was a tribally owned company. WarHorse Gaming, Nebraska's largest casino operator, is owned by Ho-Chunk Inc., which in turn is owned by the Winnebago Tribe of Nebraska. The vote is a rare, clean test of how voters treat an online sports betting proposal when a tribal enterprise is among its backers.

What the measures would have done

Under the proposal, licensed casinos would have operated the sportsbooks, with each casino permitted up to two online platforms. Backers framed the revenue as a source of property tax relief. If voters had approved, the Racing and Gaming Commission would have needed to adjust its rules by June 1, 2027. Because the amendment and the statute were designed to work together, the defeat of both returns the state to the status quo: sports wagering is legal only in person at the racetrack-based casinos that voters authorized in 2020.

The pro-measure committee, Tax Relief Nebraska, raised close to $22.5 million, with national sportsbook operators supplying the largest checks. Opponents, including a group backed by former Cornhuskers coach Tom Osborne and several faith-based and family organizations, argued that putting wagering on phones raises addiction risks, particularly for younger bettors, and questioned whether promised tax relief would materialize. Senator Pete Ricketts, who was reelected the same night, publicly dismissed the property tax argument.

Why the tribal ownership structure matters

It is easy to read Nebraska as a typical tribal sports betting story. It is not, and the distinction is worth stating precisely. WarHorse's casinos in Lincoln and Omaha operate under racetrack licenses issued under Nebraska law, not under the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act. That is why this fight ran through the ballot box and the state regulator rather than through tribal-state compact negotiations. The measures as reported did not change anything about compact-based tribal gaming. Readers who want the legal background can consult our Legal Guide on IGRA and Class III gaming, which explains why gaming on trust land follows a different path from gaming a tribal enterprise conducts under a state license.

The ownership model nonetheless carries sovereignty-adjacent implications. Revenue earned by a tribally owned enterprise supports tribal government and member services, a point the company's backers emphasized. The campaign's defeat therefore represents a foregone growth channel for the Winnebago Tribe's business arm, even though it does not diminish any existing right. WarHorse is also building its next property, a roughly $100 million casino and hotel in South Sioux City, which we covered in our report on the WarHorse groundbreaking. Gaming at that site is not expected before late 2027, and an in-person-only sports betting regime means that facility's sportsbook, if it opens one, will depend on foot traffic.

Read-through for compact-based tribes

Tribes with compact-based sports betting in other states will study this result for signals about voter appetite. Three observations stand out. First, a well-funded campaign, backed by the largest national operators, was not enough to carry either measure; money did not translate into votes. Second, the property tax relief message, which has worked in some legislative debates, did not persuade a majority of Nebraska voters. Third, the vote shows that a locally owned operator at the table did not change the outcome, which should temper assumptions that tribal participation alone is a persuasive credential with the electorate.

We have tracked this pattern in our analysis of ballot measures and tribal gaming: tribes sometimes oppose such measures to protect exclusivity and sometimes sponsor them to open new markets. Nebraska was an example of the second pattern, and it did not succeed. In states where tribes hold exclusivity, including California and Oklahoma, the Nebraska result may be cited by advocates on both sides, either as evidence that voters are cautious about expanding mobile betting or as a reminder that campaigns financed by outside operators face an uphill climb.

What comes next

Two paths remain. Backers have previously indicated a preference for a legislative route if the ballot effort failed, so a bill in the next session is plausible, though the margin of defeat will give lawmakers pause. The Nebraska Legislature also faces pressure from the state's border geography: bettors in the Omaha metro can cross into Iowa, where mobile wagering is legal. That cross-border leakage is the strongest argument available to a future bill, and it is the same dynamic shaping debate in Oklahoma, as discussed in our Oklahoma state hub.

For now, the practical effect is limited. Nebraska's licensed casinos continue to take in-person wagers, WarHorse proceeds with its construction schedule, and no tribal compact is altered. Readers should confirm final figures with the Secretary of State once certification is complete.
