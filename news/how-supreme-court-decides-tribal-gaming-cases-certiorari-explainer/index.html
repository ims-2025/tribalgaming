How the Supreme Court Decides Whether to Hear a Tribal Gaming Case

Certiorari is discretionary, and most petitions are denied. Here is why a tribal coalition's amicus brief can matter before the justices decide anything.

By Sarah BegayPublished October 12, 20264 min read

When more than 140 Tribal Nations and organizations filed an amicus brief urging the Supreme Court to hear a sports contract dispute, they were participating in one of the least understood parts of American litigation: the certiorari process. Most people assume the Supreme Court hears appeals the way lower courts do. It does not. Understanding how the Supreme Court decides whether to hear a tribal gaming case explains why a brief filed before any decision on the merits can matter so much, and why the Court takes so few cases from the thousands it is asked to review.

What certiorari means

A petition for a writ of certiorari asks the Court to review a decision of a lower court. Unlike a first appeal, review is discretionary. The Court is not obligated to correct every error, and it generally denies petitions without explanation. By long-standing practice, the Court grants review when at least four of the nine justices vote to do so, a convention often called the rule of four. A grant does not signal how the Court will rule; it signals only that the question is worth deciding.

The Court's own rules identify the kinds of considerations that favor a grant. Among them is a conflict among federal courts of appeals on an important federal question, along with a state or federal court deciding an important question in a way that conflicts with Supreme Court precedent, or deciding an important federal question that the Court has not yet settled. Tribal gaming cases that reach the Court usually do so on questions of federal law, such as the reach of the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act or the scope of tribal sovereign immunity. Our explainers on Michigan v. Bay Mills and Ysleta del Sur Pueblo v. Texas show how earlier cases reached that stage.

Why amicus briefs matter at the petition stage

An amicus curiae, or friend of the court, is a non-party that files a brief to inform the justices about a case's wider significance. At the merits stage, amicus briefs often supply legal analysis. At the petition stage, their role is different and more practical: they help persuade the Court that a question is important beyond the parties. A brief signed by a large number of sovereign governments is a way to show breadth of impact. In the recent Kalshi-related filing, the coalition, represented by the Native American Rights Fund and Zimmer, Citron & Clarke LLP, argued that a conflict between the Ninth and Third Circuits requires national resolution; the details are in our report on the amicus filing.

The strength of such a brief does not rest on numbers alone. Justices and their clerks look for a clear explanation of the question presented, an accurate account of the lower-court split, and a concrete description of what is at stake. For tribes, that last element often concerns the dependence of tribal governments on gaming income for health care, education, housing and public safety, and the integrity of the compact system that governs Class III gaming.

What happens after a petition is filed

After a petition is filed, the opposing party may file a brief in opposition, and the petitioner may reply. The case is then considered at a conference of the justices. Outcomes include denial, which leaves the lower-court decision in place; a grant, which sets the case for full briefing and oral argument; and occasionally a summary disposition that sends the case back to a lower court. If the Court grants review, a decision on the merits normally follows within the same term or the next, depending on the timing of the grant.

For tribal gaming, the practical stakes of each outcome differ. A denial does not mean the Court approves of the decision below; it means only that the Court has chosen not to take the case now. A split between circuits that persists can lead to a later petition. A grant would produce a binding national rule. Tribes in states governed by the Ninth Circuit's California jurisdiction, for example, already operate under appellate reasoning favorable to them, while tribes elsewhere do not.

It is also useful to know what the Court does not do. It does not take new evidence or resolve factual disputes, and it ordinarily confines itself to the questions presented. That is why cases about compacts and IGRA are often framed as pure questions of statutory interpretation. The Legal Guide and our explainer on the preliminary injunction standard in tribal gaming litigation describe how earlier stages of such cases are handled. Together, these resources show why the road to the Supreme Court is long, narrow and, for most petitions, closed.

Two practical points round out the picture. First, timing: petitions and responses follow set deadlines, but the Court's own calendar determines when a petition is considered, and cases are often held or relisted before a final decision on review. Observers should expect weeks or months, not days, between a filing and an outcome. Second, interpretation: a denial of certiorari carries no precedential weight. The Court has repeatedly said that such an order is not a ruling on the merits, so commentators should avoid reading approval or disapproval into it.

For students of tribal law, the broader lesson is that the Court has shaped Indian gaming through only a handful of decisions over four decades, from Cabazon in 1987 to the immunity and compact cases that followed. Each arose from a specific dispute that satisfied the Court's criteria for review, which is why the next grant, if one comes, would be notable.
