Hannahville's Island Resort Adds a Third Course to Its Golf Complex

A nine-hole Golden Age design named Kishki extends one of the most ambitious golf build-outs at any tribal property in the Upper Midwest.

By Sarah BegayPublished September 16, 20265 min read

The Hannahville Indian Community has opened a third golf course at Island Resort & Casino in Harris, Michigan, extending a non-gaming build-out that has quietly made the Upper Peninsula property one of the most golf-heavy tribal casino destinations in the country. The Cedar Course, a nine-hole layout carrying the Potawatomi name Kishki, opened for limited preview play in 2026 with a full opening planned for the following season. It joins Sweetgrass and Sage Run to bring the resort to 45 holes.

The addition is modest in capital terms next to the hotel towers and gaming-floor expansions dominating tribal construction budgets this year, but it is strategically coherent in a way that larger projects sometimes are not. Island Resort sits in a rural county far from any major metropolitan area. Its competitive problem is not slot adjacency — it is giving guests a reason to stay a second and third night. Golf, in that context, is not an amenity. It is the demand driver.

What the Cedar Course adds

The new nine was designed by architect Paul Albanese, who also drew Sweetgrass and Sage Run, and is routed across gentler terrain adjacent to Sage Run. The stated design reference is Golden Age architecture — strategic width, land-driven routing, fewer forced carries — which positions it as the most playable of the three layouts rather than a third championship test. That is a deliberate portfolio decision. Sage Run trades on dramatic elevation change, Sweetgrass on rolling contour, and the Cedar Course on accessibility and pace.

The name matters as much as the design. Kishki means cedar in the Potawatomi language and refers to one of the four sacred medicines, associated with protection and purification. Tribal resorts have grown far more deliberate about carrying language and cultural meaning into guest-facing assets rather than confining it to lobby art, a shift we examined in our analysis of cultural design and Native artist commissions at tribal resorts.

The economics of golf at a rural tribal property

Golf is a capital-hungry, weather-exposed amenity with thin standalone margins. Very few destination courses cover their own cost of capital on green fees alone. What they do is change the composition and length of a visit. A golfer travelling to a 45-hole complex books multiple nights, arrives with a group, eats on property, and — critically for a casino operator — fills midweek and shoulder-season inventory that would otherwise sit empty.

The question is never whether the course makes money. It is whether the course makes the hotel, the restaurants and the gaming floor make money on nights they otherwise would not.

For an Upper Peninsula property, that calculation is sharpened by a short season. Michigan golf revenue is concentrated into roughly five months, which means the amenity has to convert aggressively while it is open. Adding a nine-hole option rather than a fourth full course is a reasonable hedge: it increases daily round capacity and creates an afternoon product for groups that have already played 18, without adding a full course's maintenance burden to the off-season cost base.

The broader pattern is familiar. Tribal operators have spent the past several years pushing capital into non-gaming revenue — convention space, entertainment venues, RV parks, outdoor recreation — partly to diversify away from slot-floor dependence and partly because guest expectations have shifted. We covered the national picture in our analysis of non-gaming amenity diversification at tribal casinos and the specific case for outdoor product in our look at RV parks and outdoor recreation demand.

Hannahville's long build-out

Island Resort & Casino is wholly owned and operated by the Hannahville Indian Community, a Potawatomi government in Menominee County. The property has expanded steadily rather than in single dramatic phases — hotel inventory, convention and event facilities, and golf added in sequence over roughly two decades. Sweetgrass opened first and drew national recognition; Sage Run followed as a contrasting design; the Cedar Course completes what is effectively a three-product golf portfolio aimed at different segments of the same visitor.

That incrementalism has a governance logic behind it. Phased development lets a tribal enterprise fund expansion substantially from operating cash flow and retained earnings rather than leveraging the balance sheet against a single large project, and it allows each phase to be tested against actual demand before the next commitment. For a mid-sized property in a thin market, that discipline is arguably more important than scale.

What it signals for the segment

Michigan has one of the more crowded tribal gaming markets in the Midwest, with twelve federally recognized tribes operating properties across the Lower Peninsula and the U.P., alongside commercial casinos in Detroit and a licensed iGaming market. Competition on the gaming floor alone is close to saturated, which is why differentiation has migrated to everything around it. Readers can review the state's operator landscape through our Michigan state hub.

The Cedar Course will not move Hannahville's revenue line by itself. What it does is deepen a moat that competitors cannot easily replicate: land, a consistent design voice across three courses, and two decades of reputation in the destination golf market. In a segment where most capital is chasing hotel rooms and gaming-floor refreshes, that is a less crowded place to compete.
