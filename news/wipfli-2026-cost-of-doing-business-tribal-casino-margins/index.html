Wipfli Report: Tribal Casino Revenue Up 16% as Margins Tighten

Average revenue rose $14 million per property, but operating expense ratios climbed faster — leaving 25 cents of every dollar for tribal government.

By Marcus WhitehorsePublished August 15, 20265 min read

Tribal casinos posted their strongest top-line growth in several years during 2025, but the gains did not reach the bottom line at anywhere near the same rate. Wipfli's 28th Annual Indian Gaming Cost of Doing Business Report, released on August 13, found that average casino revenue among surveyed operations climbed by roughly $14 million, or 16 percent, year over year, while average net profit margins slipped from 26.12 percent to 24.50 percent. The distance between those two figures is the clearest available summary of where tribal casino operating margins stand in 2026: demand is healthy, and the cost of serving it is rising faster than the revenue it produces.

The study is built on survey responses from 113 tribal casinos across 18 states, which makes it one of the broadest and longest-running benchmarking exercises in Indian gaming. Because participants submit comparable financial and operational data year after year, the report functions less as a headline number and more as a diagnostic tool — the place where tribal finance officers go to see whether their own payroll ratio, gaming tax load, or marketing reinvestment is normal for a property of their size.

Revenue up, expense ratio up faster

The mechanics behind the margin decline are unusually simple. Average operating expense margins rose from 73.59 percent of revenue to 74.50 percent. Slightly less than one percentage point of expense ratio does not sound dramatic, but applied against a revenue base that grew 16 percent, it was enough to convert a very strong revenue year into a merely good profit year. Wipfli attributed the revenue growth primarily to sustained customer demand and slot machine performance, and the expense growth to general inflationary pressure across labor, utilities, insurance, and goods.

That pattern matches what has been visible in the national aggregates. The National Indian Gaming Commission's fiscal 2025 figures put industry-wide gross gaming revenue at a record $46.2 billion, and operators across Indian Country have spent 2026 discussing the same tension: record handle alongside compressing returns. Our earlier look at margin compression across the 2026 outlook traced the same divergence from the operating side; the Wipfli data now supplies audited-quality benchmarks for it.

The composition of the expense growth matters more than the headline. Labor is the single largest controllable line at most tribal properties, and it has been the least controllable in practice — wage floors have reset in most regional markets, and properties that cut hours to protect margin generally pay for it in guest satisfaction scores within two quarters. Utilities and property insurance, meanwhile, are not controllable at all in the short run. What remains genuinely discretionary is marketing reinvestment, capital timing, and food and beverage subsidy, and those are precisely the levers finance officers have been pulling.

Twenty-five cents on the dollar

The report's most consequential finding for tribal governments is not the margin decline itself but what the remaining margin funds. Wipfli calculated that roughly 25 cents of every revenue dollar remains available to support tribal government programs, community services, infrastructure, and economic development. That is the number that converts casino performance into governmental capacity, and it is the reason margin compression in tribal gaming is a fiscal story rather than merely a corporate one.

Tribal gaming remains one of the strongest-performing sectors in hospitality and entertainment, but operators are facing a different challenge than they were a few years ago, said Grant Eve, partner and leader of Wipfli's tribal gaming practice. Customer demand remains strong, yet rising costs continue to pressure margins.

A commercial operator experiencing a 1.6-point net margin decline reports a softer quarter to shareholders. A tribal operator experiencing the same decline may be looking at a smaller transfer to the general fund, which flows through to health clinics, language programs, housing, and elder services. The permitted uses of net gaming revenue are fixed by statute, and the five categories IGRA allows are all governmental in character. That structural fact is why tribal finance departments track expense ratios with a seriousness that surprises operators coming from the commercial side.

Why the sector still outperforms

It is worth keeping the compression in proportion. A 24.5 percent average net margin remains well above what most of the hospitality and entertainment industry produces, and the report described tribal gaming balance sheets as broadly healthy. The sector arrived at 2026 with low leverage by commercial standards, meaningful cash positions at larger enterprises, and — critically — the ability to fund expansion from operations rather than exclusively from capital markets. That combination is what has allowed the current construction cycle to proceed even as costs rise.

The risk is not that margins fall below viability in the near term. It is that the cushion which historically absorbed shocks — a pandemic closure, a regional competitor opening, a compact renegotiation — is thinner than it was three years ago. Properties in the mid-market tier, roughly $25 million to $250 million in annual revenue, feel this most acutely, because they carry the fixed-cost structure of a full-service resort without the scale to spread it. What operators are likely to do

Three responses are already visible across the properties covered in this cycle. The first is expense discipline that targets structure rather than headcount: renegotiated vendor agreements, consolidated player-development spend, and energy retrofits with measurable payback. The second is revenue mix, with non-gaming amenities pushed to carry more of the load so that food and beverage stops functioning purely as a loss-leading traffic driver. The third is capital sequencing — phasing projects so that a single construction cycle does not coincide with the trough of a margin cycle.

None of those is novel. What is different in 2026 is that they are being pursued simultaneously and deliberately, by operators who no longer assume that revenue growth alone will restore the margin. Wipfli's framing is that the operators positioned to do well over the next decade are the ones actively managing expenses, investing strategically, and protecting the competitive advantages that built the sector. On the evidence of this year's benchmarking data, that has stopped being advice and started being a description of what the strongest tribal enterprises are already doing.
