Mohegan Pennsylvania Turns 20: A Tribe's First Casino Beyond Its Own Lands

Two decades after opening Pennsylvania's first casino at Pocono Downs, the Mohegan Tribe's racetrack property remains a case study in tribal gaming under a state license.

By Marcus WhitehorsePublished November 17, 20264 min read

Mohegan Pennsylvania marked its 20th anniversary on November 14, two decades after the Mohegan Tribe of Connecticut opened the state's first casino at a harness racing track outside Wilkes-Barre. The milestone is a useful moment to look at what the Mohegan Pennsylvania anniversary represents for tribal gaming more broadly: the property was the tribe's first gaming venture beyond its own lands, and it remains one of the longest-running examples of a tribal nation operating under a commercial state license rather than under the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act.

The casino opened on November 14, 2006, with just over 1,000 slot machines at the Pocono Downs harness track in Plains Township, Luzerne County. It was known for most of its history as Mohegan Sun at Pocono Downs and later Mohegan Sun Pocono before adopting its current name in 2022. Today the 400-acre property on Route 315 has more than 1,500 slot machines and electronic table games, nearly 60 live table games across roughly 80,000 square feet of gaming space, a 238-room hotel with a spa, a 20,000-square-foot convention center and live harness racing.

A tribal enterprise under a commercial license

The Mohegan Tribe's casino in Uncasville, Connecticut, operates on reservation trust land under a tribal-state compact and IGRA. The Pennsylvania property does not. The tribe's gaming enterprise acquired Pocono Downs in 2005, after Pennsylvania's 2004 gaming law authorized slot machines at racetracks, and the casino holds a license from the Pennsylvania Gaming Control Board like any other operator in the state. Pennsylvania regulators renewed that license for five years in 2024.

That distinction matters in practice. A tribal casino on Indian lands is regulated primarily by a tribal gaming regulatory authority, with oversight from the National Indian Gaming Commission and whatever role the compact assigns the state. Its revenue is governed by IGRA's net revenue rules, and any payments to the state flow from negotiated compact terms. A state-licensed casino owned by a tribe is a commercial licensee: it pays state gaming taxes at statutory rates, answers to the state regulator on licensing and compliance, and has no IGRA protections. Our explainer on state-licensed versus IGRA tribal-owned casinos walks through those differences.

Pennsylvania illustrates the cost side of that trade-off. The state's effective tax rate on slot revenue is among the highest in the country, at roughly 54 percent once state, local and horse-racing allocations are included. Compact revenue-sharing payments in tribal jurisdictions typically run far below that level. A tribe entering a commercial market accepts that tax burden in exchange for access to customers it could not reach from its own lands.

How the property has evolved

The Pennsylvania casino has expanded alongside the state's gaming laws. Table games arrived after Pennsylvania authorized them in 2010, and the property began offering sports betting in 2019 after the state legalized it. In 2022 the casino opened the Embers Terrace outdoor gaming area, which reopened this year after a refresh. In 2024 it completed a $10 million hotel renovation and added a smoke-free gaming area in partnership with Aristocrat Gaming.

The anniversary program reflected a regional property built on repeat local and drive-in visitation. Momentum rewards members competed in a $200,000 giveaway that ran from October 1 through drawings on the anniversary date, and country artist Ernest headlined a November 14 concert in the Keystone Grand Ballroom. The property also held appreciation events for employees with 20 years of service and for community partners, including a happy hour for local charities and nonprofits on November 12. Anthony Carlucci, the property's president and general manager, has described the celebration as recognition of guests, team members and community partners.

Part of a wider Mohegan portfolio

Mohegan Pennsylvania was the first step in a diversification strategy that has since made the Mohegan Tribe's gaming arm one of the most geographically spread tribal operators in North America, with interests that have reached Ontario, Las Vegas and South Korea. Our profile of Mohegan as a global tribal gaming operator traces that expansion.

The anniversary comes during a busy stretch at the tribe's Connecticut flagship, which is marking its own 30th year. Mohegan Sun Arena, the 10,000-seat venue in Uncasville, will close on December 15 for the main phase of a $30 million, multi-phase renovation that includes new seating, a refreshed concourse, upgraded food and beverage outlets, restroom and elevator updates and new digital displays. The arena is scheduled to reopen on February 28, with a 4,000-square-foot VIP lounge to follow in 2027. Mohegan Sun has also said it will begin a $22 million renovation of its Earth Tower hotel next summer. Coverage of the Connecticut market is collected on our Connecticut state hub.

What 20 years in Pennsylvania shows

When Mohegan entered Pennsylvania, tribal gaming beyond Indian lands was rare. Two decades later, it is an established strategy. The Poarch Band of Creek Indians operates Wind Creek Bethlehem in the same state, and other tribal enterprises hold commercial licenses in markets from Arkansas to Illinois to Nevada. For tribes with limited land bases or saturated home markets, a commercial license offers growth that IGRA cannot.

The Pennsylvania experience also shows the limits of that approach. A commercial licensee competes on the state's terms, carries the state's tax rate, and can see its operating environment change with each legislative session, from table games to sports betting to the online casino market Pennsylvania launched in 2019. The tribe's Connecticut property, by contrast, rests on a compact and on federal law that a single state legislature cannot rewrite.

That the Mohegan Tribe has kept a Pennsylvania racetrack casino growing through 20 years of those changes is a measure of how far tribal gaming enterprises have moved from reservation operators to multi-jurisdictional companies, while their home properties remain grounded in sovereignty.
