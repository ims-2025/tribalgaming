Ts'elxwéyeqw Tribe Acquires Elements Casino Chilliwack in B.C. Deal

Seven Chilliwack-watershed communities take over a commercial casino, the latest B.C. venue to pass into First Nations hands.

By Linda CardinalPublished August 01, 20265 min read

Great Canadian Entertainment has completed the sale of Elements Casino Chilliwack to a consortium led by the Ts'elxwéyeqw Tribe, a group of seven First Nation communities in the Chilliwack River watershed of British Columbia. The transaction, which closed at the end of July 2026, deepens a trend that has been reshaping the province's casino landscape: commercial operators steadily divesting land-based venues while First Nations step into ownership. The Chilliwack purchase is the latest chapter in a broader expansion of First Nations casino ownership across British Columbia, and it signals that the shift is no longer confined to the province's largest urban properties.

What the deal actually transfers

British Columbia's gaming framework is structurally different from the tribal model that governs Indian Country in the United States. The province, through the British Columbia Lottery Corporation, remains the legal conductor of gaming; private companies operate casinos on its behalf as contracted "service providers." When a First Nation acquires a property such as Elements Casino Chilliwack, it is therefore assuming the service-provider role — taking over management, staffing, and facility operations — rather than obtaining a sovereign gaming licence of the kind U.S. tribes hold under the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act. That legal distinction, explored in our explainer on the U.S. tribal versus Canadian First Nations gaming models, shapes how revenue flows and how much control the acquiring community ultimately holds.

In practical terms, the Ts'elxwéyeqw consortium gains the operating business of a mid-sized community casino: its gaming floor, food and beverage outlets, and the workforce that runs them. Revenue continues to be split according to British Columbia's established formula, under which the province retains gaming proceeds and distributes a share to host local governments and to First Nations through the province's revenue-sharing agreement. Ownership of the operating company gives the tribe a direct management stake and a claim on the operator's margin, alongside the community-benefit payments that already flow to First Nations under existing arrangements.

Part of a province-wide pattern

The Chilliwack deal does not stand alone. Over the past two years Great Canadian Entertainment has sold a string of British Columbia venues to Indigenous buyers, and the Snuneymuxw First Nation has emerged as one of the most active acquirers, assembling a portfolio that includes major properties such as River Rock Casino Resort in Richmond — the largest Indigenous-operated casino in the country, as detailed in our profile of the Snuneymuxw and Musqueam stake in River Rock. Other watershed and coastal nations have pursued similar transactions, and the cumulative effect is a marked change in who controls commercial gaming in the Lower Mainland and on Vancouver Island.

For a commercial operator, divesting a mid-market community casino frees capital; for a First Nation, acquiring one converts a venue that has long drawn spending from its territory into a community-owned asset.

That dynamic explains why the pace has accelerated. Commercial operators, facing softer land-based margins and rising competition from provincial iGaming platforms, have incentives to concentrate on their most profitable assets and shed smaller regional venues. First Nations, meanwhile, see acquisition as a faster route to a gaming revenue stream than pursuing a greenfield build through provincial approvals. Our analysis of how First Nations ownership is reshaping Canadian gaming traces how this buy-versus-build calculus has tilted toward acquisition.

The timing also reflects anxieties about where gaming revenue is heading. British Columbia's land-based venues, like their counterparts across the country, are contending with the migration of play to regulated online platforms, and First Nations leaders in several provinces have warned that the growth of provincial iGaming risks siphoning revenue away from the bricks-and-mortar casinos on which their communities depend. Acquiring a physical venue is, in part, a hedge: owning the operating business gives a nation both a current revenue stream and a seat at the table as the province weighs how digital and land-based gaming will coexist. For the Ts'elxwéyeqw communities, control of a casino in their own watershed is a tangible stake in that future rather than a passive royalty.

What comes next for Chilliwack

For the Ts'elxwéyeqw communities, the immediate priority is continuity. Community casino acquisitions in British Columbia have generally preserved existing staff and operations, and the province's service-provider rules require operators to meet the same regulatory standards regardless of ownership. The longer-term question is whether the consortium reinvests in the property — expanding non-gaming amenities, upgrading the floor, or adding hospitality capacity — to capture more of the regional market that Elements Casino Chilliwack serves in the eastern Fraser Valley.

The transaction also carries a symbolic weight that extends beyond its balance sheet. Casino revenue in British Columbia has flowed to First Nations for years under the province's revenue-sharing framework, but direct ownership places decision-making authority in Indigenous hands. As more properties change hands, the province's commercial gaming sector is coming to reflect a governance model in which host nations are not merely beneficiaries of gaming conducted on their traditional territories but the operators of it. The Chilliwack deal, modest in dollar terms, is another concrete step in that transition.
