Why Rhode Island Has No Tribal Casino: Narragansett Settlement and Carcieri

Between Foxwoods and Mohegan Sun to the west and state-run casinos at home, the Narragansett Indian Tribe's gaming story shows how Congress can rewrite IGRA one tribe at a time.

By Sarah BegayPublished November 25, 20264 min read

Rhode Island sits between two of the largest tribal casino markets in North America, yet the state has never had a Rhode Island tribal casino. The Narragansett Indian Tribe, federally recognized since 1983, has pursued gaming for more than three decades. Its path has been closed not by a failed compact negotiation alone but by a series of federal legislative and judicial decisions that, taken together, are among the clearest illustrations of how land status and settlement acts can override the general framework of the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act.

The 1978 settlement and its conditions

The modern story begins with the Rhode Island Indian Claims Settlement Act of 1978, which resolved the tribe's claims to land in Charlestown that had been taken without federal approval. In exchange for extinguishing those claims, the tribe received roughly 1,800 acres of settlement lands. The act also provided that those lands would be subject to the state's civil and criminal laws and jurisdiction, a concession common to Eastern land-claim settlements of that era. Our explainer on Indian land claims settlement acts explains why those provisions continue to shape gaming eligibility for tribes across New England and beyond.

When Congress passed IGRA in 1988, it did not expressly address how the new law interacted with earlier settlement acts. The Narragansett Tribe took the position that its settlement lands qualified as Indian lands eligible for gaming, and the question was litigated in the federal courts in the early 1990s, as the tribe sought a path to Class III gaming.

How Congress closed the IGRA door

In 1996, Congress resolved the question for this one tribe. A provision added to a federal appropriations measure amended the settlement act to specify that the settlement lands would not be treated as Indian lands for purposes of IGRA. The effect was to remove the tribe's existing land base from the federal gaming framework entirely, regardless of how a compact negotiation might otherwise have unfolded. A federal appeals court later upheld the amendment against the tribe's challenge.

The Narragansett experience demonstrates that IGRA is a floor set by Congress, and that Congress can carve individual tribes out of it through targeted legislation.

The tribe then turned to the state political process. In 2006, Rhode Island voters were asked to approve a constitutional amendment that would have authorized a casino in West Warwick to be developed by the tribe with Harrah's Entertainment. Voters rejected it by a wide margin. That outcome contrasted sharply with Connecticut, where the Mashantucket Pequot and Mohegan tribes built Foxwoods and Mohegan Sun under IGRA; their market is profiled on our Connecticut state hub.

Carcieri v. Salazar and the land-into-trust question

A second avenue would have been to acquire new land and have it taken into trust. In the late 1990s, the Interior Department agreed to take a 31-acre parcel in Charlestown into trust for tribal housing. Rhode Island challenged the decision, and in 2009 the U.S. Supreme Court ruled in Carcieri v. Salazar that the Secretary's authority under the Indian Reorganization Act extends only to tribes that were "now under federal jurisdiction" when the act was passed in 1934. Because the Narragansett Tribe was not federally recognized until 1983, the Court held that the Secretary lacked authority to take the parcel into trust under that statute.

The ruling did not involve gaming directly, but its consequences for gaming were significant. It foreclosed the most common route to new trust land for the tribe and introduced a threshold question for every tribe recognized after 1934. Interior has since issued, withdrawn and reinstated guidance on how to determine whether a tribe was under federal jurisdiction in 1934, and legislative proposals to restore the Secretary's authority for all tribes have repeatedly stalled. Our analysis of Carcieri and 1934 jurisdiction covers that continuing fight, and our explainer on congressional land-into-trust acts describes the legislative workaround some tribes have pursued.

A state-run market instead

Rhode Island's gaming market developed through the state rather than a tribe. The Rhode Island Lottery controls video lottery terminals and table games at the two casinos in Lincoln and Tiverton, now operated by Bally's, and the state runs sports wagering through the Lottery as well. That model gives the state a direct revenue share without IGRA compacting, a structure similar in some respects to the provincial Crown model used in Canada.

The Narragansett case is often cited alongside Maine, where a separate settlement act limited the Wabanaki Nations' access to IGRA for decades. Both show that the national tribal gaming picture is not uniform: for some tribes, the controlling law is a tribe-specific statute rather than IGRA itself. Readers interested in how these layers fit together can consult the TribalGaming Legal Guide, which outlines IGRA's general framework and its exceptions.

For the Narragansett Indian Tribe, any future gaming path would likely require congressional action, either restoring IGRA's application to its lands or clarifying the Secretary's trust authority after Carcieri. Until then, the tribe remains one of the clearest examples of a federally recognized nation with a deep historical claim to its homelands but no practical route to the gaming economy that transformed its neighbors.
