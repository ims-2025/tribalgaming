Haaland Win Puts a Former Interior Secretary Over New Mexico's Compacts

The first Native woman elected governor will inherit a mature compact market, an open question on mobile betting and pueblo-led prediction-market litigation.

By Daniel YazziePublished November 23, 20264 min read

Deb Haaland's election as governor of New Mexico places a former U.S. Secretary of the Interior, and a citizen of the Pueblo of Laguna, on the state side of New Mexico's tribal gaming compacts. Haaland, a Democrat, defeated Republican Gregg Hull, the former mayor of Rio Rancho, on November 3, winning about 57 percent of more than 720,000 votes in unofficial results, a margin of roughly 14 points. She is the first Native American woman elected governor of any U.S. state and is scheduled to be sworn in on New Year's Day.

Haaland gave her election-night speech at Route 66 Casino Hotel, on Laguna Pueblo land west of Albuquerque. Her campaign emphasized health care, early-childhood programs and literacy, and she has not set out a detailed gaming agenda. But the office she is entering carries direct responsibility for the state's government-to-government relationship with the tribes and pueblos that operate New Mexico's casinos.

The New Mexico tribal gaming compacts she inherits

New Mexico's tribal gaming market is mature and compact-driven. As we described in our New Mexico market deep dive, more than a dozen tribes and pueblos operate roughly two dozen casinos, concentrated along the Interstate 25 corridor near Albuquerque, with Apache and Navajo properties serving more regional markets. The current Class III compacts run through 2037, giving both sides an unusually long planning horizon.

Those agreements exchange revenue sharing with the state for exclusivity over casino-style gaming. Because the terms are fixed for more than a decade, a new governor has limited occasion to reopen them unless a tribe asks to, or unless a new product, such as mobile sports wagering, requires an amendment. The mechanics of that process are covered in our Legal Guide to IGRA and Class III compacts.

That second scenario is already on the table. New Mexico offers in-person sports betting at tribal properties but has no statewide mobile framework. In June 2026, state Rep. John Block urged gaming tribes to consider reopening their compacts to add online wagering, a proposal we covered in our report on calls to reopen New Mexico's compacts. Any such change would have to be negotiated tribe by tribe, approved by the Legislature under state law, and reviewed by the Interior Department under the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act.

A regulator's perspective on the other side of the table

What distinguishes Haaland from most incoming governors is that she has already sat in the federal chair that reviews compacts. During her tenure as Interior Secretary, the department finalized revised land-into-trust regulations in late 2023 and revised its Class III compact review regulations at 25 CFR Part 293 in late 2024. The Part 293 rule addressed, among other things, how Interior evaluates revenue-sharing provisions and compact terms involving wagers placed by patrons who are not physically on Indian lands. Interior also allowed the 2021 Seminole Tribe of Florida compact, which included a hub-and-spoke sports wagering model, to take effect by operation of law; that framework is discussed in our Seminole compact analysis.

For the first time, a governor negotiating Class III terms will have previously overseen the federal review of those same kinds of agreements.

That experience does not determine how she will approach New Mexico's compacts. A governor represents the state's interests in negotiations, and those interests include revenue sharing that flows to the general fund. But tribal leaders and state officials alike will be negotiating with a counterpart who knows the federal standards for what a compact may and may not contain, including the longstanding Interior position that revenue sharing must be justified by meaningful concessions such as exclusivity.

Prediction markets and the exclusivity bargain

The most active gaming issue in New Mexico is not in Santa Fe but in federal court. The Mescalero Apache Tribe and the Pueblos of Isleta, Pojoaque and Sandia have sued prediction-market operators, arguing that sports event contracts offered to people on tribal lands amount to unlicensed gaming that violates IGRA and undercuts compact exclusivity. The state is not a party, but the dispute bears on the value of the bargain the state struck with tribes: if wagering-like products can reach New Mexico residents outside the compact system, the exclusivity that justifies revenue sharing is worth less.

How a Haaland administration responds is an open question. Governors and attorneys general in several states have taken public positions on event contracts, and state officials have weighed in through amicus briefs and enforcement letters elsewhere. Whether New Mexico's executive branch joins that effort, and whether it coordinates with tribes in doing so, will be one of the first signals of how the new administration treats gaming policy.

What to watch in 2027

The Legislature's 60-day session begins in January, shortly after the inauguration. Three items bear watching for tribal gaming. The first is whether the administration signals interest in mobile sports wagering and, if so, whether it frames the issue as a tribal-led expansion or invites a broader debate. The second is appointments to the Gaming Control Board and the governor's tribal liaison roles, which shape day-to-day compact administration. The third is the state's posture on prediction markets as the pueblo litigation moves forward.

New Mexico's compacts were built to provide stability, and nothing in the election changes their terms. What changes is the experience and perspective of the official on the other side of the table, and the 2027 session will be the first test of what that means in practice.
