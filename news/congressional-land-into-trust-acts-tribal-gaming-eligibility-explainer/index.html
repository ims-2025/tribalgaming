How Congressional Land-Into-Trust Acts Decide Tribal Gaming Eligibility

When Congress, not the Interior Department, decides a parcel's status, the 1988 cutoff, litigation risk and gaming rules can all be written into the statute.

By Linda CardinalPublished November 16, 20265 min read

Most new tribal casino sites reach gaming eligibility through the Interior Department: a fee-to-trust application, environmental review, and, for land acquired after 1988, a determination that the parcel fits one of the exceptions in the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act. But there is a second route, used less often and with far more finality. Congress can pass a statute that places specific land into trust, defines its status, and says directly whether gaming may occur there. This explainer covers how congressional land-into-trust acts work, why tribes pursue them, and what they can and cannot settle for tribal gaming.

The problem Congress is solving

The starting point is Section 20 of IGRA, codified at 25 U.S.C. § 2719. It generally prohibits gaming on land taken into trust for a tribe after October 17, 1988, the date IGRA was enacted. The statute then lists exceptions, including land within or next to a tribe's 1988 reservation, land acquired as part of a land claim settlement, a tribe's initial reservation, restored lands for restored tribes, and the two-part determination, which requires findings by the Interior Secretary and the governor's concurrence. Our explainer on Section 20 exceptions covers each in detail.

The administrative path is slow and frequently litigated. Neighboring governments, competing tribes and commercial operators can challenge trust decisions under the Administrative Procedure Act, and the Supreme Court's 2009 decision in Carcieri v. Salazar, which limited the Secretary's trust authority under the Indian Reorganization Act to tribes "under federal jurisdiction" in 1934, added another ground for challenge. A tribe can win approval and still spend years defending it in court.

Congress is not bound by those administrative rules. Because IGRA is itself a federal statute, a later act of Congress can direct the Secretary to take land into trust, declare it part of a reservation, and specify how IGRA applies, including by overriding the 1988 cutoff for that land.

How a land act is built: the Old Pascua example

The Pascua Yaqui Tribe's new Casino Del Sol, Vahi Taa'am in Tucson, which opened on November 15, sits on land that reached gaming eligibility this way. The Old Pascua Community Land Acquisition Act, Public Law 117-275, was signed on December 27, 2022, after passing the House in November 2021 and the Senate in December 2022. Its structure shows the main tools Congress uses.

First, it defines the land. Rather than listing parcels, the Act covers tribally owned land within a "Compact-Designated Area" in Tucson bounded by West Grant Road, Interstate 10, West Calle Adelanto and North 15th Avenue, an area defined by reference to the tribe's 2021 amended gaming compact with Arizona. Tying the statute to the compact aligned federal land status with the state's agreement on where the tribe could build.

Second, it makes the trust acquisition mandatory. At the tribe's request, the Secretary "shall" accept qualifying land into trust, subject to valid existing rights. That removes the discretionary decision that normally invites APA litigation.

Third, it sets the land's legal history. Land taken into trust under the Act becomes part of the Pascua Yaqui Reservation and is treated as having been acquired and taken into trust on September 18, 1978, the date of the federal statute that recognized the tribe. Because that date precedes October 17, 1988, Section 20's general prohibition does not reach the land.

Fourth, it addresses gaming expressly. Section 4 provides that gaming conducted by the tribe in the area is subject to IGRA and to 18 U.S.C. §§ 1166 through 1168, the federal criminal provisions that apply state gambling law in Indian country and penalize theft from tribal gaming establishments. The Act also includes rules of construction stating that it does not affect existing water rights, existing rights-of-way, or the tribe's ability to seek other trust land.

A land act does not exempt a casino from regulation. It settles where gaming may occur; IGRA, the compact, the National Indian Gaming Commission and the tribe's own regulators still govern how.

Other uses: reaffirmation and prohibition

Congress has also used land legislation to protect trust status after the fact. In 2014, after a neighbor's challenge to the trust land under the Gun Lake Tribe's casino in Michigan reached the Supreme Court, Congress passed the Gun Lake Trust Land Reaffirmation Act. It ratified the trust acquisition and directed that suits challenging the land's status not be filed or maintained in federal court. The Supreme Court upheld the law in Patchak v. Zinke (2018). Our report on the Gun Lake casino's first 15 years describes the property that statute protected.

Land acts can cut the other way as well. Congress frequently attaches a gaming prohibition to trust legislation, providing that land taken into trust under the act may not be used for Class II or Class III gaming. Those provisions are often the price of passage when local governments or other tribes are concerned about a new casino, and a tribe accepting land under such an act cannot later rely on IGRA's exceptions to game there.

Not every effort succeeds. Bills to reaffirm the Mashpee Wampanoag Tribe's reservation in Massachusetts passed the House in 2019 but did not become law, leaving the tribe to pursue its trust status through the administrative and judicial process.

Tradeoffs for tribes

The advantages of a congressional route are finality and speed once a bill passes: a statute is far harder to challenge than an agency decision. The disadvantages are political. A tribe needs a sponsor, committee action in the House Natural Resources and Senate Indian Affairs committees, floor time in both chambers, and a presidential signature, and bills that fail in one Congress must start over in the next. Opposition from a single senator can stall a bill indefinitely, and the price of support may include gaming restrictions, revenue terms or local agreements written into the text.

For that reason, legislative trust acquisitions tend to work best where local support is already in place, as in Tucson, where the city entered an intergovernmental agreement with the tribe, and where the land has a clear cultural or historical connection.
