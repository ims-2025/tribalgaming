Smoking at Tribal Casinos: Why State Indoor-Air Laws Don't Apply

Tribal casino smoking policy is set by tribal governments, not state legislatures. A guide to the law, the trade-offs and the models in use.

By James KingfisherPublished November 22, 20264 min read

Smoking policy at tribal casinos is one of the clearest everyday illustrations of tribal sovereignty in gaming. In most states, indoor smoking in casinos is either banned outright or restricted by state law. On tribal lands, those state laws generally do not apply, and the decision about whether guests can smoke on a casino floor belongs to the tribal government that owns the property. This explainer covers why state indoor-air laws stop at the reservation boundary, where tribal-state compacts and tribal ordinances come in, and how tribes weigh the choice between smoking, partial smoke-free and fully smoke-free operations.

Why state indoor-air laws generally do not apply to tribal casinos

Federally recognized tribes are sovereign governments, and states have only the authority over tribal lands that Congress has granted. Congress has never extended state public-health or workplace smoking laws to Indian country. Even in states covered by Public Law 280, which transferred certain criminal and civil jurisdiction to some states, the Supreme Court's decision in California v. Cabazon Band of Mission Indians drew a line between criminal-prohibitory laws, which may apply, and civil-regulatory laws, which do not. A statute that permits smoking in some places and restricts it in others is regulatory in character, which is why state clean indoor air acts are generally not enforced on tribal casino floors. Our explainer on Public Law 280 and state jurisdiction covers that distinction in more detail.

The Indian Gaming Regulatory Act does not address smoking. IGRA governs who may conduct gaming, how it is classified and regulated, and how revenue may be used, but it leaves facility operations such as air quality to the tribe and, where relevant, to the terms of a tribal-state compact. Federal agencies likewise have no general rule prohibiting smoking in casinos. The question of which federal laws reach tribal enterprises at all is addressed in our explainer on how federal laws apply to tribal casinos.

Where compacts and tribal ordinances come in

Because IGRA allows tribal-state compacts to cover standards for the operation and maintenance of gaming facilities, smoking can in principle be a subject of negotiation. In practice, compacts that mention the issue tend to do so through general health-and-safety provisions or commitments about ventilation and non-smoking areas, rather than through outright bans. States have rarely made a smoking prohibition a condition of a compact, and tribes have generally treated air-quality policy as an internal operational decision. The overall layering of tribal, federal and state oversight is described in our guide to who regulates tribal casinos and in the site's Legal Guide to IGRA and Class III gaming.

Tribes that choose to restrict smoking typically do so in one of three ways. Some enact a tribal law or ordinance that applies across tribal government buildings and enterprises, including casinos. Others adopt the policy at the enterprise level through a gaming board or management decision. A third group builds smoke-free areas into facility design, using separate rooms, dedicated ventilation, or physically distinct gaming spaces, while leaving the broader policy unchanged.

The legal question is settled in most places; the practical question of what to do with that authority is where tribes differ.

How tribes weigh smoke-free gaming

The considerations are largely the same ones any casino operator faces, with some that are specific to tribal governments.

Revenue and competition. Many operators have long believed that a meaningful share of high-frequency slot players smoke, and that a ban risks pushing that play to competitors. Where a tribal casino competes with other properties that permit smoking, the concern is sharper. Others argue that smoke-free floors attract guests who avoid smoky environments and improve non-gaming business such as dining, hotel stays and events. Evidence has been cited on both sides, and results appear to vary by market. Our analysis of smoke-free tribal casinos and revenue performance looks at how several properties have fared.

Workforce and health. Tribal casinos are often among the largest employers in their regions and employ many tribal citizens. Exposure to secondhand smoke is a workplace health issue, and tribal health departments and employee advocates have increasingly raised it in policy debates. Tribal governments, which also run health programs for their citizens, frequently weigh those costs alongside enterprise revenue.

Culture. Many tribal health advocates distinguish between traditional and ceremonial uses of tobacco, which hold cultural significance in many Native communities, and commercial cigarette smoking. Smoke-free casino policies typically address commercial tobacco and do not restrict ceremonial use.

Capital and design. Converting a large floor to smoke-free operation, or building separate smoke-free areas, can require significant investment in ventilation, layout and renovation. That is why smoke-free changes are often announced alongside broader renovations.

The range of models in practice

Tribal casinos today span the full spectrum. Some went fully smoke-free when they reopened after pandemic closures in 2020 and kept the policy. Others have expanded non-smoking floors in steps. Choctaw Casino & Resort in Durant, Oklahoma, has unveiled what the operator calls the largest non-smoking gaming floor in the U.S., as covered in our report on Choctaw Durant's non-smoking floor. In Florida, Seminole Hard Rock Hotel & Casino Tampa completed a $30 million renovation in November 2026 that created a smoke-free route through the resort while keeping designated smoking areas, an example of the hybrid approach.

For players, the practical takeaway is that a tribal casino's smoking policy cannot be inferred from state law. It is set by the tribe and can differ from one property to the next, even within the same state. For policymakers and industry observers, the issue is a reminder that tribal gaming operations are governed first by tribal law, and that changes in areas such as smoking come from tribal governments' own decisions rather than from state legislatures.
