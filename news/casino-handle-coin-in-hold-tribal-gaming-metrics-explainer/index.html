Handle, Coin-In, Hold and Win: Reading Tribal Casino Metrics Explained

The words casinos use to describe money moving across the floor are precise, distinct, and easy to confuse. Here is what each one means.

By James KingfisherPublished August 12, 20264 min read

Read any report on tribal gaming performance and you will encounter a cluster of terms that sound interchangeable but are not: handle, coin-in, drop, hold, and win. Each measures a different thing, and confusing them can badly distort what a casino's numbers actually say. This explainer walks through the core metrics operators use to describe money moving across the floor, why the distinctions matter, and how they connect to the revenue figures that ultimately fund tribal governments.

Handle and coin-in: how much was wagered

The most misunderstood term is handle. Handle is the total amount wagered — not the amount lost, and not the amount the casino keeps. On the slot side, the equivalent term is coin-in: every dollar (or credit) cycled through a machine, including winnings that a player immediately re-wagers. Because a single $20 bill can be bet, won back, and bet again many times in a session, coin-in on a machine routinely dwarfs the actual cash a player brought to the floor. A slot might report enormous coin-in while the casino's real revenue from it is a small fraction of that figure.

This is why handle, on its own, is a measure of activity rather than profit. A sportsbook that reports a billion dollars in handle has taken a billion dollars in total bets; what it keeps is far smaller. Treating handle as revenue is one of the most common errors in reading gaming data, and it is the reason careful analysts always pair it with a second number.

Drop, win and hold: how much the casino keeps

On table games, drop refers to the total value of cash and chips that players exchange for play at a table — a rough proxy for the money brought to that table, captured in the locked box beneath it. Win is what the casino actually keeps after paying out winning bets. Win is the number that matters for revenue: it is the house's gross gaming take from that game or floor over a period.

The ratio between the two is the hold percentage — win divided by drop (for tables) or win divided by coin-in (for slots). Hold expresses how much of the money that flowed through a game the casino retained. A slot machine's theoretical hold is set by its programmed payback: a machine built to return 92% of coin-in to players over the long run has a theoretical hold of about 8%. Actual hold fluctuates in the short term because of variance, but over millions of plays it converges on the theoretical figure.

Handle tells you how busy a game is. Win tells you what the casino earned. Hold tells you the relationship between the two.

Why the distinctions matter

These metrics are not just industry jargon; they determine how a tribal gaming operation is understood and governed. The figure that flows up into a tribe's finances — and into the government programs that gaming revenue supports — derives from win, not handle. It is closely related to gross gaming revenue, the standardized measure we break down in our explainer on gross gaming revenue. Regulators, too, rely on these measures: accurate tracking of coin-in, drop, and win is central to the internal controls that tribal gaming commissions and the federal regulator enforce, a system outlined in our overview of how the NIGC regulates tribal gaming.

Two further terms round out the picture and explain how casinos value individual players. Theoretical win — often shortened to "theo" — is the amount a casino expects to earn from a given player based on how much they wager, how long they play, and the house advantage of their game, rather than what they happened to win or lose on a particular visit. Average daily theoretical, or ADT, applies the same idea over time and is the number hosts rely on when deciding how much to reinvest in a patron through comps and offers. It is why a player who loses on a given night can still be highly valuable to the casino, and why reinvestment decisions are made on expected long-run play rather than short-term results. These concepts sit beneath the high-limit and loyalty strategies that increasingly define competition among tribal resorts.

Understanding the vocabulary also guards against being misled. A press release touting record handle may describe a property that is busier but no more profitable; a modest-sounding win figure may reflect a highly efficient operation with a healthy hold. When comparing properties or evaluating a market's health, the meaningful comparisons are win and hold, adjusted for the size and mix of the floor. For readers who want to see how these concepts fit into the broader legal and regulatory framework of Indian gaming, our Legal Guide connects the operational metrics to the statutes that govern them, and the directory shows the properties where those numbers are generated. Once the terms are clear, the data stops being a wall of interchangeable-sounding words and starts telling a coherent story about how a casino actually performs.
