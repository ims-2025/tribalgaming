How tribes finance billion-dollar casino resorts

Sovereign borrowers, tax-advantaged bonds and a hard cost environment: the mechanics behind the industry's resort building boom.

By Daniel YazziePublished July 21, 20266 min read

Behind every billion-dollar tribal resort is a financing structure most casino guests never see. When a tribe announces a project measured in the hundreds of millions or more, the interesting question is not just what it will build but how it will pay for it — and the answer says a great deal about how far tribal casino project finance has matured over three decades of Indian gaming. Tribes today borrow at scale, in public markets, as sovereign governments, and they have learned to do so on terms that protect the sovereignty that made their gaming possible in the first place.

The core challenge is straightforward. A destination resort is enormously capital-intensive, and few tribes can fund one from cash on hand. Yet tribes are also wary of any arrangement that hands operational control or a large share of profits to outside parties. The financing tools that dominate the industry today are, in effect, answers to that tension — ways to raise large sums while keeping the gaming enterprise under tribal control and the proceeds flowing to the governmental purposes that federal law requires.

The main sources of capital

Most large tribal projects are funded through some blend of a few instruments. The first is debt raised in the capital markets, often structured as gaming revenue bonds secured by the future cash flow of the enterprise rather than by a mortgage on tribal trust land — a critical distinction, since trust land generally cannot be pledged or foreclosed on the way ordinary real estate can. Because tribes are governments, some of their borrowing can carry tax-advantaged treatment for certain governmental purposes, lowering the cost of capital relative to a comparable commercial developer.

The second source is bank and institutional debt: term loans and revolving credit facilities from lenders that have grown comfortable underwriting tribal gaming cash flows over the years. The third, especially for tribes newer to gaming or building their first major property, is a management or development partner. Under the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act, management contracts are tightly regulated — the National Indian Gaming Commission must approve them, fees are capped, and terms are limited — precisely so that outside expertise and capital can be brought in without a tribe surrendering ultimate control of its enterprise.

The defining feature of tribal gaming finance is that it must raise commercial-scale capital while preserving governmental control — a balance ordinary corporate borrowers never have to strike.

Sovereignty shapes every deal

What makes tribal finance distinct is that the borrower is a sovereign. That status cuts both ways. On one hand, it enables tax-advantaged governmental borrowing and reflects the reality that gaming revenue, by law, funds public services — the five permitted uses of net gaming revenue range from tribal government operations to member welfare, economic development and charitable giving. On the other hand, sovereign immunity complicates a lender's remedies if a deal goes bad. Tribes routinely grant limited, negotiated waivers of immunity to make financing possible, but the scope of those waivers is itself a point of careful negotiation, because it touches the sovereignty at the heart of the enterprise.

The collateral question is equally distinctive. Because trust land cannot be mortgaged in the conventional sense, lenders look to the revenue stream, to enterprise assets, and to structural protections rather than to the land itself. This is why the health of the underlying operation matters so much to a tribal project's cost of capital: the cash flow is, in a real sense, the collateral. The scale and stability of that cash flow across the industry is documented in our 2025 economic impact report, and it explains why established operators can borrow on far better terms than first-time developers.

A harder environment for new borrowers

The current construction-cost environment has sharpened the divide between operators. Elevated materials and labor costs have pushed resort budgets higher across the country, and projects that pencil out comfortably for a well-capitalized tribe can strain the finances of a smaller one. The result is a widening execution gap — visible in markets such as California, where an ambitious pipeline is running into schedule and delivery pressure, as we detailed in our analysis of the state's resort delivery crunch.

Established tribes with strong existing cash flow, diversified enterprises and a track record with lenders can absorb cost inflation, refinance opportunistically and still access favorable terms. Tribes pursuing a first major project face the same rising costs without the same financial cushion or borrowing history, which can translate into higher interest rates, more conservative lenders and greater reliance on management partners. That spread — visible across the range of operators in our tribal casino directory — is one of the quiet forces determining which of today's announced projects actually get built.

The through-line is that tribal gaming finance has become sophisticated capital-markets work performed by sovereign governments under a body of federal law designed to keep control in tribal hands. As the industry's building boom continues into a high-cost era, the tribes that understand and command these financing tools will be the ones that turn announcements into open doors — and the ones that translate gaming revenue into lasting community assets.
