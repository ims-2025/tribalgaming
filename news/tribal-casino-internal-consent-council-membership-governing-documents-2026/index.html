Before the Compact: How Tribal Governing Documents Gate Casino Decisions

Two Upper Midwest and Southeast projects this fall illustrate a point outsiders often miss: the first approval a tribal casino needs is the tribe's own.

By Elena RuizPublished October 19, 20264 min read

Public attention to tribal casino projects tends to follow the federal and state milestones: a Bureau of Indian Affairs decision, a governor's concurrence, a compact signing. Yet in practice the first and often most consequential approval a tribal casino needs is internal. Tribal governing documents determine who may adopt a gaming ordinance, who may commit tribal assets, and whether members must vote. Recent events involving the Lumbee Tribe of North Carolina and the White Earth Nation of Minnesota show how that internal layer shapes timing, risk and credibility.

The constitution comes first

The Indian Gaming Regulatory Act presumes that a tribe has a functioning mechanism to adopt a gaming ordinance, and it requires the ordinance to meet federal content standards. It does not supply that mechanism. That is left to each tribe's constitution, bylaws or other governing documents, which vary widely: some vest ordinance-making power in a tribal council, some require membership referenda for certain actions, and some are silent on the question.

The Lumbee experience this year is a clear illustration. As we reported in June, an independent legal review commissioned by tribal leadership concluded that the existing constitution lacked a workable path for approving a gaming ordinance, and the proposed fix addressed procedure rather than policy. Even so, the amendment did not carry at the ballot box. The lesson was that members were weighing more than drafting mechanics; for many, the vote was a referendum on whether casino gaming fits community priorities.

The tribe then pursued a different route. Draft ordinance text went through committee meetings and a series of public hearings, and on Friday the Tribal Council approved a gaming ordinance 14-2. Speaker Alex Baker described the next question as one that "belongs to the Lumbee People," a reference to further member involvement that local reporting has not detailed. The sequence demonstrates that internal consent is not a single event. It can be a constitutional question, a legislative question and a membership question, each with its own standard of legitimacy.

Why internal consent matters to outside reviewers

Federal reviewers and state negotiators do not adjudicate a tribe's internal politics, but they care about whether an action was validly taken. The National Indian Gaming Commission reviews a gaming ordinance as an instrument of the tribe, and an ordinance adopted through a contested or procedurally uncertain process invites challenge. Our explainer on gaming ordinance approval outlines what the commission examines.

Lenders and vendors apply the same logic. A tribal enterprise that borrows against future gaming revenue must show that the borrowing was authorized under tribal law, a point that has become more prominent as financing costs have risen. A project that later faces a membership dispute over authority can find itself delayed or refinanced on worse terms. The internal record, in other words, is part of the project's collateral.

Authorization versus consent

The White Earth Nation offers a complementary example. Its proposed Moorhead casino was paused in August under new tribal leadership, which cited long-term financial risk, effects on existing casinos and concerns about transparency. The nation then held a member information meeting before advancing a federal application. At an October 13 briefing for Clay County, officials said the tribal council has approved the site but that the fee-to-trust application itself still needs formal authorization, as detailed in our report on the narrowed 80-acre trust bid.

That distinction between approving a site and authorizing an application is worth noting. Tribal governments often take decisions in stages, and each stage can draw on a different source of authority: a council resolution, a committee recommendation, an executive action or a member vote. Treating a single announcement as final can overstate where a project actually stands. For observers, the practical question is always which body acted, under which provision, and what remains.

The accountability dimension

There is also a sovereignty argument embedded in this process. A tribe's authority to game rests on its inherent governmental powers, affirmed in IGRA, and the legitimacy of casino revenue as government revenue depends on how decisions are made. Member participation is not a federal requirement, but where governing documents or political practice demand it, it functions as a check on the concentration of enterprise risk. Off-reservation projects are the most capital-intensive undertakings a tribe pursues, and enterprise revenue that backs them would otherwise fund services or distributions. Members carry the downside if projections fail, which is why leadership changes can reverse a project's direction, as White Earth's has.

The contrast with commercial casinos is instructive. A corporate board answers to shareholders on a defined cycle; a tribal government answers to citizens who are also the program's beneficiaries and its regulators' stakeholders. Our Legal Guide covers how that distinction shapes the regulatory structure.

What to take from it

Readers tracking tribal casino proposals can use a simple checklist. Identify what the governing document says about gaming ordinances and asset commitments. Note whether the action in question was taken by the council, by the membership or by an executive. Distinguish between approval of a concept, a site, an ordinance and a financing. And recognize that a rejected measure, a paused project or a narrowed application is often a sign of a functioning internal process rather than a failure of one.

The federal and state approvals that follow will continue to draw the headlines. But as the Lumbee and White Earth cases show, they follow a series of decisions that only the tribes themselves can make.
