Public Law 280 and Tribal Casinos: Who Has Criminal Jurisdiction?

Criminal jurisdiction on Indian lands depends on the state, the tribe and the compact. Here is how Public Law 280 and IGRA fit together.

By Daniel YazziePublished October 06, 20264 min read

When a crime occurs at a tribal casino, who has the authority to investigate and prosecute it? The answer depends on where the casino sits, who committed the offense, and what the tribe and state agreed to in their compact. Public Law 280 and tribal casinos are linked in ways that surprise many readers, because a 1953 statute and a 1988 gaming law operate on the same ground and sometimes point in different directions.

This explainer sets out the basic framework. It is general information, not legal advice, and individual situations turn on specific facts and agreements.

What Public Law 280 does

Congress enacted Public Law 280 in 1953 to transfer certain criminal and civil jurisdiction over Indian country from the federal government to a group of states. The original mandatory states were California, Minnesota (with an exception for the Red Lake Reservation), Nebraska, Oregon (with an exception for the Warm Springs Reservation) and Wisconsin, and Alaska was added in 1958. Other states could opt in to varying degrees under the statute as originally written, and later amendments required tribal consent for new assumptions of jurisdiction.

In practice, in those states the state government prosecutes most criminal offenses committed in Indian country, including on casino property, rather than the federal government. In states not covered by the statute, jurisdiction is divided differently: the federal government generally prosecutes major crimes and certain offenses involving Indians, tribes prosecute members for lesser offenses, and states retain authority over crimes between non-Indians. The map is a patchwork, and the same casino-floor incident can fall under different authorities depending on state and parties.

Cabazon and the birth of IGRA

Public Law 280 became central to gaming through the Supreme Court's 1987 decision in California v. Cabazon Band of Mission Indians. California had tried to apply its bingo regulations to tribal operations on the strength of the statute. The Court distinguished between state laws that are criminal and prohibitory, which Public Law 280 allows states to enforce in Indian country, and those that are civil and regulatory, which it does not. Because California permitted bingo and cardrooms under regulation rather than prohibiting them outright, the Court held that the state could not enforce its regulations against the tribes. Our Cabazon explainer covers the case in detail.

Congress responded the following year with the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act. The statute created the three classes of gaming and the compact requirement for Class III, partly as a compromise to give states a role that Cabazon had denied them.

How IGRA changes the criminal picture

IGRA includes a provision, codified at 18 U.S.C. section 1166, that makes state laws governing the licensing, regulation or prohibition of gambling applicable in Indian country as though they were federal law, with exceptions for activities conducted under the Act. The same provision gives the federal government exclusive jurisdiction over prosecuting violations of those state gambling laws, unless a tribal-state compact transfers that jurisdiction to the state. In other words, a compact can allocate enforcement authority over gambling offenses to the state, to the tribe or to the federal government, depending on what the parties negotiate.

Two further federal statutes matter at the casino. Section 1167 addresses theft from gaming establishments on Indian lands, and section 1168 addresses theft or embezzlement by officers, employees or licensees of gaming establishments. These give federal prosecutors tools for cash-handling crimes that fall outside ordinary tribal or state authority.

The layering means that a single event can implicate several systems. A violent offense on casino property is generally governed by the underlying criminal jurisdiction rules, which depend on whether Public Law 280 applies, who the parties are and where the incident occurred. A gambling-specific offense such as cheating or illegal gaming is governed by section 1166 and the compact. A theft by an employee may involve federal prosecutors, tribal authorities or both. For regulators, this is why tribal gaming commissions, surveillance departments and security staff typically coordinate with several agencies.

Why it matters to patrons and operators

For visitors, the practical effect is usually invisible. Security staff handle routine issues, tribal or state officers respond to serious incidents, and compacts often set out how agencies cooperate, including information sharing and law enforcement agreements. But the legal rules shape how disputes are handled. Civil claims against a tribal enterprise, for example, raise separate questions of sovereign immunity, which we address in our explainer on patron disputes.

For operators and regulators, jurisdiction determines who investigates, who prosecutes, and how evidence moves between agencies. In Public Law 280 states such as California, where many of the largest tribal casinos operate, state prosecutors and local law enforcement play a larger role than in non-280 states. Readers exploring individual properties can consult the California state hub and the Legal Guide for related background.

The key takeaway is that there is no single national rule. Criminal jurisdiction at a tribal casino is the product of a 1953 statute, a 1987 Supreme Court decision, a 1988 federal law and, often, the specific compact the tribe signed with its state. Anyone assessing a particular incident should look at all four layers.
