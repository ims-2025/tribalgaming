Tlingit & Haida Open Two Coppers Casino, Juneau's First Gaming Hall

Juneau's first casino runs Class II machines on a Native allotment — a modest hall with outsized implications for Alaska Native gaming rights.

By Sarah BegayPublished August 14, 20265 min read

The Central Council of the Tlingit & Haida Indian Tribes of Alaska has opened Two Coppers Casino, Juneau's first casino, capping a years-long effort to bring tribal gaming to a state where such operations remain rare. The gaming hall sits on North Douglas Island at 2270 Fish Creek Road and offers Class II electronic play, but its most consequential feature may be the ground beneath it: the casino is built on a Native allotment, a land status that places it at the center of unresolved questions about Alaska Native jurisdiction.

Tlingit & Haida opened the venue in two stages. A soft opening on June 3, 2026, brought roughly 100 slot-style electronic machines online, and the tribe followed with a formal grand-opening celebration in early July that drew tribal officials, Alaska Native dancers and more than a hundred attendees. For a state with almost no tribal casino footprint, the debut of even a modest 100-machine hall is a milestone.

The years-long path to opening is itself part of the story. Tribal gaming projects in Alaska have historically foundered on the twin obstacles of land status and legal uncertainty, and Two Coppers reached its ribbon-cutting only after sustained effort by tribal officials and the family associated with the allotment on which it sits. That persistence is why the grand opening carried an emotional weight beyond the commercial: for many attendees it represented the culmination of a long campaign to establish that tribal gaming has a legitimate place in Alaska at all.

Why Alaska is different

Most tribal gaming in the Lower 48 operates on reservation land or on parcels the federal government holds in trust for a tribe. Alaska's land picture is distinct. The 1971 Alaska Native Claims Settlement Act extinguished most aboriginal land title and conveyed acreage to Native corporations in fee, leaving the state with limited "Indian country" as courts have defined it. That history is why gaming has been slow to take hold, and why the legal footing of any Alaska tribal casino draws immediate scrutiny. We examine the underlying dispute in our analysis of ANCSA and the shifting jurisdictional landscape for Alaska Native gaming.

Two Coppers is situated on a Native allotment — an individual parcel held under a federal restriction on alienation rather than corporate fee title. Under the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act, gaming is permitted on "Indian lands," a category that can include restricted allotments, but applying that framework in Alaska is contested terrain. Observers have flagged the casino as a potential test case that could clarify — or unsettle — the boundaries of tribal gaming authority in the state. For readers new to these land categories, our explainer on Native allotments and gaming eligibility lays out how restricted parcels fit within IGRA.

A 100-machine hall on North Douglas Island may look small, but its location on a Native allotment turns Two Coppers into a live question about how far tribal gaming rights extend across Alaska.

Class II gaming and the road ahead

The casino offers Class II gaming — electronic bingo-based machines regulated primarily by the tribe and the National Indian Gaming Commission rather than through a state compact. That distinction matters in a state that has not negotiated Class III compacts of the kind common elsewhere. Tribes that operate Class II floors can do so without a state agreement, which is part of why the format has become a foothold in jurisdictions where compacting is difficult. The mechanics of that divide are covered in our guide to Class II versus Class III tribal gaming.

Two Coppers is not the only recent Alaska development testing these limits; the broader trend includes expansion efforts near Anchorage documented in our reporting on the Eklutna Chin'an gaming hall and its jurisdictional questions. Together, these projects suggest Alaska Native tribes are prepared to build first and litigate the boundaries later, rather than wait for every legal question to resolve.

The economic stakes for the community are concrete. Juneau, Alaska's capital, has a seasonal, tourism-heavy economy, and a year-round tribal enterprise adds jobs and a revenue stream that does not ebb when the cruise ships leave. Tlingit & Haida is one of the largest tribal governments in the state, serving tens of thousands of citizens, and gaming proceeds are directed toward tribal programs rather than private shareholders. Even a 100-machine hall, sustained over time, can underwrite services that a tribe would otherwise have to fund through grants or federal appropriations.

Tlingit & Haida has also paired the opening with public commitments on responsible gaming, pledging safeguards around problem gambling as it scales the operation. That posture reflects an industry-wide expectation that new tribal facilities arrive with harm-reduction measures built in, not bolted on later — a standard that also helps insulate a young operation from criticism while its legal footing is tested.

For Tlingit & Haida, the immediate significance is economic and symbolic: a tribally owned revenue source in a community that had none, and a demonstration that Alaska Native gaming can move from aspiration to operation. The larger significance will depend on how regulators and courts respond to a casino whose legal foundation rests on a single allotment. Whatever the outcome, Two Coppers has already changed the conversation about where tribal gaming can exist in Alaska.
