Osage Nation's Missouri Resort Waits on a Single Trust Decision

Five years after the application was filed, Missouri's first tribal casino is held up by one signature at the Department of the Interior.

By Sarah BegayPublished August 19, 20264 min read

The Osage Nation's proposed Lake Ozark casino resort has completed every approval within the reach of local government and now waits on a single federal decision. All municipal agreements between the tribe and the City of Lake Ozark have been executed, covering revenue-sharing terms, a hotel resort fee, water and sewer service, and infrastructure coordination. What remains is the Department of the Interior's decision on whether to take the 28-acre site into federal trust — the step that would make the parcel eligible for gaming under the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act.

The Osage Nation, headquartered in Pawhuska, Oklahoma, filed its trust application in October 2021 and announced the project in late that month. Nearly five years later, the file remains open. The Bureau of Indian Affairs released a Draft Environmental Assessment in 2025, and the trust determination is the last major action required before site work can begin.

A Class II resort, by necessity

The project's design reflects a legal constraint rather than a commercial preference. Missouri has no tribal-state gaming compact, so a casino on newly acquired trust land there would operate under Class II authority — bingo and electronic bingo-based games regulated by the tribe and the National Indian Gaming Commission, without the table games and conventional slot machines that a Class III compact would authorize.

The preferred plan calls for a 237,160-square-foot building with a 40,000-square-foot Class II gaming floor holding 750 machines, a 150-room hotel tower, five restaurants, meeting and event space, entertainment venues, a pool and fitness center. Development is structured in phases, with the casino, restaurants, sports bar and meeting space in the first phase and the hotel and resort amenities following. City officials have indicated the tribe may open a temporary gaming facility immediately after federal approval while the permanent resort is built — the same interim strategy several tribes have used to accelerate market entry, examined in our analysis of the temporary and interim casino model.

Missouri does not have a tribal-state gaming compact, so the resort will operate under Class II gaming as permitted by federal law — a structural choice that shapes the property's economics before the first machine is installed.

What the delay costs

The site sits near the intersection of Bagnell Dam Boulevard and Osage Beach Parkway in Miller County, positioned to serve the Lake of the Ozarks tourism corridor. The Osage Nation has already cleared the property, selecting a demolition contractor in early 2022 to remove a vacant motel and accessory structures.

Local consent was not always assured. In June 2023 the City of Lake Ozark Board of Aldermen split evenly on a resolution supporting the project, and the mayor's vote defeated it. Public meetings in Camden County that year featured extended debate over the benefits and risks of a casino in the Lake of the Ozarks corridor. That the tribe has since executed a full set of municipal agreements — revenue sharing, resort fee, utilities and infrastructure — marks a genuine reversal of local sentiment, and it is the kind of host-community groundwork that federal reviewers weigh. Our explainer on municipal services agreements covers how these arrangements are typically structured.

Project cost estimates have ranged from $60 million to $100 million across the life of the application, a spread that itself illustrates the problem: construction pricing has moved substantially since 2021, and a development held in federal review for five years is repriced repeatedly against a budget approved under earlier conditions. That escalation risk is a recurring theme in current tribal development, discussed in our coverage of construction cost inflation and the labor squeeze.

An established operator entering a new state

The Osage Nation is not a first-time developer. The tribe operates seven casinos across Oklahoma — in Bartlesville, Hominy, Pawhuska, Ponca City, Sand Springs, Skiatook and Tulsa — giving it an operating base and balance sheet that many tribes pursuing off-reservation projects lack. The Lake Ozark development represents an expansion across state lines into a jurisdiction with no existing tribal gaming presence, in a market where commercial riverboat casinos have operated for three decades.

Missouri's existing gaming market is entirely commercial. The state has licensed riverboat-style casinos since the mid-1990s, with a cluster around St. Louis and Kansas City and none in the central lake region. A Class II property at Lake Ozark would not displace an incumbent so much as open a market segment the commercial licensing framework never reached, which is part of why local opposition softened over time.

Missouri would join a small group of states where tribal gaming exists without a compact framework, operating entirely on the Class II side of the line. For readers unfamiliar with that distinction, our explainer on Class II versus Class III gaming sets out what each authority permits, and the Oklahoma directory covers the Osage Nation's existing properties.

No groundbreaking date has been announced. The tribe has said it intends to begin construction immediately upon approval, with an 18-to-24-month phased build. Until Interior acts, that timeline has no starting point.
