Cherokee Nation Opens Stilwell Head Start, Final of Seven Centers

The 9,300-square-foot center closes out a multi-year capital program and shows how tribal governments turn enterprise income into community infrastructure.

By Sarah BegayPublished November 03, 20264 min read

The Cherokee Nation has opened a $6.7 million Head Start facility in Stilwell, Oklahoma, completing the last of seven projects funded under the tribe's $80 million early childhood education initiative. The ribbon-cutting and dedication took place on May 6, 2026. The project illustrates a question that runs through the economics of Indian Country: how tribal governments translate enterprise income, including income from gaming, into long-lived public infrastructure for their citizens. This article examines what the Stilwell Head Start facility is, how the underlying legislation works, and why it matters for the Cherokee Nation's model of tribal government services.

The Stilwell facility in detail

The new building covers 9,300 square feet and is designed to serve nearly 20 children from nearby communities. It replaces two older facilities, one in Stilwell and one in Cherry Tree, consolidating services into a single modern site. The design includes classrooms with heated floors, an enclosed outdoor playground with a hanging garden, and an indoor gross motor room that doubles as a FEMA-rated storm shelter, a practical feature in a region that experiences severe weather.

Principal Chief Chuck Hoskin Jr. said early Head Start and Head Start programs can change children's life paths and strengthen their communities. Deputy Principal Chief Bryan Warner said it is "a blessing to see all these little Cherokees learning in a safe, warm and loving environment." District 7 Councilor Joshua Sam noted that council members unanimously supported the underlying act and said families and children will benefit for generations.

The Verna D. Thompson Early Childhood Education Act

The facility was built under the Verna D. Thompson Early Childhood Education Act of 2021, which was reauthorized in 2024 through legislation proposed by Chief Hoskin and Deputy Chief Warner. The act provides $80 million to replace or rehabilitate aging Head Start centers across the reservation. It is named for Verna Thompson, a Head Start director who has worked in early childhood education with the Cherokee Nation for nearly 40 years.

Stilwell is the final of seven projects under the act. The other completed facilities are in Tahlequah, Jay, Salina, Nowata, Pryor and Kenwood. The Cherokee Nation's Head Start program, which began in 1978, serves more than 900 children from six weeks to five years old. The federal Head Start program, established in 1965, marked its 60th anniversary in May 2025 and, according to the reporting, serves more than 40 million children nationally, with over half of its facilities located in rural communities.

The phrase "replace or rehabilitate" is significant. Head Start centers are often housed in aging buildings that are expensive to maintain and may not meet current safety expectations. By funding capital replacement directly, the tribe reduces the burden on program operating budgets, which are typically supported by federal Head Start grants that do not generally cover large construction costs.

How gaming enterprises fit into tribal government services

The announcement does not itemize the funding sources for the $80 million, so this article does not attribute the money to any specific revenue stream. It is nonetheless relevant to explain the general framework. Under the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act, net gaming revenue from tribal operations must be used for specified purposes, including funding tribal government operations and programs, providing for the general welfare of the tribe and its members, promoting tribal economic development, donating to charitable organizations and helping fund the operations of local governments. Our explainer on IGRA's five permitted uses of net revenue lays out those categories in detail.

Many large tribes operate gaming and diversified enterprises through business entities that distribute profits to the tribal government, which then budgets those dollars for services such as housing, health care, education and infrastructure. The Head Start example is a useful corrective to the assumption that gaming income primarily flows to individuals. While some tribes make per capita payments, many direct substantial resources toward community assets. Why early childhood infrastructure matters

Early childhood programs are among the most widely cited investments for long-term community outcomes, and in rural reservation communities, access to quality facilities can be limited. A modern facility with safety features such as a storm shelter reduces risk and can improve program capacity and staff retention. The consolidation of two older sites into one also reflects an operational logic: fewer, better-equipped buildings can be easier to staff and maintain.

The completion of all seven projects also marks a transition from construction to operations. The tribe must now sustain staffing and program quality across the new facilities, and enrollment will depend on demographics and family demand in each community. The announcement states that the Stilwell building will serve nearly 20 children, a modest number that underscores the small, local scale of many rural Head Start sites.

For readers following tribal economies in the state, the project fits within a broader Oklahoma pattern of tribal governments serving as major providers of education, health and infrastructure. See the Oklahoma state hub for operators and compacts across the state, and our 2025 economic impact report for national context on tribal gaming's economic footprint.

In short, the Stilwell Head Start opening is a concrete example of tribal self-governance in action: a tribal legislature authorizes a multi-year capital program, a tribal administration delivers it, and communities gain durable facilities. The financial details behind the act are not itemized in the reporting available, but the outcome, seven completed centers across the reservation, is a measurable result of the Cherokee Nation's investment priorities.
