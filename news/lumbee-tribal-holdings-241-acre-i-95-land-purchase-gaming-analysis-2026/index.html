Lumbee's I-95 Land Purchase: What the Parcel Means for a Casino Path

The 241-acre Robeson County tract at the center of the Lumbee gaming debate raises a separate legal question from the tribal vote: whether and how the land could ever host Class III gaming.

By James KingfisherPublished October 31, 20266 min read

Public attention on the Lumbee Tribe's casino effort has centered on votes: a failed constitutional amendment in June, a 14-2 Tribal Council vote in October, and a referendum expected in December. A less visible question sits underneath all of them. Even if the Lumbee people allow the gaming ordinance to stand, a casino requires land that qualifies for gaming under federal law. This analysis looks at what is publicly known about the 241-acre parcel near Interstate 95 in Robeson County, and what the Lumbee gaming land question depends on.

What is known about the acquisition

According to reporting by the North State Journal on October 30, Lumbee Tribal Holdings (LTH) bought a 241-acre tract near Interstate 95 in Robeson County in December 2025 for $6.8 million. The purchase came shortly before the tribe received full federal recognition. The same report says Wyoming-based Western Agricultural Holdings LLC had acquired the same parcels for $3.2 million days earlier, a difference that drew scrutiny. The report adds that an independent legal review found no wrongdoing and concluded that LTH did not know Western Ag's acquisition cost when it negotiated the deal.

Those are the reported facts, and they should be kept separate from interpretation. A price gap between two sequential sales is a common subject of questions about governance and procurement, particularly when the buyer is a tribal entity spending tribal funds. The independent review's conclusion addresses the process, not whether the price was ideal. For readers, the practical point is that the transaction is now part of the political record around the gaming question, and members voting in December may weigh it alongside the ordinance itself.

Owning land is not the same as gaming on it

The legal distinction that matters most for the parcel is between land a tribe owns and land on which the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act allows gaming. IGRA permits tribal gaming only on Indian lands, a term the statute defines by reference to reservations and to land held in trust or subject to restrictions on alienation over which a tribe exercises governmental power. Our explainer on the IGRA definition of Indian lands walks through that definition, and the companion piece on fee land versus trust land explains why a parcel purchased on the open market is not automatically eligible.

A tribe that buys land in fee simple holds it much as any landowner would, subject to state and local law. To make it eligible for gaming, the tribe generally must ask the Secretary of the Interior to take the land into trust, a process involving environmental review and consultation, as we described in our explainer on NEPA review for fee-to-trust applications. Even then, gaming on land acquired after October 17, 1988 is generally prohibited unless an exception applies, such as the exception for lands taken into trust as part of the restoration of lands for a tribe that has been restored to federal recognition. Whether any exception fits the Lumbee parcel is a question for the Interior Department and, if contested, the courts. We are not aware of a public determination on that point, and this article does not predict one.

Why recognition timing matters

The sequence of events adds a layer. The reported purchase came shortly before recognition, which means the tribe acquired the land while it lacked the federal status needed to apply for trust acquisition. Congress later granted recognition, as reported in our coverage of the certified ordinance and December referendum, and that status is a precondition for pursuing gaming under IGRA. Our guide to federal recognition and gaming eligibility describes why recognition opens the door but does not by itself authorize a casino.

Buying land before recognition is not unusual in itself, since tribes seeking to build an economic base often acquire property when it becomes available. The relevant analytical questions are narrower: how the tribe will document that the parcel meets a statutory exception, how local governments and neighboring tribes respond, and how long the trust process takes. Processing times can be substantial, a point we explored in our analysis of fee-to-trust timelines.

The other requirements still ahead

Land is one of several gates. The ordinance that members will consider creates a regulator, the Lumbee Gaming Commission, and an operating entity, the Lumbee Gaming Authority, and the commission portion requires review by the National Indian Gaming Commission. For Class III gaming, which includes slot machines and banked table games, the tribe would also need a compact with North Carolina. The 45-day federal review window that follows submission of a signed compact is explained in our piece on approval by operation of law. No tribal vote can stand in for any of these steps.

The June vote shows the stakes of public confidence. About 62 percent of voters rejected the constitutional amendment, and opponents raised governance concerns alongside religious objections, as we reported in our account of the June rejection. A land transaction that has been questioned may deepen that debate regardless of its legal merits, which is why the independent review's findings are likely to be cited by both supporters and skeptics.

What to watch

Three developments would clarify the picture. The first is any formal fee-to-trust application and the legal theory it invokes. The second is the Election Board's rules for the December referendum, including how the 30 percent participation threshold for rescission is applied. The third is any signal from North Carolina officials about compact discussions. Until then, the parcel is best understood as an asset with potential rather than a site with approval.
