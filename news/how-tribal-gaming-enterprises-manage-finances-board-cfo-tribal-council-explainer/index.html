How Tribal Gaming Enterprises Manage Money: Board, CFO and Tribal Council

Gross revenue becomes government services through a chain of financial decisions that is set by IGRA, tribal law and enterprise governance.

By James KingfisherPublished October 24, 20266 min read

When a tribal casino reports revenue, the figure is only the start of a longer financial chain. Tribal gaming finance, in the sense of how money moves from the casino floor to the tribal government and its members, is governed by federal law, tribal ordinances and the governance structure of the gaming enterprise itself. This explainer sets out the main pieces: the enterprise board, the chief financial officer, the tribal council, and the legal rules that decide where net revenue can go.

Three layers of decision-making

Most larger tribal gaming operations are organized as a tribally owned enterprise or corporation rather than as a department of the tribal government. The structure varies by nation, and the details are set by tribal law and governing documents, so no single description fits every tribe. In a common arrangement, a board of directors oversees the enterprise and appoints management; the tribal council or other governing body remains the owner's representative; and the enterprise's executives, including the chief executive and chief financial officer, run the business day to day.

The CFO's role sits at the junction of those layers. A recent example is the appointment of a new finance chief at Seneca Resorts & Casinos, whose announced duties include budgeting, financial reporting, general accounting and purchasing, plus monitoring liquidity and capitalization and making recommendations to the board. Our report on the Seneca CFO appointment describes that role. The same pattern, a finance chief reporting results and recommendations to an enterprise board, appears across the industry.

What IGRA says about net revenues

The Indian Gaming Regulatory Act limits how a tribe may use net revenues from Class II and Class III gaming. Under the statute, those revenues are to be used for specified purposes: funding tribal government operations or programs, providing for the general welfare of the tribe and its members, promoting tribal economic development, donating to charitable organizations, and helping fund operations of local government agencies. Per capita payments to members are permitted only under a revenue allocation plan approved by the Secretary of the Interior, and those payments are subject to tax. Our explainers on the revenue allocation plan and per capita payments and on how tribal casino revenue is taxed cover those rules in more detail.

For readers who want the statute's structure in context, the Legal Guide sets out how IGRA treats the classes of gaming and the role of the National Indian Gaming Commission.

The financial controls around the enterprise

Several oversight mechanisms shape what the finance function can do. First, tribal gaming operations are subject to annual independent audits under NIGC regulations, and the audit results are part of the oversight record; see our explainer on the annual independent audit requirement. Second, minimum internal control standards govern cash handling, accounting and reporting at the operational level, which tribal gaming regulators enforce; our piece on tribal gaming commissions and MICS explains how. Third, where a tribe uses an outside management company, IGRA requires NIGC review of the management contract, discussed in our explainer on IGRA management contracts.

Compacts add a further layer in Class III gaming. Many tribal-state compacts include revenue-sharing or fee provisions that are a cost to the enterprise before any distribution to the tribe. Those payments differ widely among states, as our comparison of revenue sharing in Arizona, Connecticut and Oklahoma shows, and they are one of the main variables a finance team models.

Capital, debt and reinvestment

A tribal gaming enterprise typically weighs three competing uses of cash: distributions to the tribal government, reinvestment in existing properties, and debt service on borrowing for expansion. Compact term length affects lenders' willingness to finance, a subject we examine in our analysis of compact term length, credit and capital planning. Tribal sovereign immunity also shapes lending, because lenders and vendors usually rely on limited, negotiated waivers of immunity; our explainer on limited sovereign immunity waivers covers that point.

The outcomes show up in tribal budgets. Gaming revenue supports services such as water, health, education and housing, as described in our analysis of how tribal gaming revenue funds government services. Industry-wide, nearly 250 tribal governments operate gaming facilities in 29 states and generated more than $46 billion in gross gaming revenue in fiscal year 2025, according to figures cited in recent Supreme Court filings.

What this means for readers

Understanding the chain helps in reading any headline about a tribal operator. A change in revenue does not translate one-for-one into a change in distributions, because compact payments, regulatory costs, operating expenses, debt service and reinvestment come first. Conversely, a new executive hire, a compact amendment or a capital project each acts on a different link.
