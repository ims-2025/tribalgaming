Washington Tribal Gaming Compact Amendments: Appendix Changes Add Up

From restated compacts to single-appendix memoranda, Washington's amendment model moves wager limits, device caps and player protections.

By Daniel YazziePublished October 16, 20264 min read

Washington State's approach to tribal gaming compacts has produced a steady stream of amendments rather than a few large renegotiations. Two recent items illustrate the range: a comprehensive restated compact for the Puyallup Tribe of Indians, announced as a tentative agreement in July 2025, and a narrow Appendix E revision for the Stillaguamish Tribe that took effect on October 14, 2026. Read together, they show how Washington tribal gaming compact amendments accumulate into substantive policy, even when each individual filing looks technical.

The comprehensive model: Puyallup's seventh amendment

On July 16, 2025, the Washington State Gambling Commission announced a tentative agreement with the Puyallup Tribe on a seventh amendment to its Class III compact. The amendment restates the original 1996 compact and folds in amendments one through six, producing a single current document. According to the Commission, the proposed changes include a framework for electronic table games, an extension of credit with the removal of per-facility limits, and $30 wagers on tribal lottery system player terminals. It would allow table wagers up to $1,000, and up to $5,000 at limited tables after customer screening, and it includes an option to add 1,000 player terminals to the tribe's allocation through a stepped approach.

The same package added new appendices on tribal licensing and state background provisions, clarified community support commitments to emergency services, government and non-profit organizations, required a non-smoking room if smoking is allowed in the facility, and strengthened responsible gaming provisions covering training, self-exclusion, signage and self-imposed limits. This article describes the tentative agreement as announced; it does not assert the amendment's final outcome, and readers should check the Federal Register and the Commission's records for the compact's current status.

The incremental model: Stillaguamish's Appendix E

At the other end of the spectrum, the Stillaguamish filing published on October 14, 2026 is a memorandum of incorporation that, per the Federal Register summary, revises Appendix E to increase the purchase price of a single scratch ticket and set online wager limits at $50. The Secretary of the Interior took no action within 45 days, so the change was deemed approved to the extent consistent with IGRA. Our report on the Stillaguamish change covers the notice in detail.

Both approaches operate within the same framework. Class III gaming requires a tribal-state compact under IGRA, and Washington's compacts place operating detail in appendices that can be changed without reopening the entire agreement. That structure gives tribes a way to adjust limits and technical rules quickly, and gives the state a defined mechanism to attach conditions such as responsible gaming measures.

Why the details add up

Three policy threads emerge from the comparison. The first is wager limits. Limits set in appendices determine how much a player can bet at a terminal, a table or online, and therefore influence revenue per visit. The Puyallup package moves limits upward and adds a screening layer for the highest table wagers; the Stillaguamish filing sets a specific online ceiling. Our analysis of credit markers and wager-limit appendices examines how widespread these provisions are.

The second thread is capacity. An option for 1,000 additional terminals in a stepped approach shows that device allocations remain a negotiated, state-approved quantity rather than a market-driven one. The concept is discussed in our explainer on device caps in tribal-state compacts. For tribes, an allocation is a capital planning input; for the state, it is a lever that ties expansion to commitments.

The third thread is the pairing of expansion with consumer protection. The Puyallup amendment's self-exclusion, signage and self-imposed limit provisions, together with a non-smoking room requirement, indicate that Washington attaches public-interest conditions to commercial flexibility. That pattern is consistent with the trends we track in responsible gaming provisions in compact amendments.

A fourth, quieter thread is administrative durability. A restated compact, such as the Puyallup proposal that consolidates amendments one through six, reduces the risk of conflicting versions and makes it easier for regulators, lenders and vendors to confirm which terms apply. Narrow memoranda, by contrast, create a layered record that must be read in sequence. Neither is inherently better, but the choice affects transparency for anyone trying to understand a tribe's current obligations.

Process as policy

The Puyallup announcement also laid out Washington's multi-step process. After a tentative agreement, formal notice goes to the Governor, the Legislature and others; legislative committees hold public hearings; the Gambling Commission votes on whether to forward the amendment; the tribal chair and the Governor sign; and the tribe sends the amendment to the Secretary of the Interior for consideration and publication in the Federal Register. Each step is a point at which terms can be debated publicly, which is why narrow filings like the Stillaguamish memorandum still pass through a documented path. For the broader landscape, see our reporting on the 2026 wave of Washington compact amendments and the Washington state hub.

The lesson for observers is to read the appendices. A restated compact grabs attention, but a single revised appendix can change what a tribe is able to offer. Understanding Washington's market requires tracking both, along with the approval clock that decides when changes take effect. The Legal Guide provides the statutory background.
