SIGA PlayNow Profit Split: How Saskatchewan's Online Model Works

With online exclusivity extended to 2029, the 50/50 split on PlayNow profits shows how a First Nations-owned operator can anchor a provincial iGaming market.

By Linda CardinalPublished October 07, 20264 min read

The SIGA PlayNow online profit split is the clearest example in Canada of a First Nations-owned authority sitting at the center of a provincial iGaming market. Under an amended Gaming Framework Agreement between the Government of Saskatchewan and the Federation of Sovereign Indigenous Nations (FSIN), the Saskatchewan Indian Gaming Authority will keep exclusive authority to operate online gambling in the province through September 2029. Understanding how the money is divided explains why both sides have been willing to extend the arrangement.

The numbers behind the extension

Reporting on the extension, announced at the end of August 2026, put SIGA's 2025-26 results at $418 million in gross revenue, with $167 million returned to the province, marking the fourth consecutive year of record profits. SIGA's PlayNow platform, which launched in November 2022, generated more than $31 million in gross gaming revenue, up 23 percent year over year, and has more than 59,000 registered users. Our earlier report on SIGA's record 2025-26 net income covers the annual results in more detail.

PlayNow is small next to the land-based business, which is the point worth keeping in mind. Online play is growing quickly from a modest base, but the casinos still generate the bulk of revenue. The extension is therefore as much about protecting future growth as about current earnings.

How the 50/50 split works

Under the sharing arrangement described in the reporting, 50 percent of SIGA's online gaming profits go to Saskatchewan's First Nations, and the remaining 50 percent supports the province's General Revenue Fund. That structure differs from the models used elsewhere. In some jurisdictions, First Nations receive a share of provincial revenue through a negotiated formula; in others, Indigenous-owned operators compete in an open market. Saskatchewan's design instead makes a First Nations-owned authority the sole licensed online operator and divides what it earns.

The practical effects are straightforward. Because the operator is Indigenous-owned, the First Nations share is not a payment made out of someone else's profit but a direct allocation of the operator's own. Because the province receives the other half, it has a fiscal reason to defend the exclusivity rather than open the market. Both parties benefit from keeping outside competitors away, which is the logic that has sustained the Gaming Framework Agreement through its various amendments. Saskatchewan Minister Jeremy Harrison said that through the agreement and its amendments, the gaming sector provides significant First Nations employment and supports economic development.

For readers weighing how this compares with other approaches, our analysis of the carve-out versus exclusivity models sets out the trade-offs, and the site's comparison tools offer a broader look at how jurisdictions differ.

The risks and limits of exclusivity

Exclusivity is valuable only while it holds. A provincial government could in principle decide that the revenue from open licensing would exceed its half of a single-operator model, and the 2029 end date creates a natural point at which that question will return. A shorter extension gives the province flexibility; a longer one would give SIGA more certainty for platform investment. A three-year horizon is a compromise between the two.

There are also market realities. Players can reach unlicensed offshore sites, and PlayNow competes with them on product and convenience, not just on legality. Growth of 23 percent is encouraging, but sustaining it will depend on the quality of the platform, responsible gambling tools and marketing that can compete without a licensing advantage over rivals who ignore the rules entirely. The comparison to larger open markets elsewhere in Canada is imperfect, but it shapes how Saskatchewan's choices will be judged.

A land-based complement

The same announcement approved a gaming centre pilot at Flying Dust First Nation, featuring up to 49 slot machines as part of a larger development that includes a hotel and conference centre. Chief Tyson Bear indicated that construction would begin within weeks, with completion expected in about six months. The pilot is a reminder that Saskatchewan's approach is not purely digital: it pairs the online exclusivity with incremental land-based opportunities for individual First Nations.

That pairing matters for the distribution of benefits. A province-wide online profit share spreads revenue across First Nations broadly, while a gaming centre concentrates jobs and spending in a particular community. Together they address different goals, and the pilot will provide data on whether small-format venues can succeed alongside the larger casinos. For context on the wider Saskatchewan picture, see our coverage of the SIGA expansion.

Why it matters beyond Saskatchewan

Other provinces and First Nations are watching how online markets are structured, and Saskatchewan offers a concrete data point: a First Nations-owned monopoly operator, a fixed profit split and a defined renewal horizon. Whether it is a model to copy or a cautionary example will depend on how PlayNow performs through 2029 and on what happens at renewal.

For now, the evidence in the public numbers is favorable. Record profits, rapid online growth and a stable relationship between the province and the FSIN suggest that the partnership is working on its own terms. The test will come when the extension expires and each side decides whether the arrangement still serves it better than the alternatives.
