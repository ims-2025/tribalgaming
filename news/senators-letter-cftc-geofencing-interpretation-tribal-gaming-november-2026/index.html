Nine Senators Press CFTC to Withdraw Geofencing Guidance Tribes Oppose

Led by Adam Schiff, senators from five tribal gaming states want an answer by November 23 and a records hold reaching back to January 2025.

By Daniel YazziePublished November 19, 20264 min read

Nine Democratic senators have asked the Commodity Futures Trading Commission to withdraw or suspend its November 5 interpretation on geographic access restrictions, arguing that the guidance collides with court orders protecting tribal and state gaming jurisdiction. The letter, led by Sen. Adam Schiff of California and addressed to CFTC Chairman Michael Selig, asks the agency to respond by November 23 and to preserve internal records related to the policy going back to January 1, 2025.

The CFTC geofencing letter is the most direct congressional pushback yet on an interpretation that tribal governments regard as a threat to the injunctions they have won against prediction-market operators. Co-signers include Amy Klobuchar of Minnesota, Alex Padilla of California, Catherine Cortez Masto and Jacky Rosen of Nevada, Maria Cantwell and Patty Murray of Washington, and Gary Peters and Elissa Slotkin of Michigan. Every one of those states has a significant tribal gaming industry, and several are home to tribes actively litigating against event-contract platforms.

What the senators are objecting to

The CFTC interpretation gives federally registered exchanges 30 days to remove domestic geographic restrictions that conflict with the agency's impartial-access requirement, or to explain to the agency why they cannot. The CFTC's position is that derivatives markets must operate uniformly nationwide. In practice, the guidance would press platforms such as Kalshi to offer contracts in jurisdictions, including Indian lands, where courts have ordered them blocked. Our earlier report on the CFTC geofencing interpretation and the Kalshi injunction covers the text of the guidance in detail.

According to Tribal Business News, the senators raise three main concerns. First, the interpretation conflicts with court orders that require platforms to geofence certain jurisdictions. Second, it was issued only hours after a federal court ordered Kalshi to block access on Blue Lake Rancheria's lands, a sequence the senators view as more than coincidence. Third, they suggest the agency may be coordinating with platforms that are themselves parties to active litigation, which they argue could encourage violations of state and tribal gaming law. The letter characterizes sports event contracts as "indistinguishable from sports bets" subject to those laws.

That last point is the substantive heart of the tribal position. Blue Lake Rancheria and Chicken Ranch Rancheria of Me-Wuk Indians won a federal appellate ruling finding they were likely to succeed on claims that Kalshi's sports-event contracts violate the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act. Under IGRA, Class III gaming on Indian lands is lawful only under a tribal-state compact or secretarial procedures, and the tribes argue that a nationally available sports contract is Class III wagering by another name. Background on how IGRA allocates that authority is in our legal guide to IGRA and Class III gaming.

What an oversight letter can and cannot do

A letter from members of Congress carries no binding legal force. The CFTC is an independent agency, and it is not obligated to withdraw an interpretation because legislators object. What the letter does accomplish is to put the agency on notice that its conduct is being documented. The records-preservation request, in particular, is the kind of step that typically precedes formal oversight activity, such as hearings or requests for testimony, if a committee with jurisdiction chooses to pursue it.

The timing constrains what can follow quickly. Congress is in its short post-election session, and the senators who signed belong to the minority party in the chamber. Legislative proposals aimed at prediction markets, including House efforts we have covered, have not advanced. That leaves courts as the principal venue for the tribes' arguments for now. The interpretation's 30-day window, which runs into early December, and the November 23 response date fall close together, meaning exchanges may have to decide how to treat existing geofences before Congress has any opportunity to act.

For tribes, a geofence is not a technical preference. It is the mechanism by which a court order protecting their gaming jurisdiction is actually enforced.

How the letter fits the broader prediction-market fight

The senators' intervention adds a federal political dimension to a dispute that has largely played out in district and appellate courts. Results have diverged: California tribes have won injunctive relief, while a federal court in Wisconsin denied relief to the Ho-Chunk Nation, a split we examined in our analysis of the diverging Kalshi rulings. State attorneys general and tribal nations have also asked the Supreme Court to take up the question of whether federal commodities law displaces state and tribal gaming law for sports contracts.

The geographic spread of the signatories is notable. Washington, Michigan, Minnesota, Nevada and California represent very different tribal gaming models, from Washington's tribal-exclusive sports wagering to Michigan's mixed commercial and tribal online market. Klobuchar, who takes office as Minnesota's governor in January, will carry the issue to a state that enacted its own prohibition on sports event contracts this year and is now defending it against a Kalshi lawsuit. A shared concern across those states is that a uniform federal rule would override the location-based structure on which tribal exclusivity has been built since IGRA's enactment in 1988.

The CFTC has not publicly responded to the letter. Whether the agency answers by November 23, and whether it adjusts or delays implementation, will be the next marker. Tribes operating under court orders, meanwhile, are expected to continue asking judges to enforce those orders regardless of the agency's guidance.
