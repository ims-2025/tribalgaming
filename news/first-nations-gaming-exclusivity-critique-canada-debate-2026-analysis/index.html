The Exclusivity Critique of First Nations Gaming in Canada, Examined

Think-tank criticism of provincially granted gaming rights has sharpened. The economics deserve a fair hearing, and so do the treaty and fiscal arguments on the other side.

By Linda CardinalPublished September 16, 20266 min read

First Nations gaming exclusivity in Canada has become a live policy argument rather than a settled arrangement. Over the past two years, as provinces have opened competitive iGaming markets and First Nations have moved from revenue-sharing beneficiaries to outright casino owners, think-tank and editorial criticism of the underlying framework has grown louder. The core charge is that provincially granted exclusive rights function as protected regional monopolies, insulating operators from competition and raising costs for consumers. The Fraser Institute has advanced a version of this argument in published work on First Nations gaming, and variants of it now appear in provincial budget debates.

The critique deserves engagement on its merits rather than dismissal, and so do the responses. Both sides are arguing about real things, but they are frequently arguing past each other because they start from different premises about what gaming policy in Canada is for.

What the competition argument actually establishes

The economic case against exclusivity is not exotic. Where a single operator holds protected rights to a defined catchment, standard industrial-organisation reasoning predicts weaker price competition, slower product innovation and higher rents captured by the incumbent. Applied to gaming, that would show up as lower payout percentages, thinner amenity investment and less aggressive reinvestment in property refresh than a contested market would force.

Canada's provincial structures do concentrate rights. Saskatchewan's framework channels most on-reserve casino operation through a single First Nations authority; Manitoba and Ontario operate through negotiated arrangements that allocate defined shares of provincial gaming proceeds; British Columbia and Alberta route a percentage of gross gaming revenue to First Nations bodies. These are administered systems, not open markets, and the critique is correct that they were designed that way.

The argument also carries a fiscal edge: if provincial gaming proceeds are partly earmarked before general revenue, then every dollar so directed is a dollar not subject to ordinary legislative appropriation. That is a legitimate transparency question, and one that applies to earmarked revenue of any kind.

What the critique tends to omit

Three things typically go missing from the market-efficiency framing. The first is the counterfactual. Gaming in Canada is not a naturally open market that exclusivity closed off. Under the Criminal Code, lawful gaming is conducted and managed by the provinces; there is no baseline of free entry from which First Nations rights represent a departure. The realistic alternative to a First Nations share is not open competition but a larger provincial Crown-corporation share. Comparing an administered arrangement against an idealised competitive market, rather than against the actual alternative, inflates the apparent cost.

The second is the character of the entitlement. Provincial revenue-sharing frameworks were negotiated — in several cases after litigation and prolonged intergovernmental dispute — as recognitions of jurisdiction and as fiscal instruments for communities with constrained own-source revenue. They function less like a licensing subsidy and more like a negotiated fiscal transfer with a defined base. Whether that is the best instrument is a fair question; treating it purely as a market distortion misdescribes what it is. The honest version of the debate is not monopoly versus competition. It is which fiscal instrument best serves the objectives governments agreed to — and whether the current one is being measured against its actual alternatives.

The third omission is that the model is already changing, and changing in the direction the critics nominally favour. First Nations in British Columbia and Alberta have been acquiring established commercial casinos outright, moving from a share of provincial proceeds to ownership and operating risk. That shift — which we analysed in revenue sharing versus ownership — exposes those operators to exactly the competitive discipline the critique calls for, in properties that compete for the same customers as any other commercial venue.

Where the critique lands hardest: iGaming

The sharpest version of the argument concerns online. Land-based exclusivity is bounded by geography; a customer in a catchment either drives to the property or does not. Online exclusivity has no such boundary, which makes it both more valuable to the holder and harder to justify on the distributional grounds used for land-based rights. Saskatchewan's extension of exclusive online rights to its First Nations gaming authority, and the contrasting decision in Alberta to open a competitive iGaming market with a revenue allocation to First Nations initiatives, represent two genuinely different answers to the same question.

Neither answer is obviously correct. The exclusivity model concentrates the benefit and protects a revenue base that funds community programming; the open-market-with-allocation model likely grows the regulated channel faster and captures more grey-market play, but distributes the proceeds more thinly and exposes land-based properties to cannibalisation. Which performs better is an empirical question that the next three to five years of Alberta and Saskatchewan data will start to answer.

What would improve the argument on both sides

Critics would strengthen their case by specifying the counterfactual precisely and by separating the land-based and online questions, which have different economics. Defenders would strengthen theirs by publishing more: audited allocation data, programme-level reporting on where proceeds go, and payout and reinvestment figures that would let the monopoly-rents claim be tested rather than asserted. Financial opacity is the critique's most durable foothold, and it is the one most easily removed.

There is also a measurement gap that neither side fills well. Gaming proceeds in most provinces fund housing, education, language and health programming in communities where the alternative funding source is federal transfer. Assessing the arrangement without pricing that substitution — and without accounting for the employment concentrated in regions with few other employers — produces a partial answer. Readers can follow the provincial frameworks in more detail through our comparison of Canadian revenue-sharing frameworks.

The debate is likely to intensify as more provinces regulate online play and as First Nations ownership expands. That is not a reason to avoid it. It is a reason to insist that it be conducted with the actual alternatives, and the actual numbers, on the table.
