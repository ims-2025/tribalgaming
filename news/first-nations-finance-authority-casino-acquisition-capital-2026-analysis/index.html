The Balance Sheet Behind First Nations Casino Buying: Inside the FNFA

A purpose-built Indigenous borrowing institution let First Nations buy their way into an industry Canadian law would not legislate them into.

By Linda CardinalPublished August 15, 20264 min read

The most consequential institution in Canadian First Nations gaming this year does not run a casino, employ a dealer, or hold a licence. The First Nations Finance Authority — a borrowing body created under the First Nations Fiscal Management Act to give participating Nations access to institutional debt markets — has become the quiet enabler behind the wave of First Nations casino acquisitions that reshaped British Columbia's gaming ownership map over the past eighteen months. Understanding First Nations Finance Authority casino financing is now essential to understanding why Indigenous ownership of Canadian gaming assets accelerated so abruptly.

The acquisitions themselves have been well covered. Snuneymuxw First Nation, partnered with Musqueam, moved on River Rock Casino Resort in Richmond, the largest property in British Columbia. Semiahmoo bought into Surrey. The Ts'elxwéyeqw Tribe took Chilliwack. Tsleil-Waututh Nation pursued Hastings Racecourse in Vancouver. Taken together, First Nations interests now hold stakes in roughly a dozen casinos across seven provinces. What has received less attention is the question of how a Nation with a few thousand members finances a nine-figure acquisition of a mature gaming asset.

What the FNFA actually does

The FNFA pools the borrowing needs of participating First Nations and issues debentures into the public bond market against that pooled security, backed by the certification regime administered by the First Nations Financial Management Board. The effect is to convert what would otherwise be a series of small, expensive, individually negotiated loans into a single investment-grade credit that institutional buyers will hold at conventional spreads.

The scale is no longer marginal. The FNFA surpassed $4 billion in cumulative financing since its founding as of late 2025, and in June 2026 it completed an $800 million offering of 3.25 percent senior secured bonds due 2031. That last figure is the operative one for gaming: a Nation borrowing at rates in that neighbourhood, on a term structure that matches a casino's cash flows, can underwrite an acquisition that would be uneconomic at the double-digit rates historically available to on-reserve borrowers without a security interest a commercial lender would recognize.

This is the structural point that distinguishes the Canadian story from the American one. In the United States, tribal gaming expansion has been financed increasingly through capital markets and, more recently, through REIT sale-leaseback structures that trade long-term rent obligations for immediate liquidity. In Canada, a purpose-built Indigenous borrowing institution supplies comparable pricing without conveying an interest in the asset to an outside landlord.

Why buying beat building

The acquisition strategy also reflects a regulatory reality. Canada has no equivalent to the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act, and no federal statute establishing a right for First Nations to conduct gaming on their own terms. Under the Criminal Code, provincial governments conduct and manage lottery schemes; a First Nation's participation is a matter of provincial arrangement rather than federal entitlement, as set out in our comparison of the two legal models. Building a new casino therefore requires provincial appetite for a new facility in a market the province already considers served.

Buying an existing one requires only a willing seller and a regulator prepared to approve a change of control. When Great Canadian Entertainment began divesting British Columbia properties, that willing seller appeared — and First Nations with FNFA-backed balance sheets were among the few buyers able to move at the speed and certainty the process demanded. The path around the legal constraint was financial, not legislative.

The risks the model carries

Three deserve attention. The first is concentration. A Nation whose own-source revenue is dominated by a single acquired casino has replaced dependence on transfers with dependence on a cyclical consumer business, and has done so using leverage. The FNFA's structure disciplines this to a degree — its certification requirements are meaningful — but the exposure is real, and it is the same exposure that has prompted American tribes to pursue diversification beyond gaming.

The second is timing. These assets were purchased into a Canadian market that is simultaneously absorbing regulated online gaming in Ontario and Alberta. Land-based revenue in mature urban markets is not obviously a growth story, and an acquirer underwriting flat-to-modest growth on a leveraged basis has less room for error than the headline purchase prices suggest.

The third is operational. Owning a casino and running one are different capabilities. Several of the transactions preserve existing management arrangements precisely because the acquiring Nations recognize this, but management agreements expire, and the transition from owner to operator is where the economic case either closes or does not.

What it means for the next five years

The FNFA's role suggests the pattern will continue as long as sellers exist and rates remain workable. Ontario, Manitoba, and Saskatchewan all contain assets that could plausibly change hands, and Nations in those provinces have watched the British Columbia transactions closely. Our running analysis of Indigenous ownership in Canadian gaming tracks the pipeline as it develops.

The larger significance is that First Nations in Canada have found a route to gaming ownership that does not depend on persuading a province to expand gaming or a Parliament to amend the Criminal Code. They bought their way into an industry that would not legislate them in. Whether that produces durable economic self-determination or concentrated exposure to a maturing sector will not be clear for a decade — but the financing architecture that made it possible is already the most important piece of infrastructure in Canadian Indigenous gaming.
