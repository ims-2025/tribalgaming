Fee Land vs. Trust Land: What It Means for Tribal Gaming

Why buying property does not create gaming rights, and how land status shapes taxes, jurisdiction and federal approvals.

By Daniel YazziePublished October 14, 20264 min read

When a tribe buys a hotel, a golf course or a parcel of farmland, the first legal question is not what it will cost but what fee land versus trust land status the property will have. That distinction determines whether a tribe's gaming law can apply, whether state and local governments can tax and regulate the site, and what federal approvals are required. It is one of the most important and most frequently misunderstood ideas in tribal gaming law.

Two ways a tribe can hold land

Land held by or for a tribe generally falls into one of a few categories. Trust land is owned by the United States in trust for a tribe or individual Indians. Restricted fee land is owned by the tribe or individuals but subject to federal restrictions on sale or encumbrance. Ordinary fee land is held like any other private property, with full title in the owner and no federal restrictions, even when the owner is a tribal government.

Tribes acquire trust status primarily through the Secretary of the Interior's authority under Section 5 of the Indian Reorganization Act of 1934, implemented through the fee-to-trust regulations at 25 CFR Part 151. The process involves an application, environmental review, consultation with state and local governments and a federal decision. Our explainer on land into trust and gaming eligibility walks through the steps, and the piece on fee-to-trust processing timelines explains why it can take years.

Why gaming law cares

The Indian Gaming Regulatory Act applies to gaming on Indian lands. As defined in the statute, that means lands within a reservation, and lands held in trust or subject to restriction against alienation for a tribe or individual, over which the tribe exercises governmental power. Our Indian lands explainer covers the definition in detail.

The consequence is straightforward. A tribe cannot conduct IGRA-governed gaming on ordinary fee land outside its reservation. Even trust land acquired after October 17, 1988, is generally closed to gaming unless an exception in Section 20 applies, such as a restored-lands exception, an initial reservation exception or a favorable two-part determination with the governor's concurrence. These are covered in our explainers on the Section 20 exceptions and the two-part determination.

Put differently, buying property does not create gaming rights. A tribe that purchases a luxury resort outside its reservation, as San Manuel did in October 2026 with the Fairmont Scottsdale Princess in Arizona, owns a business, not an Indian gaming facility. Public reports of that transaction do not mention gaming at the property, and nothing in the federal framework would make an ordinary fee purchase a gaming site.

Taxes and state jurisdiction

Fee status also matters outside gaming law. The Supreme Court held in County of Yakima v. Confederated Tribes and Bands of the Yakima Indian Nation (1992) that fee land within a reservation, once freely alienable, could be subject to state and local property taxation. In City of Sherrill v. Oneida Indian Nation of New York (2005), the Court held that a tribe could not unilaterally revive tribal sovereignty over reacquired fee parcels and thereby avoid local property taxes, pointing the tribe instead toward the federal trust process. The practical takeaway is that fee land owned by a tribe is generally treated like other private land for state and local taxes and regulation, unless and until it is taken into trust.

Trust land typically brings immunity from state and local property taxes, though the surrounding questions, including payments for services, are often resolved through agreements. Our explainer on municipal services agreements shows how tribes and host communities handle that relationship.

What sovereignty does and does not do

A tribe's sovereign immunity from suit is a separate question from land status. The Supreme Court has held that tribal immunity extends to commercial activities, including those conducted off a reservation, in Kiowa Tribe of Oklahoma v. Manufacturing Technologies (1998), and reaffirmed the doctrine in Michigan v. Bay Mills Indian Community (2014). Whether a particular business entity shares that immunity depends on its legal form and relationship to the tribe, as discussed in our article on enterprise structures. Immunity from suit does not, however, exempt off-reservation fee property from generally applicable state law. Those two doctrines operate on different tracks.

Why tribes still buy fee land

If fee land carries taxes and cannot host IGRA gaming, why acquire it? There are several reasons. Hospitality, real estate and other commercial investments can generate steady income that diversifies a tribe away from dependence on gaming. Fee parcels are also the usual first step in an eventual trust application, since the tribe typically needs to own or control land before asking to have it taken into trust. And some acquisitions simply serve governmental or cultural goals, such as reclaiming ancestral sites. The trend toward diversification is examined in our analysis of non-gaming revenue diversification.

A quick reference

Fee land: held without federal restrictions, generally subject to state and local property taxes and regulation, and not eligible for IGRA gaming. Trust or restricted land: held with federal protections, generally outside state property taxation, and potentially eligible for gaming only if it meets the definition of Indian lands and an applicable IGRA exception. The details can be intricate, and individual cases turn on specific facts, so readers evaluating a particular project should consult counsel and the Interior Department's published decisions.
