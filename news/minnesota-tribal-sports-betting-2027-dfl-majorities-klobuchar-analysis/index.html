Minnesota Tribal Sports Betting in 2027: What Bigger Majorities Change

The election removed the one-vote choke points that killed past bills. The harder question, consumer-protection terms that hold a coalition together, is still open.

By Sarah BegayPublished November 19, 20264 min read

Minnesota's November 4 election reshaped the arithmetic around Minnesota tribal sports betting more than any single committee vote in the last four years. Democratic-Farmer-Labor candidates widened their Senate majority from 34-33 to 39-28 and turned a tied House into a 77-57 advantage, according to results compiled by Covers, while Sen. Amy Klobuchar won the governor's race and will become the state's first woman governor in January. For a tribal-led sports betting framework that has repeatedly stalled on one-vote margins, larger majorities remove the most obvious procedural obstacle. They do not resolve the disagreements that actually stopped the bill.

The 2027 session will be the first test of whether Minnesota's eleven gaming tribes, the state's two horse racing tracks and its charitable gaming sector can convert a negotiated compromise into law under conditions that are structurally more favorable than any they have seen.

Why the margins mattered before

Minnesota is not a state where tribal primacy in sports wagering is seriously contested. The failures have been distributional and procedural. In 2022, a House-passed bill was derailed after a Senate committee added racetracks as licensees. A 2025 proposal stalled on a tied committee vote. In 2026, a bipartisan Senate bill, SF 4139, would have given tribes exclusive mobile licenses while sharing financial benefits with the tracks and charitable operators, but it did not clear committee before adjournment. We examined that framework's design in our analysis of SF 4139 and its fate in our report on the bill stalling again.

In a 34-33 Senate and a tied House, every committee seat was effectively a veto point. A single defection on a gambling bill, an issue where members of both parties regularly break from leadership, could end a session's effort. A five-seat Senate cushion and a twenty-seat House majority change that. Leaders can now afford to lose several members of their own caucus on a sports betting vote and still move a bill, provided they choose to prioritize it.

What the election did not change

The compromise that emerged in 2026 is the more important development, and it predates the election. Canterbury Park, which had long sought its own sports betting license, moved to support a tribal-licensed model that shares benefits with the racing industry. That realignment removed the coalition fracture that defined earlier sessions. The remaining resistance comes largely from lawmakers concerned about gambling harms, and that concern is not confined to one party.

Sen. John Marty, a DFL member and the most persistent critic of expanded wagering in the Legislature, retained his seat. Marty has pressed for restrictions on in-game wagering and push notifications, and earlier tribal-led bills already incorporated limits on prop bets involving college athletes and on sportsbook marketing. Covers reported that no major sponsor or leading critic of the 2026 debate lost re-election. In other words, the same people will be negotiating the same issues, with more room for leadership to act but no fresh consensus on the consumer-protection terms.

Bigger majorities lower the cost of defections. They do not tell leadership how many defections to expect, or what concessions will be needed to limit them.

The 2027 calendar and competing priorities

The 2027 Legislature convenes in early January for a budget-setting session, and the biennial budget will dominate floor time and leadership attention. Sports betting has historically been framed in Minnesota as a policy question rather than a revenue measure, partly because the tribal-exclusive model directs the core economic benefit to tribal governments rather than to a state tax line. That framing has protected the issue from being traded in budget negotiations, but it also means the bill has to compete for attention on its own merits.

The governor's office is a second variable. Klobuchar's position on a tribal-led framework will be closely watched, and her recent record offers at least one signal: as a senator she co-signed the November letter urging the Commodity Futures Trading Commission to withdraw guidance that tribes say undermines court-ordered geofencing of prediction markets. Minnesota also enacted a 2026 law criminalizing sports event contracts, which Kalshi has challenged in federal court, as covered in our report on the Kalshi lawsuit against Minnesota's ban. That law reflects a legislative view that wagering on sports should run through regulated, state-sanctioned channels. Supporters of the tribal model are likely to argue that legal sports betting is the natural complement to a ban on unlicensed event contracts.

What to watch

Three questions will shape the session. The first is whether committee chairs in both chambers, now assigned by a governing majority rather than negotiated under a power-sharing agreement, schedule hearings early enough to leave time for conference negotiations. The second is whether the consumer-protection package can be written tightly enough to secure votes from harm-focused DFL members without driving away tribal support. The third is whether the compact amendments that would follow passage can be negotiated efficiently with a new administration. Under the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act, in-person wagering at tribal casinos would require amended Class III compacts, a process that typically follows any authorizing legislation.

Minnesota's tribal gaming industry is among the most established in the country, built on compacts dating to the early 1990s and on a market in which tribal governments operate the state's casinos. Profiles of the state's operators are available in our Minnesota state hub. Whether 2027 becomes the year that exclusivity extends to sports wagering now depends less on the vote count and more on whether leadership treats the issue as a priority in a crowded budget year.
