Pauma Sportsbook Rests on Secretarial Procedures, Not a Compact

A week after launch, the Pauma Band's legal basis is coming into view. It turns on the fine print of procedures Interior imposed when California would not sign a compact.

By James KingfisherPublished November 20, 20265 min read

When the Pauma Band of Luiseño Indians opened eight sports wagering kiosks at Casino Pauma on November 12, the tribe's announcement said nothing about the legal basis for the operation. As we noted in our launch-day report, that gap made the Pauma sportsbook hard to assess. Over the following week, coverage by Casino.org and industry analyst Steve Ruddock filled in the outline. The tribe's position rests on its federal secretarial procedures, the Interior Department rules that govern Pauma's Class III gaming in place of a tribal-state compact. Whether that argument holds is now the central question, and California's sports betting debate turns on it.

How Pauma ended up under secretarial procedures

Most California tribes conduct Class III gaming under compacts negotiated with the governor and ratified by the Legislature. Pauma does not. The tribe signed a compact in 1999 and later amended it to add machines. It then spent years in federal litigation with the state over the fees it had paid, and a federal court eventually ordered California to refund about $36.2 million in overpayments. After the relationship broke down, the tribe pursued the remedy the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act provides when a state will not negotiate in good faith.

That remedy runs through a fixed sequence under 25 U.S.C. § 2710(d)(7). A federal court finds the state failed to negotiate in good faith. The parties then submit last best offers to a court-appointed mediator. If the state still refuses to accept the mediator's choice, the Secretary of the Interior issues procedures under which the tribe may conduct Class III gaming. Our explainer on secretarial procedures covers each step. According to Ruddock's reading of the record, the mediator selected Pauma's final offer, and Interior's procedures for the tribe followed in 2025.

The argument in the fine print

The procedures authorize the familiar California menu: slot machines, house-banked table games and other games the state expressly authorizes. According to Casino.org, the tribe contends that because it operates under federal procedures rather than a compact, it is legally entitled to offer sports betting. Ruddock's analysis points to the specific language involved. One provision requires the tribe to notify the National Indian Gaming Commission at least 60 days before offering a new Class III game, and the argument draws on Sections 4.1(d) and (e) of the procedures.

The contrast with other tribes' documents is the point of the argument. Secretarial procedures issued to Blue Lake Rancheria, Ruddock notes, expressly tie any added games to state authorization. Pauma's text, built from the tribe's own winning offer, appears looser. In the tribe's reading, the procedures set up a federal process for adding games that does not require new state action.

Secretarial procedures replace a compact. They do not replace the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act.

The difficulty is the statute above the procedures. IGRA allows Class III gaming only if it is located in a state that "permits such gaming for any purpose by any person, organization, or entity," and that condition applies whether the tribe operates under a compact or under procedures. Secretarial procedures exist to stand in for a compact the state refused to sign. They are not generally understood to authorize gaming the statute itself would not allow. Ruddock reaches the same conclusion: in his view the procedures gave Pauma a more flexible process for adding games, not an exemption from IGRA.

California law is not ambiguous on sports wagering. The state constitution authorizes tribal slot machines and banking and percentage card games under compacts, and voters rejected both sports betting propositions in 2022. Our explainer on IGRA's scope-of-gaming test explains how federal courts have read the "permits such gaming" language, generally asking whether the state allows the particular game, not gambling in general. Under that test, a state that bans sports betting for everyone is difficult to describe as one that permits it.

Who could respond, and how

The enforcement picture is unusual because there is no compact. A compact state's main tool is a federal suit to stop Class III gaming conducted in violation of the compact. With Pauma, the procedures take the compact's place, and Interior and the NIGC sit closer to the center of any response. The NIGC can issue notices of violation and closure orders for gaming that violates IGRA, and those tools are described in our NIGC enforcement explainer. Separately, 18 U.S.C. § 1166 gives the federal government, not the state, criminal jurisdiction over state gambling laws as applied in Indian country, absent a tribal-state agreement saying otherwise.

As of November 20, TribalGaming.com is not aware of any public statement from Gov. Gavin Newsom, Attorney General Rob Bonta, the California Gambling Control Commission, the NIGC or the Interior Department about the Pauma operation. Nor have we seen any public statement on whether the tribe filed the 60-day notice its theory depends on. The most prominent critic so far has been Victor Rocha, conference chairman of the Indian Gaming Association, who called the launch illegal in a November 13 post on X.

The tribe's partners say the operation will grow. SCCG Management, the Las Vegas advisory firm that worked on the launch, has said on-property mobile wagering is coming. Casino.org reports that the kiosks run on terminals from ISI Sports.

Why the rest of California is watching

The stakes reach well beyond one small casino in Pauma Valley. California's tribes are working toward a 2028 ballot measure, and the California Nations Indian Gaming Association has circulated three possible 2028 sports betting models. A single tribe offering wagers under a disputed federal theory complicates that effort. If the theory fails, it may give opponents material for the campaign. If it succeeds, it raises questions about whether procedures-based tribes have a route that compacted tribes lack.

Only a few California tribes operate under secretarial procedures, so the theory would not carry over to the state's compacted tribes. That limits how far it could spread. It also means the dispute, if one comes, will most likely be settled by federal agencies and federal courts, not in Sacramento.
