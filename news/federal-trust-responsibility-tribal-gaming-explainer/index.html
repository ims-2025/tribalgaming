The Federal Trust Responsibility, Explained for Tribal Gaming

Tribal leaders invoke it in nearly every gaming fight with Washington. Here is what the doctrine requires, and where its legal force runs out.

By Linda CardinalPublished November 14, 20265 min read

When tribal gaming leaders challenge a federal agency, they frequently frame the dispute in terms of the federal trust responsibility: the obligation the United States owes to tribal nations as a result of treaties, statutes and the historical relationship between the two. The phrase appeared again this month when California tribal leaders criticized the Commodity Futures Trading Commission's guidance on geographic access restrictions, as covered in our report on the CFTC interpretation. Because the doctrine is invoked so often, it is worth understanding precisely what it is, where it carries legal force, and where it functions mainly as a policy commitment.

Where the federal trust responsibility comes from

The doctrine traces to the early decisions of the Supreme Court under Chief Justice John Marshall. In Cherokee Nation v. Georgia in 1831, the Court described tribes as "domestic dependent nations" whose relationship to the United States resembled that of a ward to a guardian. The language reflected the treaty bargains of the period, in which tribes ceded vast territories in exchange for federal promises of protection, reserved lands and continued self-government.

Over the following century the relationship was used both to protect and to override tribal interests. In the twentieth century, however, courts increasingly applied it as a standard of conduct for the federal government. In Seminole Nation v. United States in 1942, the Supreme Court said the government had charged itself with moral obligations of the highest responsibility and trust, and that its conduct should be judged by the most exacting fiduciary standards. Congress and successive administrations have since restated the trust relationship in statutes, executive orders and agency policies, and federal Indian policy since the 1970s has tied it to tribal self-determination.

What courts will actually enforce

The trust responsibility is broad in principle but narrower in litigation. The modern Supreme Court has distinguished between a general trust relationship, which informs how statutes are read and how agencies should behave, and specific trust duties, which can be enforced in court. In United States v. Mitchell, decided in 1983, the Court allowed a tribe to seek money damages for federal mismanagement of timber resources because the governing statutes and regulations gave the government comprehensive control over those resources. In later cases, including United States v. Navajo Nation in 2003 and United States v. Jicarilla Apache Nation in 2011, the Court emphasized that enforceable duties must be grounded in specific statutory or regulatory language rather than in the general relationship alone.

That approach was reinforced in Arizona v. Navajo Nation in 2023, where the Court held that the Navajo Nation's 1868 treaty did not impose an affirmative duty on the United States to take steps to secure water for the tribe. The practical lesson for tribal gaming is that the trust responsibility rarely wins a case on its own. It is most powerful when a statute gives it concrete content.

The trust responsibility is a standard the federal government holds itself to, and a tool for reading statutes. It becomes a cause of action only when Congress writes specific duties into law.

How the trust responsibility appears in gaming law

The Indian Gaming Regulatory Act gives the doctrine several concrete footholds. IGRA's declared purposes include promoting tribal economic development, self-sufficiency and strong tribal governments, which courts consider when interpreting the statute. More directly, IGRA authorizes the Secretary of the Interior to disapprove a tribal-state compact only if it violates IGRA, another provision of federal law, or the trust obligations of the United States to Indians. That makes the trust responsibility an explicit standard in compact review, which is carried out by Interior's Office of Indian Gaming and described in our explainer on the Office of Indian Gaming. When Interior lets a compact take effect by operation of law, or approves one with a letter raising concerns about revenue sharing, it is often weighing the same obligation.

The doctrine also underlies the land-into-trust system. When the Secretary takes land into trust for a tribe under the Indian Reorganization Act of 1934, the United States holds legal title for the tribe's benefit, and the gaming eligibility of that land is then governed by IGRA's provisions on Indian lands. Many of the federal government's decisions about where tribes may build casinos are, in form, trustee decisions about trust property. The statutory framework is summarized in our Legal Guide to IGRA and Class III gaming.

Consultation and independent agencies

The most common modern expression of the trust relationship is consultation. Executive Order 13175, signed in November 2000, directs executive departments and agencies to engage in meaningful consultation with tribal officials when developing policies that have tribal implications. The order applies to executive departments such as Interior, which houses the NIGC, but it treats independent regulatory agencies differently: it encourages them to comply rather than requiring it. That distinction matters in disputes involving financial regulators such as the CFTC, which are structured as independent commissions. Tribal advocates argue that the trust relationship binds the federal government as a whole, while agencies outside the order's mandatory scope have broader discretion over how, and whether, to consult.

Consultation obligations are generally not enforceable in court on their own, but a failure to consult can feature in administrative-law challenges and frequently shapes congressional oversight.

A parallel in Canada

Canadian law has developed a related but distinct framework. In Guerin v. The Queen in 1984, the Supreme Court of Canada recognized a fiduciary duty of the Crown in dealing with reserve land, and in Haida Nation in 2004 it grounded a duty to consult in the honour of the Crown. Those principles inform how provinces and First Nations negotiate gaming arrangements, though gaming authority in Canada flows primarily through provincial control under the Criminal Code rather than through a federal statute like IGRA.

Why it matters

For tribal gaming, the trust responsibility functions on three levels: as a standard Interior must apply in reviewing compacts, as a lens for interpreting statutes such as IGRA, and as a political commitment that tribes use to press agencies and Congress. Its limits are equally important. Without specific statutory language, it seldom compels a particular outcome, which is why tribes facing new federal positions often pair trust-based arguments with direct claims under IGRA and with requests for legislation.
