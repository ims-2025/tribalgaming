Who Regulates a Tribal Casino? Tribal, Federal and State Layers Explained

A tribal casino answers to up to three regulators at once. Here is what each layer does, where they overlap, and why the answer depends on the type of game.

By James KingfisherPublished October 12, 20266 min read

Who regulates a tribal casino depends on three layers of government and on the type of game being played. The Indian Gaming Regulatory Act of 1988 (IGRA) divides responsibility among the tribe itself, the federal government and, for casino-style gaming, the state. Understanding the division explains why a single casino can have a tribal gaming commission on site every day, a federal agency reviewing its audits and a state agency inspecting its slot machines under a compact.

The tribal layer: the first and most active regulator

IGRA places primary regulatory responsibility for tribal gaming with the tribe. A tribe regulates its own gaming through a gaming ordinance, which the National Indian Gaming Commission (NIGC) must approve, and through a tribal gaming commission or regulatory authority that carries out day-to-day oversight. Our explainer on tribal gaming regulatory authorities describes the structure in detail.

In practice the tribal regulator does most of the hands-on work. It licenses gaming employees and vendors, conducts or reviews background investigations, monitors surveillance and cash handling, investigates patron complaints and violations, and enforces internal control standards. Because the regulator is part of the tribal government but separate from casino management, a well-run system keeps those functions independent of the people operating the business. The tribe's ordinance also sets the framework for how net gaming revenue may be used, which IGRA limits to purposes such as funding tribal government operations and programs, promoting general welfare, supporting economic development and charitable giving, and helping fund local government agencies.

The federal layer: the NIGC and the Interior Department

The NIGC is the federal regulator created by IGRA, housed within the Department of the Interior. Its work is oversight and enforcement rather than daily operation. It reviews and approves tribal gaming ordinances and management contracts, receives and reviews background investigation reports for key employees and primary management officials, requires annual independent audits of gaming operations, and can inspect facilities. When it finds violations, it has enforcement tools including notices of violation, civil fines and temporary closure orders. Our guide on how the NIGC regulates tribal gaming explains each of these. In remarks reported in 2026, the commission's vice chair described oversight of more than 250 tribes and over 500 gaming operations.

The Secretary of the Interior has a separate role: reviewing tribal-state compacts for Class III gaming. Under IGRA the Secretary has 45 days to approve or disapprove a compact, and a compact that is not acted on within that period is considered approved to the extent it is consistent with IGRA. Notice of approval is published in the Federal Register. The Department of Justice also has a role, because IGRA makes certain violations federal crimes that U.S. Attorneys can prosecute.

Federal regulators generally do not run casinos and do not substitute for tribal oversight. IGRA's design assumes the tribal regulator is the front line and that federal oversight is a check.

The state layer: compacts for Class III gaming

The state's role depends on the class of game. IGRA divides gaming into three categories. Class I covers traditional social games and is regulated exclusively by tribes. Class II, principally bingo and related games, is regulated by the tribe and the NIGC, with no state role. Class III, which includes slot machines, banked card games and table games, may be conducted only under a tribal-state compact negotiated between the tribe and the state. The Class II vs Class III explainer sets out the differences.

The compact is where the state's authority comes from. Compacts commonly address the application of tribal and state laws related to licensing and regulation, allocation of criminal and civil jurisdiction, the state's assessment of regulatory costs, remedies for breach, and standards for operation and maintenance of the facility. Many compacts give a state gaming agency the right to inspect Class III operations, test slot machines or review certain records, though the details vary widely. A state cannot impose regulatory requirements on Class III gaming outside what the compact provides, and it has no authority over Class II gaming. For how states recover their costs, see our analysis of state regulatory cost assessments.

How the layers overlap in practice

The same casino can be subject to all three layers. Consider a property offering both bingo-based electronic games and slot machines under a compact. The tribal gaming commission regulates both. The NIGC reviews the tribe's ordinance, receives its audit and can enforce federal standards across the facility. For the Class III floor, the state agency exercises whatever inspection and testing rights the compact grants. For the Class II floor, the state has no role.

Disputes over these boundaries are common. They can concern the scope of state authority under a compact, whether land qualifies as "Indian lands" eligible for gaming, or whether a new product is Class II or Class III. These are the questions that end up before the NIGC, the Interior Department or the courts. Our Legal Guide covers the statutory framework behind them.

Why the structure matters

For anyone evaluating a tribal casino, whether as a patron, vendor, lender or policy observer, the layered structure answers a basic question about accountability. The tribal regulator is the closest and most active. The federal layer provides a uniform oversight baseline across Indian Country. The state layer, where it exists, reflects a negotiated bargain, often tied to revenue sharing and exclusivity. Properties across the country operate under this system, and the tribal casino directory lets readers look up individual operators and states.

The specifics vary from tribe to tribe and compact to compact, so readers with a particular situation should consult the relevant ordinance and compact. The general rule holds: the tribe regulates first, the federal government oversees, and the state participates only to the extent a compact allows.
