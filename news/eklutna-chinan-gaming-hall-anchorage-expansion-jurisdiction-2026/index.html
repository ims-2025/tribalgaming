Eklutna's Chin'an Gaming Hall Expands as Alaska Jurisdiction Fight Endures

The Native Village of Eklutna's Anchorage-area gaming hall keeps growing even as the state and the Interior Department spar over whether Alaska Native land can host a casino.

By Marcus WhitehorsePublished July 08, 20266 min read

The Native Village of Eklutna is enlarging its Chin'an Gaming Hall on the northern edge of Anchorage, pressing ahead with a physical expansion of the facility even as the legal foundation for Alaska tribal gaming remains contested in the courts and inside the U.S. Department of the Interior. The hall, located in the Birchwood area off the Glenn Highway, is the only operating gaming facility in the Anchorage bowl, and the tribe has treated each phase of construction as a step toward a larger, permanent casino on the same allotment.

The current build-out adds roughly 5,000 square feet to the temporary structure, along with space intended for a bar once the tribe secures the necessary liquor licensing. Tribal representatives have said the expansion would roughly double employment at the site, to somewhere near 75 workers. For a small tribe with limited land and few other commercial anchors, the hall represents an unusually direct route to jobs and revenue, which helps explain why Eklutna has kept building through years of legal uncertainty.

Why the legal ground is still shifting

What makes the Eklutna project distinctive is that it sits at the intersection of two unresolved questions that do not arise the same way in the Lower 48. The first is jurisdictional: the State of Alaska sued in 2025 seeking to reassert its primary authority over Native allotments, arguing that a 2024 federal action had improperly narrowed the state's role over land held by Alaska Native people. The second is a definitional question about gaming rights themselves. The Interior Department signaled that Alaska Native entities do not automatically enjoy the same footing under federal gaming law that reservation tribes in the contiguous states do, a position that complicates any straightforward reading of the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act as applied to Alaska.

Those two threads are tangled together. Alaska has almost no reservations in the conventional sense; instead, the state's Native land base is dominated by allotments and corporate holdings created under the Alaska Native Claims Settlement Act. Whether a given parcel counts as the kind of "Indian lands" over which a tribe may conduct gaming is therefore not a formality but the whole ballgame. Readers who want the deeper background on how the settlement act reshaped jurisdiction can consult our analysis of the ANCSA jurisdiction reversal, which traces how a single administrative decision can move the line between state and tribal authority.

The hall keeps expanding not in spite of the legal fight but as a practical answer to it: a functioning business is harder to unwind than a proposal on paper.

What the expansion signals

The tribe's decision to keep investing carries a clear strategic logic. A gaming operation that is already open, staffed, and generating community benefit is a very different thing to challenge than a blueprint. Each addition of floor space, employees, and amenities raises the practical and political cost of forcing a shutdown, and it gives the tribe a concrete record of operation to point to if the dispute reaches a federal appeals court. That does not resolve the underlying questions, but it changes the posture of the parties.

It also matters for the class of gaming involved. Much of what operates in facilities like Chin'an falls under the bingo-based and electronic-aided categories that tribes can pursue without a state compact, rather than the Las Vegas-style Class III games that require negotiated agreements. That distinction—between what a tribe may offer on its own authority and what requires the state's sign-off—is the hinge of nearly every tribal gaming dispute in the country. Our explainer on Public Law 280 lays out how the criminal-versus-regulatory divide in state law determines what a tribe can do, and it is directly relevant to how Alaska's courts may frame the Eklutna question.

For now, the state's suit is unresolved, and Interior's posture leaves room for further administrative movement in either direction. The tribe, for its part, has been consistent: it intends to keep operating and building while the questions work their way through the system. Businesses and observers who want to understand the regulatory scaffolding behind decisions like these can review our legal guide to tribal gaming, which explains the roles of the Interior Department, the National Indian Gaming Commission, and the states.

A test case with national echoes

Although the immediate stakes are local—jobs in Birchwood, revenue for a small tribe, a single contested allotment—the Eklutna dispute is being watched well beyond Alaska. If federal authorities and the courts ultimately treat Alaska Native land as fully eligible for gaming on the same terms as Lower 48 reservations, the practical map of where tribal casinos can exist in the state would expand considerably. If, instead, the narrower reading prevails, Alaska's tribes may find their gaming ambitions boxed in by a jurisdictional framework that no amount of construction can overcome.

What is not in doubt is the trajectory of the facility itself. The Chin'an Gaming Hall opened modestly and has grown in stages, and this latest expansion continues that pattern. Whether the permanent casino the tribe envisions ever rises on the site will depend less on demand—which appears steady—than on how two governments answer a question that Congress never squarely resolved when it wrote the rules for Indian gaming a generation ago.
