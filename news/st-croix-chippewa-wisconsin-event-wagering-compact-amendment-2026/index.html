St. Croix Chippewa Signs Wisconsin Event-Wagering Compact

The amendment heads to a 45-day federal review, clearing the way for the tribe to become Wisconsin's second sports-betting operator.

By Elena RuizPublished July 31, 20264 min read

Wisconsin has taken another incremental step toward broader tribal sports betting. Governor Tony Evers and St. Croix Chippewa Indians of Wisconsin Chairman William Reynolds have signed a compact amendment authorizing event wagering at the tribe's casinos, a move that positions the St. Croix Chippewa to become the second tribe in the state to offer state-authorized sports betting. The signed amendment now goes to the U.S. Department of the Interior for the customary 45-day review before it can take legal effect.

The amendment permits wagering on both sporting and non-sporting events at St. Croix-operated and affiliated casino properties. It also contemplates remote event wagering, but on carefully bounded terms: the tribe may accept online wagers only on land it owns or that is held in trust for it by the federal government, and only where that land houses a commercial building the tribe owns or leases. That is a meaningful limitation. Rather than the statewide mobile framework that has powered handle in states such as Arizona and Michigan, Wisconsin's model keeps wagering tethered to Indian lands.

A tribe-by-tribe path

Wisconsin does not have a single, statewide sports-betting statute. Instead, each of the state's federally recognized tribes negotiates the activity into its own Class III compact through a bilateral amendment with the governor's office. The Oneida Nation of Wisconsin was the first to do so, launching retail sportsbooks at its Green Bay-area properties several years ago. The St. Croix Chippewa amendment makes the tribe the next in line, and it signals that Wisconsin's expansion of sports betting will continue to proceed one compact at a time rather than through a comprehensive legislative package.

That distinction matters for anyone tracking the national map. In the hub-and-spoke states, a single enabling law lets tribes serve as license holders for statewide mobile apps, dramatically enlarging the addressable market. Wisconsin's approach is narrower by design. It preserves the principle that gaming, including its digital forms, remains anchored to tribal lands and tribal regulatory authority. The trade-off is a smaller ceiling on handle, since a customer generally must be on qualifying tribal property to place a wager.

What the amendment does and does not do

For the St. Croix Chippewa, the practical effect is the ability to add retail sportsbooks and, within the geographic limits described above, some remote wagering capacity across their footprint in northwestern Wisconsin. The tribe operates gaming facilities in communities including Turtle Lake, Danbury and Hertel, and the amendment extends event wagering across affiliated properties rather than a single flagship location.

The amendment does not create a statewide online market, and it does not by itself resolve the broader policy debate in Madison over whether Wisconsin should authorize mobile sports betting more expansively. Legislation touching on mobile wagering has surfaced in the statehouse without producing a comprehensive framework, leaving the compact-amendment route as the operative mechanism. Economically, the near-term upside for the St. Croix Chippewa is likely to be measured rather than transformative. Retail and on-land sports betting typically carries thinner margins than the slot floor, and its value to a regionally focused operator lies as much in traffic and cross-sell, keeping patrons on property longer and pairing wagering with dining, hotel and entertainment spend, as in the direct hold on bets. In that sense, event wagering functions as an amenity that rounds out the guest experience, a pattern visible at tribal properties across the Upper Midwest that have added sportsbooks in recent years. The tribe has indicated its rollout will emphasize responsible-gaming safeguards alongside the new wagering menu.

The federal clock

With the governor's and chairman's signatures in place, the amendment enters the Interior Department's review window. Under the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act, the Secretary of the Interior has 45 days to approve, disapprove, or allow the amendment to take effect by operation of law. Approval is then published in the Federal Register, at which point the tribe can move to implement the new wagering authority. Assuming the review proceeds without objection, the St. Croix Chippewa could stand up event wagering operations in the months that follow.

The signing continues a steady, low-drama pattern of tribal gaming expansion in the Upper Midwest, where individual compact amendments, rather than sweeping ballot measures or omnibus bills, have become the dependable path to new gaming authority. It also underscores how differently states have approached sports betting since the fall of the federal prohibition: some racing toward statewide mobile markets, others, like Wisconsin, expanding cautiously and keeping the activity firmly within the framework of tribal sovereignty.
