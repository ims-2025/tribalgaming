Georgia Casino Gambling Push: Why Tribal Gaming Isn't in the Picture

Bottoms wants a casino vote to fund pre-K. Federal law and history leave tribes on the outside, but their border casinos have the most at stake.

By Marcus WhitehorsePublished November 21, 20264 min read

Georgia's long-running casino debate has a new champion. Governor-elect Keisha Lance Bottoms, who takes office in January, has said she wants Georgians to vote on whether to legalize casino gambling, with the proceeds directed to universal pre-K and early learning. Her campaign's education plan assumed casinos could produce $300 million to $500 million a year. Yet for all the attention the Georgia casino gambling push is drawing, one category of operator is almost entirely absent from the conversation: tribal nations.

That absence is not an oversight. It reflects the basic architecture of federal Indian gaming law, the history of tribal removal from the Southeast, and the way a Georgia amendment would likely be written. Understanding why tribal gaming is unlikely to be part of any Georgia outcome also clarifies what a Georgia casino market would mean for the tribal casinos that ring the state's borders.

What Bottoms is proposing, and what it would take

Legalizing casino gambling in Georgia requires a constitutional amendment. That means a two-thirds vote in both the House and the Senate before the question can go to voters. Speaking at the state Capitol on November 17, Bottoms tied her support for a referendum to federal funding gaps and to her goal of expanding pre-K. According to reporting by WJCL, Georgia's state-funded pre-K program enrolled about 71,500 children in 2023-24, roughly 55 percent of the state's four-year-olds, and enrolling the remaining children would cost an estimated $330 million a year more.

The legislative math is the hard part. The Atlanta Journal-Constitution has reported that Democrats hold a very narrow House majority while Republicans hold a similarly slim edge in the Senate. State Rep. Alan Powell, a Hartwell Republican, told the paper that with the numbers as they are, neither party can pass anything alone. Prior efforts have not cleared that bar: a Senate Republican casino and sports betting proposal never left committee, and an online sports betting measure failed. Opposition from groups such as the Georgia Baptist Mission Board remains organized.

The model Georgia has used before is instructive. The Georgia Lottery, created by constitutional amendment in 1992, funds the HOPE scholarship and the state's pre-K program. A casino amendment framed around early learning would follow the same template: state-authorized, state-taxed commercial gaming with revenue earmarked for education. Nothing in that structure creates a role for tribal governments.

Why tribal gaming is unlikely in Georgia

Under the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act, only federally recognized tribes may conduct gaming, and only on "Indian lands" as the statute defines them. Georgia has no federally recognized tribes headquartered within its borders. The Muscogee (Creek) and Cherokee nations, whose homelands covered much of present-day Georgia, were forcibly removed in the 1820s and 1830s; their governments today are based in Oklahoma and, in the case of the Eastern Band of Cherokee Indians, in western North Carolina. Georgia has extended state recognition to a small number of groups, but state recognition does not confer eligibility under IGRA. (For the distinction, see our explainer on federal recognition and gaming eligibility.)

A tribe based elsewhere could theoretically seek to have land in Georgia taken into trust for gaming. But land acquired after 1988 is generally barred from gaming unless it fits a statutory exception or wins a "two-part determination" from the Secretary of the Interior, which also requires the governor's concurrence. That process routinely takes many years and faces local opposition, environmental review and litigation. Our Legal Guide walks through the Section 20 framework in detail.

Even if voters approve casinos, the most likely result is a commercial market licensed and taxed by the state, not compact-based tribal gaming.

None of the reporting on the Bottoms proposal has identified a tribal nation actively pursuing a Georgia site, and the referendum concept as described does not specify how many casinos would be permitted or who could hold licenses. One commercial real estate executive quoted by the AJC estimated the state could support as many as nine casinos; a casino company has already secured an option on a vacant department store at Macon Mall.

The tribal stake is across the border

The more concrete tribal interest in Georgia's debate is competitive. Georgia is among the most populous states that bans both casinos and sports betting, and its residents have long traveled to tribal properties in neighboring states. The Eastern Band of Cherokee Indians operates Harrah's Cherokee Valley River Casino in Murphy, North Carolina, a short drive from the north Georgia line, along with its flagship in Cherokee. The Poarch Band of Creek Indians operates Wind Creek properties in Alabama. To the south, the Seminole Tribe of Florida runs Hard Rock-branded casinos across Florida (see the Florida state hub), and to the northeast the Catawba Nation is completing its permanent resort in Kings Mountain, North Carolina.

A metro Atlanta casino would almost certainly draw some of that traffic back home. The scale of any impact would depend on how many licenses Georgia authorizes, where they are sited, and whether sports betting is bundled into the same amendment. Tribal operators in border markets elsewhere have faced similar shifts when neighbors legalized; our analysis of the Southeast as tribal gaming's next frontier outlines how the region's map has been changing.

What to watch in 2027

Bottoms is expected to be inaugurated in January, and the General Assembly convenes the same month. The early signals will be whether a casino resolution is introduced with bipartisan sponsors, whether it carries a specific license count and siting rules, and whether sports betting is included. For tribal nations in the region, the relevant question is less whether they can enter Georgia than how much of their existing Georgia customer base they would retain if the state opens its own market.

For now, any Georgia casino question remains at least two legislative supermajorities and a statewide vote away. Tribal operators in North Carolina, Alabama and Florida will be watching closely.
