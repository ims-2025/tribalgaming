Lumbee Tribe Rejects Constitutional Amendment to Enable Casino Gaming

A narrow process fix drew a favorable expert legal review—then voters said no, leaving the I-95 casino vision without a constitutional path forward.

By Elena RuizPublished June 25, 20265 min read

Members of the Lumbee Tribe of North Carolina voted on June 23 to reject a set of constitutional amendments that would have created a workable internal process for adopting a tribal gaming ordinance, halting—at least for now—the tribe's effort to pursue casino gaming along the Interstate 95 corridor in Robeson County. Tribal Chairman John L. Lowery confirmed the outcome after the special election, telling members the proposal had failed at the ballot box.

The vote closes a months-long chapter that had moved the question of Lumbee tribal gaming from the realm of speculation into a concrete governance decision. It also underscores a recurring reality in Indian Country: before a tribe can negotiate a compact, build a casino, or share in gaming revenue, it must first put its own constitutional and regulatory house in order—and its citizens have to agree.

What the amendment would have done

The proposal was narrower than its casino framing suggested. Rather than authorizing a specific resort, it sought to fix a structural gap in the Lumbee constitution: as written, the governing document did not contain a clear, functional mechanism for the tribe to enact a gaming ordinance—the foundational legal instrument that any tribe must adopt before it can lawfully operate gaming under the federal framework.

To test that question, Lumbee leadership commissioned an independent legal review from Philip Hogen, an attorney with Hogen Adams and a member of the Oglala Sioux Tribe who served on the National Indian Gaming Commission for a decade, including seven years as chairman. Hogen's opinion was direct: the existing constitution did not provide a workable path to approve a gaming ordinance, while the proposed amendments did create such a process and contained nothing he viewed as conflicting with the federal Indian Gaming Regulatory Act. In other words, the changes were about plumbing, not policy—giving the tribe the procedural ability to act, without committing it to any particular development. For readers new to that instrument, our explainer on how a tribal gaming ordinance is approved walks through why it sits at the base of every tribal gaming operation.

Even with a favorable legal review in hand, the measure did not carry. The result suggests that for a meaningful share of Lumbee voters, the decision was about more than drafting mechanics—reflecting broader community debate over whether commercial gaming fits the tribe's priorities. Casino proposals routinely surface deep questions inside a tribe about cultural identity, economic dependence on gaming revenue, and the social effects of a large entertainment operation, and those questions do not resolve simply because the legal path is clear. A clean legal opinion can answer whether a tribe may act; it cannot answer whether it should.

A recognition question that complicates the path

The Lumbee occupy an unusual position in federal Indian law. The tribe has long sought full federal recognition, a status that carries practical consequences for gaming because the federal framework generally extends only to federally recognized tribes operating on qualifying Indian lands. That recognition question remains a separate and unresolved track from the constitutional vote, and it would shape any future gaming ambitions regardless of how the amendment had fared. Our explainer on federal recognition and gaming eligibility details why that threshold matters before any compact conversation can begin.

The amendment was about giving the tribe the ability to act on gaming—not a decision to build anything. Voters declined to take even that first procedural step.

Because the two issues are intertwined, the failed vote does not foreclose the recognition effort, nor does recognition guarantee a casino. But it does mean that, for the moment, the Lumbee lack the internal constitutional machinery that a gaming enterprise would require—a gap that would have to be addressed in a future election before the tribe could credibly approach North Carolina about a compact.

What comes next

Tribal leadership has not signaled a specific timeline for revisiting the question, and constitutional amendments of this kind typically require a deliberate re-engagement with the membership rather than a quick re-vote. Any renewed push would likely pair clearer public education about what an ordinance does—and does not—commit the tribe to, with a broader conversation about economic development goals.

For North Carolina, where the Eastern Band of Cherokee Indians has operated the state's established tribal gaming market for decades, the Lumbee result removes near-term uncertainty about a potential second tribal operator entering along I-95. It also illustrates how the early, internal stages of tribal gaming—constitutional authority, ordinance adoption, and recognition status—often prove more decisive than the later, more visible steps of compacting and construction.

The vote is a reminder that tribal gaming begins with self-governance. Compacts, revenue sharing, and resort design draw the headlines, but they all rest on a tribe's own constitutional foundation. Readers tracking how those later steps unfold can consult our Legal Guide to IGRA and Class III gaming, and our earlier coverage of the Lumbee special election for the run-up to this week's decision. For now, the Lumbee have chosen to keep the door to gaming closed—an outcome that leaves the tribe's options open but its path unbuilt.
