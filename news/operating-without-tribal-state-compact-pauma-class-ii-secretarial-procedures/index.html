Operating Without a Compact: Pauma, Class II and the Limits of IGRA

Pauma's sportsbook is the boldest test yet of what tribes can do outside the compact system. Two other paths show where the boundaries lie.

By Daniel YazziePublished November 21, 20264 min read

When the Pauma Band of Luiseño Indians switched on eight sports wagering kiosks at Casino Pauma in San Diego County on November 12, it did something no other California tribe has done: it began offering sports betting in a state that has not legalized it. The move rests on an unusual legal footing. Pauma does not operate under a tribal-state compact at all. It conducts Class III gaming under federal secretarial procedures issued by the Department of the Interior. That makes Pauma the latest and most visible example of a broader question in Indian gaming: how far can a tribe go when it is operating without a tribal-state compact?

Three distinct paths exist for tribes outside the standard compact model. Each has different limits, and each is being tested in 2026.

Path one: secretarial procedures

The Indian Gaming Regulatory Act was designed around compacts negotiated between tribes and states. When the Supreme Court held in Seminole Tribe v. Florida (1996) that states could invoke sovereign immunity to avoid good-faith-negotiation lawsuits, Congress's remedy for an uncooperative state was weakened. Interior responded with regulations, now at 25 CFR part 291, allowing the Secretary to issue Class III gaming procedures in place of a compact in certain circumstances. Courts have also ordered mediation that ends with procedures when a state is found to have negotiated in bad faith.

Pauma's history shows how a tribe arrives there. It signed a Class III compact in 1999. A later dispute over the cost of additional slot licenses led to litigation in which a federal court ordered the state to refund $36.2 million in overpayments. In 2013 the tribe was permitted to withdraw from its 1999 compact, and when a new agreement could not be reached, it proceeded under secretarial procedures. Our explainer on secretarial procedures covers the mechanics.

The tribe's position is that federal law, not California's ban, determines what it may offer. Tribal Council Chairman Temet Majel described the sportsbook as part of the tribe's commitment to self-determination. Whether the procedures actually authorize sports wagering, and whether California or federal authorities will challenge the launch, are open questions. The state had not publicly responded as of mid-November, according to published reports. Our earlier piece on Pauma's legal theory examines the arguments on both sides.

Path two: Class II gaming

The second path does not require state consent at all. Under IGRA, Class II gaming, which includes bingo and games similar to bingo, including electronic versions, is regulated by tribes and the National Indian Gaming Commission. A tribe may offer Class II games on its Indian lands if the state permits bingo for any purpose. No compact is needed and no revenue is shared with the state.

Texas illustrates both the potential and the limits. The Kickapoo Traditional Tribe of Texas operates Lucky Eagle Casino in Eagle Pass under IGRA's Class II framework. The Alabama-Coushatta Tribe of Texas and the Ysleta del Sur Pueblo are governed by a separate 1987 Restoration Act, and their right to offer electronic bingo was contested for decades until the Supreme Court ruled in Ysleta del Sur Pueblo v. Texas (2022) that the Act bars only gaming that Texas prohibits outright, not gaming it regulates. Since then, the Alabama-Coushatta have continued operating Naskila Casino near Livingston and opened a temporary facility in Leggett.

The ceiling on this path is the game itself. Class II excludes house-banked table games, traditional slot machines and, under most readings, sports wagering. Operators compensate with sophisticated electronic bingo cabinets that resemble slots, but the product and the revenue potential remain more constrained than Class III. See our Class II vs. Class III explainer for the boundary.

Path three: litigation and pressure

The third path is less a legal status than a strategy. Tribes without compacts, or with compacts they consider inadequate, can use litigation, Class II operations and political engagement to build leverage for an eventual agreement. The Seminole Tribe of Florida operated high-stakes bingo for decades before reaching a Class III compact. Other tribes have kept Class II floors running through years of failed negotiations.

Each path trades certainty for autonomy. A compact brings state agreement and broader game rights; operating outside one preserves independence but invites legal risk.

What the Pauma test means for others

For the roughly 60-plus California tribes with Class III compacts, Pauma's sportsbook is not a template they can easily follow; their compacts define the games they may offer, and sports wagering is not among them. Its significance is as a test of what secretarial procedures can authorize and how states respond when a tribe acts on federal authority alone.

If the launch stands, it may encourage other tribes in difficult compacting relationships to reconsider the procedures route. If it is challenged successfully, it will reinforce that, in practice, broad Class III offerings still depend on a negotiated relationship with the state. Either way, the episode is a reminder that IGRA leaves meaningful space between full compacting and no gaming at all. Our California state hub tracks the state's tribal properties.
