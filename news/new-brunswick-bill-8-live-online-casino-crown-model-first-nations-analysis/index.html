New Brunswick's Live Online Casino Bill Keeps First Nations on the Sidelines

Bill 8 extends Crown control into live dealer iGaming just as First Nations elsewhere in Canada move from revenue-sharing toward ownership.

By Linda CardinalPublished November 22, 20265 min read

New Brunswick has moved to let Atlantic Lottery offer live dealer casino games online, and the way it has done so says a great deal about where First Nations stand in Atlantic Canada's gaming market. Bill 8, an amendment to the province's Gaming Control Act introduced by Finance and Treasury Board Minister René Legacy, cleared the legislature's Standing Committee on Economic Policy without amendment on November 17, and industry reports indicate it passed third reading on November 20, leaving Royal Assent as the remaining legislative step.

The bill keeps online gaming entirely in Crown hands. It creates no licensing route for private operators and no role for First Nations operators. That choice stands in contrast to the models in Ontario and Alberta, and to the ownership shift that has transformed First Nations gaming in Western Canada over the past two years.

What New Brunswick's Bill 8 does

The amendment defines an "online casino lottery scheme" and a "live casino lottery scheme," the latter covering games hosted or presented by a live person. With approval from the Lieutenant-Governor in Council, the New Brunswick Lotteries and Gaming Corporation could designate a qualifying corporate body as a provincial agency to conduct live casino games. A spokesperson for the minister's office has confirmed the bill applies only to Atlantic Lottery Corporation, the Crown corporation owned by the four Atlantic provinces, which already offers online casino games in New Brunswick and has done so since 2020.

The figures involved are modest. Legacy has said New Brunswick takes in about $45 million a year from its share of Atlantic Lottery's iGaming business and estimated that live casino would add $1.9 million in the first year and $2.4 million in the second. Atlantic Lottery says it holds about 35% of the online market in its region and that more than $250 million a year flows to unregulated sites. The minister has said New Brunswick is the last Atlantic province without live dealer games and that a wider review of commercial iGaming is ongoing, with Ontario and Alberta among the models being studied. The law takes effect on a date to be set by proclamation, and the government still has to bring forward regulations.

Why the Crown model matters for First Nations

Under the Criminal Code, gaming in Canada is lawful only when it is conducted and managed by a province or licensed under provincial authority. First Nations participation has therefore been negotiated with provinces rather than exercised as an independent jurisdiction, as our explainer on Section 207 and provincial authority sets out. In Atlantic Canada, those arrangements have historically centred on on-reserve gaming and revenue agreements rather than First Nations-owned destination properties or online platforms.

Bill 8 adds a new online product to a system in which First Nations are, at most, revenue participants rather than operators.

Bill 8 does not change that structure; it reinforces it. By routing live online casino exclusively through Atlantic Lottery, the province is extending Crown control into the fastest-growing part of the market at a moment when First Nations elsewhere in Canada are moving from revenue-sharing toward ownership. In British Columbia and Alberta, First Nations have acquired major casino portfolios. In Saskatchewan, the Saskatchewan Indian Gaming Authority operates casinos under a province-wide agreement, as described in our coverage of SIGA's expansion. Our comparison of Canadian First Nations gaming frameworks maps how differently those provinces treat First Nations participation.

New Brunswick's Wolastoqey and Mi'kmaq communities are not absent from the gaming conversation. Sitansisk, also known as St. Mary's First Nation, announced plans in September 2026 for a $170-million casino, entertainment centre and hotel in Fredericton, a project that would require provincial approvals and an operating arrangement that has not been publicly detailed. Our report on the Sitansisk Fredericton casino resort proposal covers what is known about that plan. Separately, Tobique First Nation has asserted its own regulatory jurisdiction, most recently by enacting a Prediction Markets Act alongside its existing gaming commission, an approach examined in our coverage of Tobique's prediction markets authority. Neither initiative is addressed by Bill 8, and the public reporting on the bill does not indicate that First Nations were consulted on its design.

The questions Bill 8 leaves open

The bill is narrow, and its narrowness is the point for observers of First Nations gaming. Three questions follow from it.

The first is whether New Brunswick's broader iGaming review will consider any role for First Nations. Saskatchewan has given SIGA a path into online gaming, while the legal framework for Ontario's open market is itself before the Supreme Court of Canada in a case whose reading of the Criminal Code could shape First Nations arrangements, as our coverage of the Ontario iGaming Supreme Court case explains. If New Brunswick eventually opens its market, the terms on which First Nations could participate, whether as operators, partners or revenue recipients, will be a central policy choice.

The second is how Crown online expansion interacts with on-reserve gaming revenue. Online products compete for the same discretionary spending as land-based play, including at First Nations venues. Revenue arrangements negotiated around physical gaming do not automatically extend to online growth, and communities that rely on land-based gaming income have reason to watch how the province accounts for that shift.

The third is jurisdictional. Tobique's assertion of inherent authority and the long-running example of Kahnawake in Quebec, discussed in our analysis of Kahnawake as a First Nations digital gaming jurisdiction, show that some First Nations do not accept that online gaming belongs exclusively to provinces. A Crown-only statute does not resolve that disagreement; it simply restates the provincial position.

For now, Bill 8 is a modest revenue measure that brings New Brunswick in line with its Atlantic neighbours. Its longer-term significance for First Nations will depend on what the province does next with its iGaming review, and on whether proposals such as the Sitansisk development produce a provincial agreement that gives a New Brunswick First Nation an operator's role for the first time.
