First Nations-Owned PURE Casino Joins Global Betting Integrity Body

Alberta's largest casino operator is Indigenous-owned — and now measures its sportsbook compliance against an international standard.

By Linda CardinalPublished August 27, 20264 min read

PURE Casino Entertainment, the operating arm of First Nations-owned Indigenous Gaming Partners, has joined the International Betting Integrity Association, becoming one of the few Indigenous-owned gaming operators in North America to hold membership in a global sports betting integrity body. The move formalizes the First Nations betting integrity commitments that come with running a licensed online sportsbook in Alberta's newly opened market, and it lands barely two months after the company completed the acquisition that made it the province's largest casino operator.

Indigenous Gaming Partners was formed in 2024 as a partnership between five Nova Scotia-based First Nations and Sonco Gaming Inc., a casino management and development firm. The structure was unusual from the outset: Atlantic Canada First Nations acquiring and operating commercial casino assets nearly 4,000 kilometres west, in a province where they hold no land base and no treaty relationship. It has since become one of the most-watched ownership experiments in Canadian gaming.

From four casinos to seven, and then online

The group's Alberta footprint began with Pure Casino Edmonton, Pure Casino Yellowhead, Pure Casino Calgary and Pure Casino Lethbridge. On June 24, 2026, IGP closed its acquisition of Gamehost Inc., taking all outstanding shares at $13.65 per share in cash and lifting its brick-and-mortar count to seven properties. That transaction made a First Nations-controlled entity the largest casino operator in Alberta by property count, a milestone we examined in detail when the Gamehost deal closed.

The online expansion followed almost immediately. Alberta's regulated iGaming market opened to the public on July 13, 2026, ending the province's single-site model and admitting private operators alongside the government channel. PURE launched purecasino.ca on July 7 as the province's 27th licensed online gambling site, making it the first Alberta land-based operator to carry its own brand into the regulated digital market. Kambi was selected to power the sportsbook.

Membership in the International Betting Integrity Association is the logical next step for an operator that now takes sports wagers. The association operates a monitoring and alert network that flags suspicious betting patterns across member books and shares those signals with regulators, leagues and law enforcement. For an operator entering a competitive open market against internationally scaled competitors, membership is also a credentialing exercise.

Why integrity membership matters more for Indigenous operators

Sports betting integrity infrastructure is not free, and it is not a legal requirement in most Canadian jurisdictions. Operators join because regulators, leagues and commercial partners increasingly expect it, and because the cost of being the book that missed a manipulated market is far higher than the subscription.

For Indigenous-owned operators specifically, the calculation carries an additional dimension. Indigenous gaming enterprises in both Canada and the United States have historically operated under heightened scrutiny of their regulatory capacity, a dynamic that shaped the entire architecture of the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act south of the border. Voluntary participation in a global integrity network is a direct answer to that scrutiny. It signals that the operator's compliance posture is measured against international peers rather than against a lower bar.

The pattern across Canada is consistent: First Nations groups are no longer negotiating for a share of gaming revenue generated by someone else. They are acquiring the operating businesses, and inheriting the regulatory obligations that come with them.

That shift is visible well beyond Alberta. In British Columbia, the Snuneymuxw First Nation has acquired five casino properties from Great Canadian Entertainment over roughly two years, including River Rock Casino Resort and Casino Nanaimo. We covered that consolidation in our analysis of First Nations ownership reshaping Canadian gaming. The common thread is a move from revenue-sharing agreements toward direct equity and operational control.

The competitive test still ahead

Integrity membership does not resolve PURE's central commercial challenge. Alberta's open market admitted dozens of licensed brands, several with marketing budgets and player databases that dwarf a regional operator's. The company's advantage is a physical network of seven casinos and the omnichannel loyalty economics that come with it — the same playbook regional operators have used in Ontario since 2022, with mixed results.

Ontario's experience offers a cautionary reference point. Land-based operators there discovered that an open iGaming market can cannibalize floor visitation as readily as it adds incremental revenue, a dynamic we traced in our examination of iGaming cannibalization of land-based First Nations casinos. Alberta's operators are entering that same environment with the benefit of hindsight but not immunity.

What distinguishes IGP's position is that the same ownership group controls both channels. Where an Ontario First Nation might hold a revenue-sharing interest in a land-based property while watching an unaffiliated online brand capture its customers, PURE's online losses and land-based gains flow to the same balance sheet. That does not eliminate cannibalization risk, but it converts a structural conflict into an internal allocation question.

A template other nations are studying

The Nova Scotia First Nations behind IGP took on meaningful acquisition debt and a management partnership to build this position. The wager is that operating margin and asset appreciation will outperform the passive revenue-sharing arrangements that have defined most First Nations gaming participation in Canada. Those arrangements — Saskatchewan's SIGA model, Ontario's OFNLP allocation, Manitoba's provincial framework — deliver predictable transfers without operational risk or operational upside.

Whether the acquisition model outperforms will not be clear for several years. What is already clear is that a Canadian gaming market long organized around provincial crown corporations and a handful of commercial operators now includes First Nations entities at every layer: as landlords, as revenue-share recipients, as licensed land-based operators, as online brands, and now as participants in the global integrity apparatus that governs sports wagering.
