Oklahoma Tribal Gaming Market: How the Model Compact Shapes It

No commercial casinos, one uniform compact and some of the largest tribal operators in the country. How Oklahoma's market works.

By James KingfisherPublished November 21, 20264 min read

Few states depend on tribal gaming the way Oklahoma does. There are no commercial casinos in the state; nearly every slot machine and card table belongs to a tribal nation. The Oklahoma tribal gaming market spans more than 130 facilities operated by dozens of tribes, from roadside travel-plaza casinos to WinStar World Casino and Resort, one of the largest gaming floors in the world. This explainer covers how the market is structured, how the state compact works, what changed during the dispute of 2019 and 2020, and the questions that will shape the next several years.

How the Oklahoma model compact works

Modern Oklahoma gaming dates to 2004, when voters approved State Question 712, enacting the State-Tribal Gaming Act. Rather than negotiating individual agreements tribe by tribe, the Act set out a model compact that any eligible tribe could accept. Dozens did, which is why Oklahoma's tribal gaming rules are unusually uniform. Our model compact explainer goes clause by clause.

The core bargain is exclusivity in exchange for fees. Tribes receive the exclusive right to offer covered electronic games and certain card games, and in return pay the state a percentage of revenue on a sliding scale: 4 percent of the first $10 million in annual adjusted net win from covered electronic games, 5 percent of the next $10 million, and 6 percent above $20 million, plus 10 percent of monthly net win from non-house-banked card games. The fees have totaled more than $200 million a year in recent years, and most of the money is directed to education. Our analysis of exclusivity fees and the education fund traces where it goes.

The compact also limits commercial competition. Horse racetracks are permitted a capped number of electronic games, with their own fee structure, and the state agrees not to authorize broader commercial gaming without consequences for the fee arrangement.

Who operates in Oklahoma

Oklahoma is home to nearly 40 federally recognized tribes, and many operate gaming. A handful of nations dominate by scale. The Chickasaw Nation operates WinStar in Thackerville, near the Texas border, along with a large network of other casinos. The Choctaw Nation operates Choctaw Casino & Resort in Durant and properties across southeastern Oklahoma. The Cherokee Nation, through Cherokee Nation Entertainment, operates Hard Rock Hotel & Casino Tulsa and other venues (see our Cherokee Nation Entertainment profile). The Muscogee (Creek) Nation operates River Spirit Casino Resort in Tulsa, and the Osage Nation runs a chain of Osage Casinos.

Geography explains much of the market's success. The southern properties draw heavily from the Dallas-Fort Worth metro area, since Texas does not permit casinos. Northeastern properties compete for Tulsa and draw from Kansas, Missouri and Arkansas. That cross-border pull has made Oklahoma one of the largest tribal gaming states in the country by revenue, and gaming revenue funds tribal health care, education, housing and infrastructure throughout the state.

Regulation follows the layered model used nationally. Each tribe maintains its own gaming commission that licenses employees and vendors and enforces internal controls, the National Indian Gaming Commission oversees tribal gaming ordinances and Class II operations, and the state, through the Office of Management and Enterprise Services, has a compliance role for covered games under the compact. Because so many tribes share one compact template, technical standards and audit expectations are broadly similar from one property to the next, which has helped the state's supplier and vendor ecosystem grow alongside the casinos.

The 2019-2020 compact dispute

The model compacts carried 15-year terms ending January 1, 2020, with language providing for automatic renewal. In 2019, Gov. Kevin Stitt argued that the compacts expired and sought to renegotiate the fee rates. The Cherokee, Chickasaw and Choctaw nations sued on December 31, 2019, and other tribes joined. In July 2020, U.S. District Judge Timothy DeGiusti ruled that the compacts had automatically renewed for additional 15-year terms.

During the dispute, Stitt signed separate compacts with several tribes, including provisions for sports betting. The Oklahoma Supreme Court held in 2020 that the governor lacked authority to enter compacts that authorized games not permitted under state law. The episode left a lasting imprint on tribal-state relations and is one reason the arrival of a new governor in January 2027 is being watched closely; our analysis of incoming governors and tribal compacts covers the transition.

Oklahoma's model compact gave tribes a stable, uniform framework. Its renewal fight showed how much that stability depends on the governor's office.

Open questions for the market

Sports betting is the most prominent. Oklahoma has not legalized it, and proposals have repeatedly stalled over how to structure tribal exclusivity, whether to include mobile wagering and how fees would be set. Because the compact framework is uniform, any expansion would likely require legislation and then amendments that dozens of tribes would need to accept.

A second question is prediction markets. Oklahoma tribes have been among the most vocal critics of federally regulated sports event contracts offered on their customers' phones, arguing that they undermine the exclusivity tribes pay for. A third is renewal itself: with the current terms running into the 2030s, tribes and the state have time, but both sides know that negotiations over fees and game scope will eventually resume.
