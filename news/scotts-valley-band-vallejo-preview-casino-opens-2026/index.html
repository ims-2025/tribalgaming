Scotts Valley Band Opens Class II Preview Casino in Vallejo

A 100-machine modular hall opens to invited guests as the tribe awaits a fee-to-trust decision that would clear its full resort.

By Elena RuizPublished July 28, 20264 min read

The Scotts Valley Band of Pomo Indians opened a small Class II preview casino in Vallejo, California, on July 25, marking the tribe's first live gaming operation at a site it hopes to grow into a $700 million resort. The soft opening, limited to invited guests, arrives as the U.S. Department of the Interior nears a decision on whether the tribe can build the far larger permanent casino it has proposed near the intersection of Interstate 80 and Highway 37.

The preview facility runs up to 100 Class II gaming machines housed in two repurposed modular structures on the property, operating by appointment from 10 a.m. to 6 p.m. The tribe secured sign-off from both the National Indian Gaming Commission and its own gaming commission before opening. The debut came roughly seven months later than the tribe originally planned, a delay that underscores how tightly the project's timeline is bound to federal process.

Why Class II, and why now

The choice to open with Class II machines is deliberate. Class II gaming — technically a form of electronic bingo — does not require a tribal-state compact, which lets a tribe begin operations on eligible land without negotiating Class III terms with the state first. That distinction, and what separates the two categories on the floor, is laid out in our Class II versus Class III explainer. For a tribe waiting on a federal land decision, a Class II beachhead is a way to establish a functioning operation and demonstrate demand while the larger questions remain open.

A preview hall lets the tribe put machines on the floor now, while the fee-to-trust question that governs the full resort is still before the Interior Department.

The pending federal decision concerns whether the Vallejo parcel can be taken into trust for gaming, the threshold determination that governs almost every off-reservation casino proposal. The stakes and the standards involved are the subject of our Section 20 two-part determination explainer. A favorable ruling would clear the way for the permanent build; an unfavorable one would leave the tribe reliant on the modest preview footprint.

The preview format also lets the tribe begin building the operational muscle a permanent resort will require. Even a 100-machine hall demands a licensed workforce, functioning internal controls, surveillance, and cash-handling systems that satisfy both the tribe's gaming commission and the NIGC. Standing those up now, at small scale, reduces the execution risk of a later full-scale opening — the tribe will not be launching a $700 million resort as a first-time operator, but as one that has already run a live floor.

A contested project in a crowded region

The Vallejo proposal has drawn scrutiny from local officials and from other tribes wary of off-reservation gaming precedents, and it sits in one of the most competitive casino regions in the country, the greater San Francisco Bay Area. Interior's recent willingness to revisit trust decisions has added uncertainty for projects of this kind; we examined that trend in our coverage of fee-to-trust reversals and off-reservation risk. For Scotts Valley, the preview opening is both a milestone and a hedge — proof of progress that does not depend on the outcome still pending in Washington.

Off-reservation proposals are among the most contested in tribal gaming precisely because they can place a casino in a populous market far from a tribe's historic land base, drawing objections from nearby governments, commercial competitors, and other tribes protective of their own catchments. That opposition typically plays out through the trust and environmental review process rather than at the gaming-license stage, which is why the Interior decision carries so much weight here. A seven-month slip on the preview opening is a reminder of how much friction these projects absorb before a single machine goes live.

The Bay Area setting sharpens the calculus. Proximity to a dense, affluent population is exactly what makes the Vallejo site valuable and what makes it contested. If the trust application succeeds, the tribe would hold one of the more strategically located gaming footprints in Northern California; if it fails, the modular hall's appointment-only model caps how much of that demand the tribe can realistically serve.

What to watch next

The appointment-only structure is itself telling. By opening to invited guests during limited daytime hours, the tribe can validate systems and gauge demand without over-committing staff or capital to a footprint that may soon be superseded. It is a soft launch in the most literal sense — a way to be operational without being fully exposed, and to gather real operating data that will inform the design and staffing of the permanent resort if it is approved.

The immediate signal will be the Interior decision itself, expected in the near term. Beyond that, the tribe will need to convert appointment-only preview traffic into a sustainable operation and manage the transition from modular buildings to permanent construction if the trust approval comes through. Readers following California's crowded development pipeline can track the region through the California state hub. Either way, the Vallejo opening shows how tribes are using Class II operations to hold ground while the slower machinery of federal land policy grinds on.
