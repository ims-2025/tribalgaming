Suing Tribal Officials in Gaming Disputes: How Immunity Works

Why gaming lawsuits often name individuals instead of the tribe, and where the limits of that strategy lie.

By Daniel YazziePublished October 22, 20264 min read

When a tribe sues another tribe over a casino, or a patron or vendor sues over a gaming dispute, the complaint often names individual tribal officials rather than the tribe itself. The reason is a body of law about when sovereign immunity stops and individual liability begins. This explainer describes how suing tribal officials works in gaming cases, what the key doctrines are, and where the limits lie. It is general background, not legal advice.

The starting point: tribal sovereign immunity

Federal Indian law treats tribes as sovereigns that generally cannot be sued without their consent or a clear waiver by Congress. The Supreme Court confirmed the breadth of that rule in Michigan v. Bay Mills Indian Community (2014), a case we describe in our Bay Mills explainer. Immunity extends to tribal enterprises, including casinos operated as arms of the tribe, which is why disputes with patrons and vendors often turn on contract language, as our piece on patron disputes and sovereign immunity explains.

Suits against officials

Immunity belongs to the sovereign, so the question is whether it also protects the people who act for it. Courts distinguish between official-capacity and individual-capacity claims. An official-capacity claim is effectively a claim against the tribe and is usually barred by immunity. An individual-capacity claim seeks to hold the person liable, and the Supreme Court held in Lewis v. Clarke (2017) that a tribal employee sued personally for conduct in the scope of employment could not claim the tribe's immunity, because the real party in interest was the employee.

A separate route is the doctrine from Ex parte Young, a 1908 decision allowing suits against government officials for prospective relief to stop ongoing violations of federal law. Courts have applied versions of this reasoning in tribal contexts, though its reach depends on the facts and the statute at issue. Plaintiffs typically use it to seek an injunction, not damages.

How the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act changes things

IGRA creates specific causes of action and specific waivers. It authorizes a tribe to sue a state that fails to negotiate in good faith over a Class III compact, a process outlined in our explainer on the good faith negotiation lawsuit. It also gives the National Indian Gaming Commission enforcement authority over tribal operations. Whether other claims against tribes or their officials can proceed depends on the statute's text and on how courts read it.

A recent illustration is the Tenth Circuit's April 21, 2026 decision in Comanche Nation v. Ware. According to legal press reports, the court found that IGRA invalidates sovereign immunity in that dispute and rejected an argument that immunity covers racketeering claims against tribal officials sued individually. We summarize it in our decision report. The holding is specific to that case and should not be generalized without reading the opinion.

What this does not mean

Several misconceptions are worth correcting. Naming an official does not guarantee a case survives; plaintiffs must still plead a viable claim and establish standing. Officials sued individually may have defenses, including that they acted within lawful authority. Tribes can also indemnify officials, which means the practical financial burden may still fall on the tribe. And suits against officials do not disturb the broader rule that the tribe itself is immune absent waiver or abrogation.

Why it matters in practice

For tribal gaming operators, these doctrines shape governance. Boards and commissions often adopt written procedures, document decisions and review contract waivers carefully, since a personal-capacity claim can attract leaders' attention in a way an enterprise-level claim may not. For vendors and lenders, the doctrine explains why many agreements contain limited waivers of immunity and designate forums; see our discussion of limited sovereign immunity waivers. For competitors and members, it shows that immunity is substantial but not absolute.

Readers who want a broader orientation to the legal framework can start with the TribalGaming.com Legal Guide. Anyone facing an actual dispute should consult counsel experienced in federal Indian law.

Practical takeaways

Three points are worth remembering. First, the identity of the defendant determines the doctrine: a claim against the tribe or an official in an official capacity raises immunity questions, while a claim against a person in an individual capacity raises different ones. Second, remedies differ, since prospective injunctions and personal damages follow separate rules. Third, the statute matters, because IGRA and other federal laws can alter the baseline in ways that vary from case to case.

Because this area of law continues to develop, summaries like this one are best treated as orientation. Primary sources, including the opinions cited here, are the right place to confirm current holdings before relying on them for any decision.
