CFTC Geofencing Guidance Collides With Tribal-Land Kalshi Injunction

A federal court wants a line drawn around tribal land. The federal derivatives regulator says lines drawn by location are presumptively out of bounds.

By Marcus WhitehorsePublished November 14, 20265 min read

The Commodity Futures Trading Commission has told federally registered exchanges that blocking customers by U.S. location is generally inconsistent with federal law, guidance that arrived one day after a federal judge ordered prediction-market operator Kalshi to keep its sports contracts off two California tribes' lands. The CFTC geofencing interpretation, issued November 5, gives designated contract markets 30 days to remove domestic geographic restrictions that conflict with the agency's impartial-access requirement, or to explain to regulators why they cannot.

The timing has placed tribal governments and the federal derivatives regulator on opposite sides of the same technical question: whether an exchange can, or must, draw a line around Indian lands. For tribes, a working geofence is the practical means by which a court order protecting their gaming jurisdiction is enforced. For the CFTC, location-based access limits are, in Chairman Michael Selig's words, "inconsistent with those purposes and the Act's impartial access requirements."

What the CFTC geofencing interpretation says

The interpretation addresses Commission Regulation 38.151(b), which requires designated contract markets to provide competitive, open, fair and impartial access to their platforms. Under the guidance, exchanges have 30 days either to stop domestic geographic restrictions that conflict with that requirement or to submit an explanation to the agency's Division of Market Oversight of why compliance is not feasible. Exchanges are also expected to ensure that intermediaries, such as brokers and distribution partners, are not impeding compliance.

The guidance stops short of several things. Published summaries of the release indicate that it does not announce penalties, does not find that any named operator has violated the rule, and does not purport to dissolve existing court orders or settlements. The Commission said it would weigh the facts and circumstances of particular cases before taking any action. That makes the interpretation a statement of the agency's position rather than a direct override, but it is a position that, read literally, sits uneasily beside court orders requiring exchanges to restrict access in particular places.

The 30-day window runs into the first week of December. Exchanges will therefore be preparing their responses at roughly the same moment Kalshi is reporting to a federal court on how it is complying with the California tribal order.

The California order the guidance now shadows

On November 4, U.S. District Judge Jacqueline Scott Corley of the Northern District of California granted Blue Lake Rancheria and Chicken Ranch Rancheria of Me-Wuk Indians a preliminary injunction barring Kalshi from offering sports-related event contracts on their lands while the case proceeds. The order followed a unanimous September decision by a Ninth Circuit panel, which found the tribes likely to succeed on their argument that Kalshi's sports contracts, when entered into from tribal lands, are Class III gaming under the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act and likely violate tribal gaming ordinances. Our earlier report sets out the order's compliance deadlines in detail.

Two of those deadlines are mechanical, and both bear directly on geofencing. The tribes were directed to supply maps of their land boundaries by November 13, and Kalshi must file a report on its compliance steps within 21 days of the ruling. In effect, the court is asking for a geofence: a defined boundary around reservation and trust land, enforced through location checks. How "Indian lands" is defined for that purpose is itself a statutory question, covered in our guide to what counts as Indian lands under IGRA.

The court is asking an exchange to draw a line around tribal land. The regulator is telling exchanges that lines drawn by location are presumptively out of bounds. Both positions cannot be fully satisfied unless one of them yields.

Kalshi has consistently argued that its event contracts are federally regulated derivatives subject to the Commodity Exchange Act and CFTC oversight, not gambling subject to state or tribal law. After the Ninth Circuit decision, a company spokesperson told Reuters the ruling was difficult to reconcile with federal law placing on-exchange derivatives under CFTC regulation.

Tribal and congressional reaction

Reaction from California's tribal gaming leadership was immediate. James Siva, chairman of the California Nations Indian Gaming Association, described the guidance as a "vindictive assault against Indian Country," told Tribal Business News that the agency "cannot erase tribal sovereignty," and urged the CFTC to withdraw it. He also called on Congress to close what he described as a gambling loophole, and framed the issue in terms of the federal government's trust obligations to tribes, a legal concept with a long history in Indian law that is outlined in our explainer on the federal trust responsibility.

Sen. Adam Schiff of California issued a statement on November 6 asserting that sports prediction markets are sports bets and accusing the agency of encouraging platforms to keep offering gambling products despite court orders. No legislative response has followed, and with Congress in its short post-election session, any statutory fix would face a compressed calendar.

What to watch before the deadline

Three developments will determine whether the guidance changes anything on the ground for tribes. The first is Kalshi's compliance report to Judge Corley, which should show how the company intends to implement location-based restrictions around the two rancherias, and whether it references the CFTC interpretation in doing so. The second is how exchanges answer the 30-day request. An explanation that a court order makes removing a particular restriction infeasible would be a straightforward response, and the agency has signaled that it will consider case-specific facts.

The third is whether the interpretation surfaces as an argument in other litigation. Tribes in several states have pursued or monitored similar claims, with differing results, as our comparison of diverging California and Wisconsin rulings shows. Operators seeking to narrow existing orders could cite a federal agency position against geographic restrictions, while tribes are likely to argue that an interpretation of a trading-access regulation cannot displace a federal statute governing gaming on Indian lands.

For now, the practical result is a federal court order and a federal regulatory interpretation pointing in different directions on the same mechanism. Tribal regulators in California and elsewhere have reason to follow the docket closely.
