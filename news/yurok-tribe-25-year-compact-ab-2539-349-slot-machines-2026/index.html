Yurok Tribe Wins 25-Year Gaming Compact: 349 Slots, 3 Sites

Replacing a short-term extension, the new agreement gives the Klamath-based tribe a long runway and no Revenue Sharing Trust Fund contributions, pending federal review.

By Sarah BegayPublished October 05, 20265 min read

California Governor Gavin Newsom signed legislation on September 14, 2026 ratifying a new 25-year tribal-state gaming compact with the Yurok Tribe, a development that gives the tribe a long-term framework for Class III gaming on California's far north coast. The measure, Assembly Bill 2539, was authored by Assemblymember James C. Ramos, and it replaces the short-term bridge extension the legislature approved in May with a durable Yurok Tribe gaming compact.

According to the bill's summary as reported by tribal gaming outlets, the compact authorizes up to 349 slot machines at no more than three gaming facilities on eligible Indian lands. The tribe reimburses the state for its regulatory costs and, under the terms reported, is not required to contribute to the Revenue Sharing Trust Fund or the Tribal Nation Grant Fund. The Yurok Tribe currently operates the Redwood Hotel Casino in Klamath, which has 99 Class III machines.

From a bridge extension to a full compact

The new compact follows a pattern that has become familiar in California. Earlier this year the legislature ratified Assembly Bill 1389, a narrow urgency measure that extended the existing compact without changing its substance. We covered that step in our report on the AB 1389 extension. Extensions of that kind keep operations lawful while negotiators finish a successor agreement. AB 2539 is that successor: a complete 25-year agreement rather than a patch.

The distinction matters for planning. A short extension leaves a tribe unable to commit capital with confidence, because the rules could change at the next renewal. A quarter-century term gives the Yurok Tribe's leadership a stable legal horizon for any decision about expanding, renovating or adding a facility, and it gives lenders and partners a clearer picture of the operating environment.

What 349 machines and three facilities mean

The numbers in the compact are modest by California standards, where the largest tribal casinos operate thousands of machines. They are, however, a substantial increase over the current footprint. Moving from 99 machines at one property to an authorized ceiling of 349 across as many as three facilities does not mean the tribe will build to the ceiling. Device caps in compacts are authorizations, and the pace of any build-out depends on financing, land status, market demand and the tribe's own priorities.

The reference to facilities on "eligible Indian lands" is also important. Under the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act, Class III gaming may occur only on Indian lands that meet federal eligibility requirements, so a compact's facility allowance does not by itself resolve which parcels qualify. Readers who want the underlying rules can consult our explainers on the definition of Indian lands under IGRA and on device caps in tribal-state compacts.

The revenue-sharing question

The most closely watched feature of recent California compacts is how they treat payments to the state. In the Yurok compact as reported, the tribe reimburses the state's regulatory costs but does not pay into the two statewide funds that historically moved gaming revenue toward non-gaming tribes and tribes with small operations. That outcome is consistent with the approach discussed in our analysis of the fiscal shift in revenue-sharing-free compacts, and with the broader explainer on the Revenue Sharing Trust Fund.

A tribe operating a small, rural casino has a different fiscal profile than the large urban-edge operators. Its machine count is limited, its customer base is regional, and its gaming revenue often funds essential tribal government services in a remote area. The compact's cost-reimbursement structure ties the tribe's obligation to what the state actually spends on oversight rather than to a percentage of revenue. Whether that approach becomes a model for other small operators is something state lawmakers and tribal negotiators will weigh, but nothing in the reported terms suggests a formal policy change beyond this agreement.

Where it fits in the September package

The Yurok measure was one of several compact bills Governor Newsom signed on September 14. The same day's actions included an amendment for the Agua Caliente Band of Cahuilla Indians, additional machines for the Santa Ynez Band of Chumash Indians and the Pechanga Band, and new or extended agreements for the Fort Mojave Indian Tribe and the Picayune Rancheria of Chukchansi Indians. We have written separately about the Pechanga and Santa Ynez expansions. Together the signings show the state working through a large backlog of compact business before the end of the legislative cycle.

Because California's compacts take effect only after the federal Interior Department publishes notice of approval, the Yurok agreement is not the final step. The Secretary of the Interior has 45 days to act on a submitted compact, and approval by operation of law is possible if the Secretary does not; the mechanics are summarized in our 45-day approval explainer. Only after notice appears in the Federal Register does the compact become effective for Class III operations under its new terms.

For the tribe, the next milestones are federal review and then the internal decisions about whether and where to add facilities. For the state, the compact adds another long-term agreement to a regulatory map that already includes dozens of tribes.
