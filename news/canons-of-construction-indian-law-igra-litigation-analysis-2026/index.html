How Indian Law Canons of Construction Shape IGRA Litigation

Courts read ambiguous federal statutes in tribes' favor, but the canons have limits. In gaming cases, the details decide.

By Sarah BegayPublished November 04, 20264 min read

Statutory interpretation sits behind many of the most consequential tribal gaming rulings. Indian law canons of construction, a set of interpretive rules developed by the Supreme Court over nearly two centuries, instruct judges how to read federal statutes and treaties that affect tribes. In gaming disputes under the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act (IGRA), those canons can tip close questions, though they rarely decide a case alone.

This analysis outlines the main canons, how they have been used in gaming-related decisions, and the limits that tribes, states and regulators should keep in mind.

The core canons

The best-known canon holds that ambiguous statutes passed for the benefit of Indians are to be construed liberally in their favor. The Court articulated a version of this rule in Montana v. Blackfeet Tribe (1985), where it said that statutes are to be construed liberally in favor of the Indians, with ambiguous provisions interpreted to their benefit. A related canon applies to treaties, which are read as the tribes would have understood them and with ambiguities resolved in the tribes' favor.

A second family of rules concerns sovereignty. Courts presume that Congress does not lightly diminish tribal powers, and that any abrogation of tribal sovereign immunity must be unequivocally expressed. The Court restated that clear-statement principle in Santa Clara Pueblo v. Martinez (1978) and again in Michigan v. Bay Mills Indian Community (2014). A third strand favors tribal self-determination in reading federal statutes that touch tribal governance.

Canons in gaming cases

IGRA is a comprehensive statute, and courts often resolve gaming disputes by applying its text directly. Where the text is clear, the canons do not override it. But in several lines of cases the canons have helped frame the analysis. When a court asks whether a state's laws apply on Indian lands, the baseline presumption of tribal sovereignty informs how the court reads the permissible scope of state authority that a compact may confer. In Ysleta del Sur Pueblo v. Texas (2022), the Supreme Court split over how IGRA and the earlier Restoration Act interact, and the dissent and majority each invoked interpretive principles to support different readings. Our Ysleta explainer describes the holding.

The canons also surface in questions about the Interior Department's role. When the Secretary reviews a compact or a land-into-trust request, a reviewing court may ask what deference the agency's reading deserves. The Administrative Procedure Act supplies the review framework, discussed in our APA review explainer, and the Indian law canons can inform how ambiguity in an agency's governing statute is resolved. Courts have not settled how the canons interact with modern doctrines of agency deference, and litigants frequently brief the point on both sides.

Where the canons stop

Three limits recur. First, the canons apply only when a statute is genuinely ambiguous. A court that finds clear text will follow it, even if the result disadvantages a tribe. Second, courts have declined to apply the canon where competing interests of different tribes, or of tribes and individual Indians, are in tension, because favoring one reading favors one Indian party over another. Third, the canons do not create jurisdiction or rights that Congress did not provide. They guide interpretation, not outcomes.

States and commercial operators have also pointed out that IGRA reflects a negotiated balance. Congress set a framework in which tribes, states and the federal government each hold defined roles, and the statute's careful allocation can limit how far a liberal reading of any one provision extends. That argument has featured in disputes over the scope of permitted gaming and the subjects that may be negotiated in a compact. Related questions about good faith negotiation are covered in our good faith negotiation explainer.

Why it matters for the industry

For tribal governments, the canons are a litigation tool and a drafting consideration. Compacts, ordinances and management agreements are interpreted against this background, and clear language remains the best protection. For states, the canons are a reminder that waivers and delegations of authority must be explicit. For investors and lenders, they underline why careful documentation of sovereign immunity waivers and governing law matters.

As new questions arise, including the treatment of online wagering offered from tribal land and the reach of federal regulatory agencies, courts will again have to decide how much interpretive weight the canons carry. Readers who want the statutory foundation can start with the site's Legal Guide and our history of IGRA.

What to watch next

Several open questions keep the canons relevant. One is how courts weigh the canons against the Supreme Court's recent emphasis on text, where some opinions treat the canons as tiebreakers of last resort and others give them more independent force. Another is how they apply when a statute benefits one tribe at the expense of another, a situation that arises in disputes over off-reservation gaming and competing land claims.

A third is the interaction with administrative law. Interior and the National Indian Gaming Commission interpret IGRA through guidance, opinions and rulemaking, and parties challenging those readings often argue that ambiguity should be resolved in tribal favor, while opponents argue that the agency's expertise, not a canon, should control. Practitioners should expect arguments on both sides in any significant gaming case, and should read each decision for how much weight the court actually placed on the canon versus the statutory text.

For readers following the field, the lesson is to treat the canons as one input among several. They matter most at the margins, where the text runs out and the structure and purpose of the statute must fill the gap.
