Seneca Nation Says Compact Framework Has No Revenue Sharing; State Disputes It

A claimed framework, a governor's office that says talks are ongoing, and three host communities that budget around casino money.

By Marcus WhitehorsePublished October 06, 20264 min read

The Seneca Nation of Indians says it has negotiated an agreement in principle with New York State on a new Class III gaming compact, and that the framework contains no revenue sharing. The governor's office says there is no agreement. That gap, reported in the first days of October 2026, now frames the most closely watched tribal gaming negotiation in the Northeast, and it matters well beyond the Nation's three casinos.

The Seneca Nation gaming compact that governed the relationship since 2002 expired in December 2023. Since then the parties have operated under a series of short extensions, and reporting indicates that the payments the Nation would ordinarily make to the state have been held in escrow rather than disbursed. The new development is the Nation's characterization of where talks stand.

What each side is saying

Seneca Nation President J. Conrad Seneca told reporters that the Nation negotiated an agreement in principle that, as he put it, currently contains no revenue sharing provision. He indicated the Nation expects the document to be finalized in the spring and then sent to the legislature, which would need to act before the compact could move to federal review.

A spokesperson for the governor's office responded that state officials have been engaged in productive discussions but that there is currently no agreement, and that talks remain ongoing. The state did not confirm the Nation's description of the terms. Both statements can be true at once: a tribe may regard a negotiated framework as settled while the state treats it as one input into a continuing process. The practical consequence is that nothing is binding until a compact is signed, ratified under state law, and then approved or deemed approved by the Interior Department under the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act.

The Nation has also said it proposed an alternative to state revenue sharing: direct payments to the host communities of Buffalo, Niagara Falls, and Salamanca. According to the Nation's account, the state rejected that approach. We could not independently confirm the state's reasoning, and readers should treat the details of that exchange as the Nation's description of private talks.

Why revenue sharing is the central issue

Under the 2002 compact, the state received 25 percent of the net drop from certain slot machines in exchange for exclusivity in western New York. A portion of that money has historically been distributed to the cities that host Seneca casinos. The dispute that ultimately led to the expiration of the compact traces to the introduction of video lottery terminals at horse tracks after 2004, which the Nation argued violated the exclusivity that justified its payments in the first place. For a broader treatment of the legal logic, see our explainer on revenue sharing in tribal-state compacts.

The local stakes are concrete. Niagara Falls Mayor Robert Restaino has said the city could lose between 10 and 13 million dollars a year if sharing ends, in a city whose total budget is on the order of 100 million dollars, meaning casino revenue supplies roughly a tenth of its spending. In Salamanca, which sits on the Allegany Territory, Mayor Sandra Magiera said casino revenue represents about 30 percent of the city's operating budget and that losing it would likely mean layoffs. The Niagara Falls City School District also receives 750,000 dollars in separate state-mandated funding connected to the arrangement, and Superintendent Stanley Wojton described that support as very significant for the district.

These figures explain why a compact negotiated between two sovereigns becomes a municipal budget story. If sharing ends without a replacement, the cities lose a predictable revenue stream. If the Nation pays host communities directly, the money would flow without passing through Albany, which changes who controls the allocation and the leverage that comes with it. Our earlier reporting on the December deadline for the compact outlines how the extension schedule created these pressure points.

What to watch next

Three things will determine whether the framework holds. First, whether the state publicly confirms any of the terms the Nation has described. Second, whether the legislature, which must ratify a compact under New York's process, signals appetite for an agreement without revenue sharing. Third, whether the escrowed funds are released as part of a settlement, which would give the state and the host cities a near-term payment even if future sharing ends.

The compact also arrives at a symbolic moment. Seneca Resorts and Casinos unveiled a refreshed corporate logo on September 29, built around a single eagle feather, ahead of the operation's 25th anniversary on January 1, 2027. Chief Executive Kevin Nephew said the design connects to Seneca identity and signals an aim to raise the guest experience. The brand update covers the Niagara, Allegany, and Buffalo Creek properties and does not change the compact discussions, but it underscores that the Nation is planning for a long-term horizon.

For readers following the broader question of how states and tribes allocate gaming revenue, the situation illustrates a recurring tension. Revenue sharing is generally permissible under IGRA only when a state offers something of substantial value in return, such as exclusivity, and the parties disagree here about whether that bargain was kept. Whatever the outcome, expect the host cities to remain the most vocal stakeholders until a signed document replaces the competing statements.
