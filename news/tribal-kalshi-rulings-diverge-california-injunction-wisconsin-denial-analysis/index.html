Why Tribal Kalshi Rulings Diverge: California Injunction, Wisconsin Denial

Same statute, different courts, different results: how circuit law, the record and the choice of claim shape tribal prediction-market litigation.

By Daniel YazziePublished November 09, 20264 min read

Tribal plaintiffs challenging Kalshi's sports event contracts have now obtained very different results from different federal courts, and the differences are instructive. In California, a district court has enjoined Kalshi from offering sports contracts on the Indian lands of Blue Lake Rancheria and Chicken Ranch Rancheria. In Wisconsin, the Ho-Chunk Nation's claims are proceeding, but a preliminary injunction was denied. Elsewhere, appellate courts have let states enforce gambling laws against the platform, while other courts have sided with Kalshi on federal preemption. This analysis of the divergent tribal Kalshi rulings looks at why outcomes differ, and what tribal governments should take from them.

The California result: IGRA first, then equities

The Blue Lake case followed a particular sequence. A three-judge Ninth Circuit panel ruled in September 2026 that the tribes were likely to succeed on their Indian Gaming Regulatory Act claim, concluding that Kalshi's sports contracts are Class III gaming when placed on tribal land and that federal commodities law does not override tribal authority there. The panel remanded, and Judge Jacqueline Scott Corley, who had denied the injunction a year earlier, granted it. As we explained in our analysis of the remand, the appellate holding settled the likelihood-of-success factor, leaving harm, equities and the public interest for the district court. The injunction is described as the first of its kind under federal Indian gaming law.

Two features of the California posture favored the tribes. The first is binding circuit authority on the statutory question; once the Ninth Circuit characterized the contracts as Class III gaming, the district court was not free to treat the issue as open. The second is the plaintiffs' status as the owners of the lands at issue, with claims grounded in their own regulatory authority. The Ninth Circuit's reasoning is covered in our report on the Class III ruling.

The Wisconsin contrast

The Ho-Chunk Nation's case is also grounded in IGRA, yet the Wisconsin court denied preliminary relief while allowing the claims to move forward. The reporting does not set out the court's full reasoning, and we avoid speculating about it. What the contrast shows is that a favorable merits outlook does not guarantee interim relief: preliminary injunctions turn on a combination of factors, and courts in different circuits are not bound by the Ninth Circuit's view of the statute. Our coverage of the Ho-Chunk ruling and the Seventh Circuit petition follows that case in detail.

A tribe's claim can be the same in two courts and still produce different outcomes, because binding precedent differs from circuit to circuit.

The state-law track

A separate line of cases concerns whether states may enforce their own gambling laws against Kalshi. According to current reporting, the Ninth Circuit refused to overturn a ruling that allowed Nevada to enforce its gambling laws, and the Sixth Circuit allowed Ohio and Tennessee to enforce theirs. Kalshi has won rulings in Illinois and New Jersey, and New Jersey has asked the U.S. Supreme Court to resolve the conflict. In another proceeding, the Third Circuit has been reported to hold that federal law preempts state regulation of sports-related event contracts, and California tribes and the state's attorney general have filed amicus briefs urging the Supreme Court to hear the question; the NFL also filed a brief in support of review. Our analysis of the Sixth Circuit split and the tribal amicus effort explain where tribes fit in.

The distinction between the two tracks matters. State-law cases ask whether a state's gambling statute can be enforced against a federally registered exchange, which depends on the scope of federal preemption. Tribal IGRA cases ask whether a bet placed from Indian lands is Class III gaming that requires a compact. A tribe can therefore prevail under IGRA even where preemption arguments succeed against states, because the tribe's claim rests on a different federal statute. That logic is the foundation of the Ninth Circuit's holding.

What tribes should take from the divergence

First, forum and circuit matter. Tribes in the Ninth Circuit now have favorable binding authority and a template for relief, while tribes elsewhere face open questions and should expect to build the record themselves. Second, evidence of on-reservation activity matters. In the California case, the tribes alleged that tribal gaming officials could still place bets from tribal land despite Kalshi's voluntary geofencing on three reservations, the type of concrete factual showing that supports both injunctive relief and enforcement. Third, tribes are not uniform. Several have partnered with Kalshi, and the Indian Gaming Association has criticized such arrangements, a split we describe in our analysis of fracturing tribal consensus.

Fourth, the Supreme Court may ultimately supersede the patchwork. If the Court takes a preemption case, its reasoning about the relationship between the Commodity Exchange Act and state or tribal gaming law would frame every pending dispute. Until then, outcomes will keep varying with the court, the record and the relief requested.

Looking ahead

In the near term, the California injunction sets deadlines of November 13 for tribal maps and November 25 for a Kalshi compliance report, and those filings will show how workable on-reservation exclusion is in practice. Tribal leaders also remain wary of federal guidance on geographic trading limits, which could affect how localized orders operate. For a state-level view of the market where the injunction applies, see the California state hub, and for the statutory background see the Legal Guide. We will continue to track how these rulings interact as additional courts weigh in.
