Can States Tax Business at Tribal Casinos? The Bracker Test Explained

States generally cannot tax tribes or tribal gaming revenue. Vendors, tenants and guests are a different matter, and the answer turns on a 1980 Supreme Court balancing test.

By Marcus WhitehorsePublished November 20, 20265 min read

A common assumption about tribal casinos is that state taxes stop at the reservation boundary. For the tribe and its gaming revenue, that is mostly true. Tribal casinos also depend on many outside businesses, including slot vendors that lease machines, restaurant and retail tenants, hotel guests and construction contractors, and for those the rule is less clear. Whether a state can tax them is decided mainly under a Supreme Court framework known as the Bracker balancing test, along with a separate tax provision in the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act. This explainer covers how the pieces fit together and how courts have applied them at casinos.

Starting point: tribes and tribal gaming revenue

Two rules set the baseline. First, under long-standing federal Indian law, a state generally may not tax a tribe, or tribal members, on activity within the tribe's Indian country unless Congress has clearly authorized it. In Oklahoma Tax Commission v. Chickasaw Nation (1995), the Supreme Court held that when the legal incidence of a state tax falls on a tribe or its members inside Indian country, the tax is categorically barred. The Court also said that who bears the legal incidence is usually the deciding question.

Second, IGRA adds a gaming-specific bar. Section 2710(d)(4) says nothing in the act confers on a state the authority to impose any tax, fee, charge or other assessment on a tribe engaged in Class III gaming, except assessments to cover the state's costs of regulating that gaming. That is why compacts cannot simply tax tribal casino revenue. The payments many tribes make to states are instead structured as revenue sharing in exchange for something of value, usually exclusivity. We cover that structure in our explainer on tribal-state compact revenue sharing and in the TribalGaming.com Legal Guide.

Neither rule addresses taxes whose legal incidence falls on non-Indians who do business at a tribal casino. That question is governed by Bracker.

The Bracker balancing test

In White Mountain Apache Tribe v. Bracker (1980), Arizona tried to impose motor carrier and fuel taxes on a non-Indian logging company working under contract with the tribe on its reservation. The Supreme Court held the taxes preempted. It set out a "particularized inquiry" into the federal, tribal and state interests involved, rather than a fixed rule. Courts applying the test generally ask three questions. How extensive is federal regulation of the activity being taxed? Does the tax burden tribal self-government or economic development? Is the state providing services connected to the activity that would justify the tax, or is it simply collecting revenue from value the tribe created?

The outcome depends heavily on the facts. In Bracker, federal regulation of tribal timber was comprehensive and the state provided almost nothing on the reservation roads it sought to tax, so the tribe prevailed. In Cotton Petroleum Corp. v. New Mexico (1989), the Court upheld a state severance tax on a non-Indian oil and gas lessee, even though the tribe imposed its own tax on the same production, because the state provided substantial services and the tax's effect on the tribe was indirect. Earlier, in Washington v. Confederated Tribes of the Colville Indian Reservation (1980), the Court allowed Washington to tax cigarette sales to non-members at tribal smoke shops. It reasoned that a tribe cannot market an exemption from state tax to outsiders when the value being sold was not generated on the reservation.

The question under Bracker is not whether a non-Indian was taxed on tribal land. It is whose interests carry more weight, given the specific activity involved.

How courts have applied it at casinos

Three appellate decisions show the range of outcomes in gaming settings.

Vendor-owned slot machines in Connecticut. In Mashantucket Pequot Tribe v. Town of Ledyard (2013), the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit upheld a municipal personal property tax on slot machines owned by outside vendors and leased to Foxwoods. The court found that IGRA did not preempt a generally applicable property tax on non-Indian vendors, and that the town's interests in raising revenue for services it provided outweighed the indirect burden on the tribe. The case is a reminder that leasing machines instead of buying them can expose a casino to costs that are passed through from the vendor. The Connecticut state hub profiles the Mashantucket Pequot Tribal Nation.

Lessees on tribal land in Florida. In Seminole Tribe of Florida v. Stranburg (2015), the Eleventh Circuit held that Florida's tax on rent paid by non-Indian businesses leasing tribal land was preempted, given the comprehensive federal regulation of Indian land leasing. The same court upheld Florida's utility tax because its legal incidence fell on the utility company rather than the tribe. Both outcomes affect businesses operating at Seminole properties, described on our Seminole Tribe of Florida profile.

Possessory interests in California. In Agua Caliente Band of Cahuilla Indians v. Riverside County (2017), the Ninth Circuit upheld a county possessory interest tax on non-Indian lessees of tribal land in the Palm Springs area. The court concluded that federal leasing regulation did not displace the tax and that the county provided services to the lessees. The Supreme Court declined review.

These decisions apply different tests to different taxes, but they follow a pattern. Taxes on non-Indian property or businesses that the state or locality actually serves tend to survive. Taxes that reach directly into tribal economic activity in heavily federally regulated areas, such as land leasing or the tribe's own gaming operations, are more likely to be preempted.

Why it matters for tribal gaming operators

For tribes, Bracker affects business decisions as well as legal ones. It can influence whether to lease or buy gaming equipment, how ground leases with hotel or retail partners are structured, and whether tax questions are addressed in a compact or an intergovernmental agreement instead of in litigation. Many tribes negotiate tax and service arrangements with local governments directly, as described in our explainer on intergovernmental agreements and host-community aid.

Separately, a patron's winnings and an employee's wages are generally taxed by federal and state income tax rules based on who the taxpayer is, not where the casino is. Our explainer on how tribal casino revenue is taxed covers those rules. This explainer is general information, not legal advice. Outcomes under Bracker depend on specific facts, and tribes and businesses facing a state tax question should consult counsel.
