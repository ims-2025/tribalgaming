Limited Sovereign Immunity Waivers in Tribal Casino Contracts Explained

Tribal governments are immune from suit unless they waive it or Congress abrogates it. Commercial counterparties rely on narrow, carefully drafted waivers.

By Sarah BegayPublished October 08, 20264 min read

Anyone who lends to, sells to or builds for a tribal casino eventually encounters the same threshold question: can the counterparty be sued if the deal goes wrong? The answer begins with sovereign immunity. Federally recognized tribes enjoy immunity from suit that, absent a waiver by the tribe or an abrogation by Congress, extends to their governmental activities and, under Supreme Court precedent, to their commercial activities as well. The practical consequence is that the contract itself must supply the remedy. A limited waiver of sovereign immunity is the tool that makes tribal casino contracts enforceable, and the precision with which it is written often decides what a lender or vendor can actually recover.

This guide explains how limited waivers function in tribal gaming transactions, what the key court decisions say, and where drafting problems tend to arise. It is general information, not legal advice, and parties to any specific agreement should consult counsel experienced in Indian law.

The legal backdrop

The Supreme Court addressed commercial immunity in Kiowa Tribe of Oklahoma v. Manufacturing Technologies (1998), holding that tribal immunity applies to contracts whether they involve governmental or commercial activities and whether they were made on or off a reservation. In C&L Enterprises v. Citizen Band Potawatomi Indian Tribe of Oklahoma (2001), the Court enforced an arbitration clause in a construction contract, finding that the tribe had clearly consented to arbitration and to judicial enforcement of the award. In Michigan v. Bay Mills Indian Community (2014), the Court reaffirmed tribal immunity in the context of an off-reservation casino, a ruling we describe in our Bay Mills explainer. Together, these decisions establish two working rules: immunity is broad, and it is waivable by clear, unequivocal expression.

That second rule drives contract practice. A waiver is not presumed from the fact of a commercial relationship, and ambiguity is generally resolved in favor of the tribe. The more clearly a contract identifies who is waiving, for what claims, in what forum and against which assets, the more likely the waiver is to be honored. Patrons face a different path altogether, as explained in our guide to patron disputes and sovereign immunity.

Who is waiving matters

Many tribal gaming operations are organized as separate entities, often tribal corporations or authorities chartered under tribal law, as described in our explainer on Section 17 corporations and enterprise structure. Whether an entity shares the tribe's immunity can depend on how it was formed, how it is controlled and whether its charter contains a "sue and be sued" clause. Courts have analyzed these questions with different results, so careful counterparties confirm the entity's legal status and its authority to grant a waiver before signing.

Authority is a related concern. A waiver granted by an official without authority under tribal law, or one that was not approved by the proper governing body, may be contested. Lenders typically ask for tribal council resolutions, legal opinions from tribal counsel and evidence that the governing documents were approved in compliance with the tribe's constitution or code. Where a gaming ordinance or management contract requires NIGC or Interior review, as covered in our explainer on IGRA management contracts, the approval status of the contract can also affect enforceability.

Scope, forum and assets

Limited waivers are typically defined along four dimensions. The first is the claims covered, such as breach of the specific contract, rather than all claims. The second is the forum: often arbitration, with judicial enforcement of the award in a designated federal or state court, or the tribal court in some arrangements. The third is the relief available, which may be limited to enforcement of the contract and exclude tort claims or punitive damages. The fourth is the source of recovery, commonly restricted to defined revenues or assets of the gaming enterprise rather than the tribe's general assets or trust land.

That last dimension is central in financing. Trust land generally cannot be mortgaged in the way fee land can, which is why project lenders focus on pledged gaming revenues, equipment, and collateral arrangements discussed in our explainer on lending against trust land. A well-structured limited waiver pairs the right to sue with an identified pool of assets, so that a judgment has something to attach to. Our coverage of debt restructuring shows what happens when that structure is tested.

Drafting issues that recur

Several patterns appear in disputes. Waivers that are broadly worded but cross-referenced to a separate agreement can fail if the referenced agreement is missing or unapproved. Waivers that are silent on whether they survive termination of the contract invite litigation. Dispute-resolution clauses that name a forum that lacks jurisdiction, or a tribal court that has not been given authority to hear the matter, can leave a party without a place to go. And waivers that purport to be unlimited may raise authority questions under tribal law, which is why careful drafters prefer defined limits that tribal governing bodies can approve without controversy.

Vendors face the same issues on a smaller scale. Equipment leases, participation agreements for slot machines and construction contracts are the most common settings, and the precision of the waiver provisions varies. Some vendors rely on tribal-state compact dispute procedures or on licensing frameworks, which are separate from contract remedies. Our overview of vendor licensing covers the regulatory layer that sits alongside contractual protections.

For tribes, limited waivers are not a concession of sovereignty but an exercise of it. A tribe that offers a clear, bounded waiver can access capital and commercial services on better terms, while retaining immunity for everything outside the defined scope. For counterparties, the lesson is that tribal casino contracts are only as enforceable as their waivers are clear. Both sides benefit from treating the waiver as a core term, drafted early and reviewed carefully.
