Tribal Gaming Court Calendar: Appeal Deadlines Open This Fall

Two September rulings are nearing appeal deadlines and a California management-contract challenge awaits a decision just before a casino opens.

By James KingfisherPublished October 21, 20264 min read

Two federal district court rulings issued in September 2026 are now approaching the end of their appeal windows, and a third case, a California challenge to a management contract, is awaiting a decision from the bench. Together they make up the litigation calendar that tribal gaming lawyers are watching most closely this fall. This article lays out where each case stands, how the federal appeal timeline works when the United States is a party, and what an appeal would and would not change. It is a status report and not a prediction: none of the parties has publicly announced a decision on appealing as of this writing.

Coquille Tribe’s Medford casino: ruling of September 4

On September 4, U.S. District Judge Amit Mehta in Washington, D.C., denied a summary judgment motion filed by the Cow Creek Band of Umpqua Tribe of Indians, the Karuk Tribe and the Tolowa Dee-ni’ Nation, and granted cross-motions from the Department of the Interior and the Coquille Indian Tribe. The court held that Interior acted lawfully when it placed the Medford, Oregon, property into trust for gaming. The ruling turned on the “restored lands” exception in the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act: because the Coquille Restoration Act of 1989 allows land to be taken into trust within the tribe’s five-county service area, which includes Jackson County where Medford sits, Interior treated the parcel as restored land and the court agreed. The judge also rejected an argument that Interior’s January 2025 approval, which reversed a 2020 rejection, was arbitrary, pointing to changed circumstances including a binding municipal services agreement with Medford.

The site is about 2.4 acres, roughly 170 miles from the Coquille’s main reservation in Coos Bay, and is planned to host a Class II gaming floor with 650 machines. Our earlier report on the Medford ruling covers the substance of the decision, and our Oregon market deep dive places it in the state’s competitive picture. The decision is a final, appealable order, so the three plaintiff tribes may appeal to the U.S. Court of Appeals for the D.C. Circuit.

Oklahoma’s 2020 compacts: ruling of September 21

On September 21, Judge Timothy Kelly of the same court granted summary judgment to the defendants in a suit brought by the Cherokee, Chickasaw and Choctaw Nations and the Citizen Potawatomi Nation against the 2020 compacts between Governor Kevin Stitt and the Comanche Nation and the Otoe-Missouria Tribe. The court did not reach the question of whether the compacts comply with IGRA. It held instead that the plaintiffs lacked standing, reasoning that both defendant tribes already operated casinos before the compacts, that sports betting and future casino development were too speculative to establish present injury, that lower exclusivity fees alone did not demonstrate competitive harm, and that invalidating the compacts would not clearly remedy the alleged harm. We analyzed the decision in our report on the dismissal, and the background on how those fees work is in our Oklahoma model compact explainer. How the appeal clock works

In ordinary civil cases a notice of appeal must be filed within 30 days of the judgment. When the United States or a federal agency is a party, the Federal Rules of Appellate Procedure extend that period to 60 days. Both the Coquille and Oklahoma cases named federal defendants, so the 60-day period applies. Counting from the dates of the rulings, the window in the Medford case runs to approximately early November, and in the Oklahoma case to approximately the third week of November. Those are our calculations from the reported ruling dates and should be confirmed against the docket entries of final judgment, which can differ from the opinion date.

An appeal does not automatically stop a casino from operating. Unless a court grants a stay or injunction, the existing decision stands. In the Coquille case, gaming began on a limited scale the day after the land went into trust. Reviewing courts examine Interior decisions under the Administrative Procedure Act’s deferential standard, a framework explained in our APA review explainer, which is one reason challengers face a steep climb on appeal.

The case still awaiting a ruling

In California, Judge Kirk E. Sherriff of the Eastern District of California heard argument on October 9 on a preliminary injunction motion and related motions to dismiss in a suit by the Picayune Rancheria of the Chukchansi Indians. The suit, filed June 5, challenges the National Indian Gaming Commission’s approval of the North Fork Rancheria’s 2024 management contract with Station Casinos. The North Fork casino is scheduled to open at midnight on October 28, so the timing of any ruling matters. Background on how such contracts are reviewed appears in our management contracts explainer.

Why the calendar matters

These cases show the three main ways tribal gaming disputes reach federal court: tribes challenging land decisions, tribes challenging other tribes’ compacts, and tribes challenging regulatory approvals. Each raises different standards of review and different remedies. Standing, in particular, has become a recurring gatekeeping issue when one tribe challenges another’s agreements, and the Oklahoma ruling is a current example.
