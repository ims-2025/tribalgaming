Who Polices a Tribal Casino? Criminal Jurisdiction on Indian Lands Explained

Three sovereigns, two general statutes, three gaming-specific federal crimes — and one power the tribe controls outright.

By Linda CardinalPublished August 28, 20265 min read

A patron is caught sliding chips off a neighbor's stack at a blackjack table in a tribal casino. Who arrests him? The answer depends on whether he is Indian, whether the tribe's land sits in a Public Law 280 state, what he did, and how much it was worth. Criminal jurisdiction on Indian lands is the most jurisdictionally layered area of American law, and a casino floor is where the layers meet the public most often.

This explainer walks through the framework: who has authority over what, where the gaming-specific federal statutes fit, and why tribal gaming commissions and surveillance departments occupy a role that has no commercial equivalent.

The three sovereigns and the default rules

Three governments can potentially exercise criminal jurisdiction in Indian country: the tribe, the federal government, and in some circumstances the state. Which one applies turns primarily on the identity of the offender and the victim.

Tribal jurisdiction. Tribes retain inherent criminal jurisdiction over Indians who commit crimes on their lands. That authority is real but bounded. Sentencing is capped by the Indian Civil Rights Act, and the Tribal Law and Order Act of 2010 allowed tribes meeting specified procedural protections — including licensed defense counsel and law-trained judges — to impose enhanced sentences. Critically, the Supreme Court held in Oliphant v. Suquamish Indian Tribe (1978) that tribes do not possess inherent criminal jurisdiction over non-Indians. Congress has since restored limited tribal criminal jurisdiction over non-Indians in narrow categories through the Violence Against Women Act reauthorizations, but those categories do not reach ordinary casino offenses.

The practical consequence is significant for gaming: a tribal police department cannot criminally prosecute a non-Indian patron for theft, fraud or cheating at play. It can detain, investigate, exclude and refer — but the prosecution must come from elsewhere.

Federal jurisdiction. Two general statutes do most of the work. The General Crimes Act (18 U.S.C. § 1152) extends federal enclave law to Indian country for interracial crimes, with an exception for offenses between Indians. The Major Crimes Act (18 U.S.C. § 1153) gives federal courts jurisdiction over an enumerated list of serious offenses — murder, kidnapping, arson, felony assault and others — committed by Indians in Indian country. Federal prosecutions are handled by the United States Attorney for the district, with investigation typically by the FBI.

State jurisdiction. Public Law 280, enacted in 1953, transferred broad criminal jurisdiction over Indian country to a set of mandatory states — California, Minnesota, Nebraska, Oregon, Wisconsin and, later, Alaska — with specific reservation-level exceptions, and permitted other states to assume jurisdiction optionally. In a Public Law 280 state, county sheriffs and state police exercise the criminal authority that would otherwise sit with the federal government. The statute does not, however, confer general civil regulatory authority, a distinction the Supreme Court drew in California v. Cabazon Band of Mission Indians and one that became the doctrinal foundation of modern tribal gaming. Our Public Law 280 explainer covers that division in more detail.

The gaming-specific federal statutes

When Congress passed the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act in 1988, it added three criminal provisions to Title 18 that apply specifically to gaming on Indian lands.

18 U.S.C. § 1166 makes state gambling laws applicable in Indian country as federal law, except for gaming conducted under a tribal ordinance or compact in compliance with IGRA, and vests exclusive jurisdiction over resulting prosecutions in the United States. This is the provision that criminalizes unlicensed gambling operations on Indian lands.

18 U.S.C. § 1167 criminalizes theft from a gaming establishment on Indian lands, with the offense grading turning on the value taken.

18 U.S.C. § 1168 covers theft by officers or employees of a gaming establishment on Indian lands — internal theft, in other words — and carries materially heavier penalties than § 1167.

Together these give federal prosecutors a direct hook for casino crime that does not depend on the offender's or victim's Indian status, which is why they matter so much in practice. A non-Indian patron who steals from a tribal casino has committed a federal offense under § 1167 regardless of the Oliphant limitation on tribal prosecution.

The gaming statutes exist precisely because the general jurisdictional framework left gaps that a casino — a cash business open to the general public on tribal land — would otherwise fall into.

Where the tribal gaming commission fits

The tribal gaming regulatory authority is not a police force, and conflating the two causes real problems. Its jurisdiction is regulatory and civil: licensing, internal controls, exclusion, and enforcement of the tribal gaming ordinance. The National Indian Gaming Commission's authority is likewise civil — notices of violation, civil fine assessments and closure orders under IGRA. Neither can prosecute a crime.

What they do instead is generate the record that criminal authorities use. Mandatory background investigations of key employees and primary management officials under 25 C.F.R. Parts 556 and 558 screen out applicants with disqualifying histories before they reach a sensitive position. Minimum internal control standards govern cash handling, drop and count, and the surveillance coverage requirements that produce the video evidence most casino prosecutions depend on. And Bank Secrecy Act obligations require currency transaction reports and suspicious activity reports filed with FinCEN, which routinely seed federal investigations. How it works on a real floor

Well-run tribal properties resolve the jurisdictional complexity through cross-deputization agreements and written referral protocols negotiated in advance with county and federal counterparts. A cross-deputized tribal officer holds state or county authority alongside tribal authority, which collapses much of the identity-based analysis at the moment of arrest. Where cross-deputization is not available, properties rely on documented mutual aid agreements and a clear internal escalation path: surveillance documents, security detains, the gaming commission is notified, and the referral goes to the appropriate prosecuting authority.

Exclusion remains the tool tribes control outright. A tribe's authority to exclude non-members from its lands is an inherent civil power that survives Oliphant, and for a large share of casino incidents — advantage play, minor disputes, intoxication, trespass — exclusion is both the fastest remedy and the one least likely to founder on a jurisdictional question.
