Connecticut's 25% Slot Revenue Share and the Fight Over Town Aid

The state keeps a quarter of Mohegan and Foxwoods slot receipts, but towns say the municipal share has shrunk for thirty years.

By James KingfisherPublished October 19, 20264 min read

Connecticut's tribal casinos return a quarter of their slot machine receipts to the state, and a long-running argument over how much of that money should reach cities and towns has resurfaced in the 2026 budget debate. The structure behind that Connecticut slot revenue share is among the oldest and most stable in Indian gaming, which is why the dispute over municipal aid says as much about state budgeting as it does about the tribes themselves.

Reporting this spring from CT Mirror put the state's expected slot receipts at about $365 million for the current fiscal year, with roughly $52.5 million of that, about 14 percent, routed to 169 municipalities. Municipal advocates are asking for something closer to a 50-50 division of future casino revenue. The governor's office has responded that any new commitment to towns must fit inside a balanced budget. Neither position changes what the tribes owe; the pressure is entirely about how Hartford spends what it already collects.

How the 25 percent Connecticut slot revenue share works

The arrangement dates to the early 1990s. The Mashantucket Pequot Tribe's 1993 agreement with the state, negotiated under then-Governor Lowell P. Weicker Jr., ended the state's legal objections to video slot machines at Foxwoods. In exchange, the tribe agreed to contribute 25 percent of slot receipts to the state. The Mohegan Tribe accepted the same terms when Mohegan Sun opened in 1996, and the two arrangements have effectively functioned as a single regime ever since.

That 25 percent is a flat percentage of video slot receipts rather than a sliding scale, which makes Connecticut an outlier among the revenue-sharing states. Many compacts use tiered rates that rise with revenue, while others, as the revenue-sharing explainer on this site details, pay little or nothing to the state at all. The Connecticut model concentrates the payment on one product line, slots, and leaves table games, hospitality and other revenue outside the contribution base.

The flat rate also explains why the state's take tracks the tribes' slot performance so closely. When floor revenue rises, so does the state's share; when it softens, Hartford feels it immediately. Both operators are central to that picture, and the Connecticut state hub and the market deep dive lay out how the two properties compete and what each contributes.

The municipal aid question

The disagreement is not about the 25 percent. It concerns what the state does with it. Under a program established alongside the original compact, a portion of slot revenue was pledged to local governments as aid, and the history of that pledge is the heart of the current complaint from town officials.

According to the figures reported by CT Mirror, the original 1993-94 commitment to towns was $88.1 million, about 78 percent of an anticipated $113 million in slot revenue. The pledge rose to $135 million in 2000, was trimmed to $127.4 million in 2001, and fell to $77.5 million in 2003. It recovered to roughly $85 million the following year, and by 2009-10 stood at $61.7 million, about 16 percent of $384 million in casino revenue. The current $52.5 million is therefore a smaller absolute number than in the program's early years, and a smaller share of a larger revenue pool.

The core municipal argument is a simple one: a revenue stream created partly on behalf of host communities and their neighbors has, over three decades, been used to balance the state's general budget instead.

State officials counter that the casino revenue flows into the General Fund like other receipts and competes with education, health care, pension obligations and other claims. Connecticut's budget picture reinforces that argument. The state faces substantial long-term pension debt, uncertainty over federal Medicaid funding, and competing proposals for child care and K-12 education spending. A larger guaranteed municipal share would require offsetting cuts or revenue elsewhere.

What the tribes have said, and what is at stake

The tribes are not the direct opponents in this fight. Both the Mashantucket Pequot and Mohegan tribes have in the past voiced support for increasing the funds that reach Connecticut communities; in 2023 they publicly endorsed legislation aimed at boosting municipal grants. Their position is that the existing 25 percent obligation is settled, and that the allocation of the proceeds is a matter for the legislature.

That distinction matters for tribal-state relations. A change to the municipal formula can be done by statute without reopening the compact, but a change to the tribes' own contribution rate would require negotiation, and both sides have strong incentives to avoid that. The tribes hold exclusivity over casino-style gaming in the state and have also taken on a prominent role in online gaming, a subject covered in the analysis of the five-year record of Connecticut's exclusive iGaming model. Their willingness to discuss the state's overall relationship is tied to the stability of that exclusivity.

For observers of tribal gaming policy, Connecticut offers three useful lessons. First, a flat rate on a single product can be remarkably durable across thirty years of market change. Second, how a state allocates compact revenue is a separate political question from how much the tribes pay, and it is where most budget conflict occurs. Third, the strength of the state's bargaining position rests on the exclusivity it grants, which is why any development that erodes tribal exclusivity, from new competitors to federal preemption fights, has budget consequences well beyond the casino floor.

Whether lawmakers ultimately move toward a larger municipal share will depend on the state's fiscal position over the next budget cycles. What is already clear is that the 25 percent contribution has become one of the best-documented examples of a tribal-state revenue arrangement, and the debate over its use is likely to recur whenever Connecticut's finances tighten.
