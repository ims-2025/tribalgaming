Chukchansi Files Third Federal Suit to Halt North Fork Casino

A fourteen-year dispute reaches the moment of maximum leverage: after the capital is spent, before the doors open.

By Elena RuizPublished September 15, 20265 min read

The North Fork Rancheria of Mono Indians is weeks away from opening a long-delayed casino resort on Highway 99 in Madera County, and a neighboring tribe is asking a federal judge to stop it. The Picayune Rancheria of the Chukchansi Indians has filed a fresh federal complaint against the North Fork casino project, arguing that the Madera site is not lawfully authorized for Class III gaming and that the opening should not proceed. It is the third federal challenge Picayune has mounted against the same project in roughly fourteen years, and it arrives at the point of maximum leverage: after construction capital has been spent, and before the doors open.

The filing does not, on its own, halt anything. A complaint is not an injunction, and North Fork has continued to prepare for an October debut. But the case crystallizes a question that has shadowed the project since 2014 and that has broader consequences for every off-reservation tribal casino now moving through the federal pipeline: what happens to a gaming site's state authorization when the political act that supplied it is later undone?

The state-authorization question at the center of the North Fork casino lawsuit

North Fork's Madera parcel reached gaming eligibility through the two-part determination pathway in Section 20 of the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act — the route that allows newly acquired off-reservation land to be used for gaming when the Secretary of the Interior finds it is in the tribe's best interest and not detrimental to the surrounding community, and when the governor of the state concurs. Both halves were satisfied a decade and a half ago. What followed was not.

California voters rejected ratification of North Fork's tribal-state compact at a 2014 statewide referendum, and the tribe subsequently obtained Class III authority through Secretarial procedures issued by the Interior Department after compact negotiations with the state did not produce an agreement. Parallel state-court litigation continued for years over the governor's concurrence itself. In December 2025, California's Fifth District Court of Appeal held that the concurrence was invalid from the outset, and the California Supreme Court declined further review in April 2026.

Picayune's federal complaint builds directly on that state-court outcome. If the concurrence was void from the beginning, the argument runs, then the Section 20 two-part determination never fully vested, and the parcel never became eligible for gaming — regardless of what Interior has approved since. North Fork's position, consistently maintained, is that its federal authorizations are independently valid, that Secretarial procedures supply the state-side authority IGRA requires, and that a state appellate ruling cannot retroactively strip a federally determined gaming eligibility.

The dispute is less about whether a casino is popular than about which sovereign's paperwork controls when two sets of approvals point in different directions.

Competition, corridors, and the commercial stakes

It would be naive to read the litigation as purely jurisdictional. Picayune operates Chukchansi Gold Resort & Casino near Coarsegold, drawing from the Highway 41 corridor that feeds Yosemite traffic out of the Fresno metropolitan area. The North Fork property sits on Highway 99, the primary north-south artery of the San Joaquin Valley, with materially higher traffic counts and a shorter drive from Fresno's population base. A new resort on that corridor does not merely add capacity to the region; it reorients where valley gaming demand naturally lands.

That commercial reality is why intertribal objections to off-reservation projects have become a structural feature of California's market rather than an occasional irritant. As we examined in our analysis of intertribal opposition to off-reservation casinos, incumbent operators increasingly use the federal administrative record and the courts as the main venue for competitive defense, because IGRA supplies no economic-injury standard on which to object directly. The litigation vocabulary is jurisdictional; the underlying interest is market share.

The financing dimension sharpens the stakes further. North Fork closed a substantial capital package earlier this year to build the Madera resort, detailed in our coverage of the tribe's $725 million Madera financing. Capital of that scale is underwritten against an opening date and a ramp schedule. Litigation that does not stop construction can still raise the cost of everything that follows it, from refinancing terms to vendor contracts to the pace of any second phase.

Why this case matters beyond Madera County

Three features make the dispute worth watching for operators and regulators well outside California.

First, it tests the durability of gubernatorial concurrence as a legal foundation. Concurrence is the single most politically contingent step in the Section 20 process — a discretionary act by an officeholder who will not be in office when the resort is built. If a state court can void a concurrence years after Interior has relied on it, the two-part determination becomes a materially weaker basis for a nine-figure capital commitment. Readers new to the mechanism can review our explainer on the Section 20 two-part determination.

Second, it tests how much weight Secretarial procedures can carry. Procedures exist precisely so that a state's refusal to negotiate cannot indefinitely block Class III gaming a tribe is otherwise entitled to conduct. Whether they can also cure a defect in the state-consent step that preceded them is a different and largely unlitigated question.

Third, it illustrates the timing asymmetry that now defines these fights. Federal review of a fee-to-trust and gaming-eligibility package routinely runs a decade or longer, while a competing tribe's complaint can be filed in an afternoon. The result is that projects clear their hardest regulatory hurdles only to face their sharpest legal exposure at the moment of commissioning.

What to watch next

The immediate procedural questions are whether Picayune seeks preliminary injunctive relief and, if so, how a court weighs the balance of hardships against a tribe preparing to open. Courts have generally been reluctant to enjoin operations at the eve of opening absent a strong showing on the merits, but the state appellate ruling gives Picayune a stronger record than it carried in earlier rounds.

Longer term, the case may push Interior toward more explicit findings about the independence of federal gaming-eligibility determinations from subsequent state-court treatment of concurrence. It may also strengthen the argument, already circulating among tribal finance professionals, that off-reservation projects should be underwritten with litigation contingency assumed as a base case rather than a tail risk. For a broader view of how California's pipeline is absorbing this pressure, see our California state hub.

North Fork has not signaled any change to its opening plans. Picayune has not signaled any intention to stop filing. Both positions are, at this point, entirely consistent with fourteen years of precedent between them.
