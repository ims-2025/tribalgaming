CFTC Sues Louisiana and Arkansas as Prediction Market Fight Reaches the South

Federal regulators are now suing any state that enforces gaming law against registered exchanges. Louisiana's three gaming tribes, and one tribe's own prediction app, sit in the middle.

By Elena RuizPublished November 17, 20265 min read

The Commodity Futures Trading Commission sued Louisiana and Arkansas on November 13, adding two more Southern states to a widening federal campaign to shield prediction markets from state gaming law. The CFTC lawsuits against Louisiana and Arkansas arrive as tribal governments press their own case that sports-event contracts are gambling, and they put the agency in direct conflict with states where tribal and tribally owned casinos are part of the market.

The Louisiana complaint, filed late Friday in the U.S. District Court for the Eastern District of Louisiana, seeks declaratory and injunctive relief against a state court order obtained by Attorney General Liz Murrill. On Thursday, November 12, a Louisiana state judge granted Murrill's request for a temporary restraining order directing prediction markets to stop operating in the state. According to the CFTC, the order shut down the Louisiana operations of more than 30 companies connected to trading in CFTC-regulated derivatives, and those companies received no notice or opportunity to be heard before it issued.

The Arkansas suit targets a civil enforcement action the state is pursuing against Kalshi in state court. The CFTC says Arkansas is trying to halt event-contract trading and to impose civil penalties that could reach hundreds of millions of dollars on federally registered exchanges. Arkansas is the 11th state the agency has sued over the issue and Louisiana the 12th. The commission has also filed amicus briefs in four federal courts of appeals and one state supreme court.

The CFTC's preemption argument

Both complaints rest on the same theory: that the Commodity Exchange Act gives the CFTC exclusive jurisdiction over event contracts listed on designated contract markets, and that the Supremacy Clause preempts state gaming laws as applied to those products. CFTC Chairman Michael S. Selig called Louisiana's action "naked lawfare," and said the federal suit is intended to preserve the status quo while the courts resolve the underlying question.

Murrill's position mirrors the one tribal gaming organizations have advanced for more than a year: that a contract on the outcome of a sporting event is a wager regardless of the exchange structure built around it. "Gambling has been regulated by states for 250 years," she has said. For tribal governments, the parallel claim rests on the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act, which assigns Class III gaming on Indian lands to tribal-state compacts and tribal regulation. Readers new to that framework can start with our Legal Guide to IGRA and Class III gaming.

The new suits follow the CFTC's interpretation earlier this month directing exchanges to end domestic geographic restrictions within 30 days. That step collided with a federal court order requiring Kalshi to keep its sports contracts off the lands of two California tribes, a conflict laid out in our coverage of the CFTC geofencing interpretation. The Louisiana and Arkansas complaints extend the same logic: if federal law occupies the field, neither a state attorney general nor a tribal regulator can force an exchange to wall off a jurisdiction.

Where Louisiana's tribes stand

Louisiana is home to three federally recognized tribes that operate Class III casinos under IGRA compacts: the Chitimacha Tribe, which owns Cypress Bayou Casino Hotel in Charenton; the Coushatta Tribe, owner of Coushatta Casino Resort in Kinder; and the Tunica-Biloxi Tribe, which operates Paragon Casino Resort in Marksville. Their properties compete with riverboat and land-based commercial casinos, racinos and a regulated online sports betting market. Our Louisiana tribal gaming market deep dive maps that competition in detail.

The state court order lands in an unusual place for one of those tribes. In September, the Tunica-Biloxi Tribe announced SaltTrade Derivatives, a tribally owned prediction market brand built on Kalshi's exchange infrastructure, making it the first tribal nation to announce its own prediction market app. That venture, described in our report on SaltTrade, placed a Louisiana tribe on the operator side of an industry most tribal organizations oppose. Public reporting on the restraining order has not specified whether it reaches SaltTrade's plans, and the tribe had not addressed the question publicly as of this writing.

The larger point for Louisiana's tribes is that the state's attorney general is now litigating a version of the argument they have made in other forums: that the location of a wager, not the label on the contract, determines which gaming law applies. A federal ruling for the CFTC would cut against that argument. A ruling for Louisiana would reinforce it, and would give tribal compacts in the state a firmer footing against unlicensed sports wagering.

Arkansas and tribal operators outside Indian lands

Arkansas has no IGRA casinos, but tribal enterprises are part of its commercial market. The Quapaw Nation's Saracen Casino Resort in Pine Bluff operates under a state license issued under Amendment 100, the 2018 constitutional amendment that authorized casino gaming in four counties. The Cherokee Nation's bid for the Pope County license ended after voters approved Amendment 104 in 2024, which revoked the license that had been awarded to Cherokee Nation Entertainment.

For a state-licensed tribal operator such as Saracen, the stakes resemble those facing any commercial casino. Prediction markets offering sports contracts compete for the same customers without holding an Arkansas casino license, paying the state's gaming taxes or following its responsible-gaming rules. The tribe's interest is less about IGRA jurisdiction than about whether a state licensing regime can be enforced at all against federally registered exchanges.

Congress and the tribal-law argument

The suits also arrive amid pressure from Capitol Hill. Nine Democratic senators wrote to the CFTC urging it not to interfere with state prohibitions on prediction markets, arguing that the agency's decision on geographic restrictions "undermines state and tribal law, and must be reversed" and conflicts with active court orders. Democrats will control both chambers of Congress next year, a shift tribal advocates have pointed to as an opening for legislation clarifying that sports-event contracts remain subject to state and tribal gaming law.

Public opinion is also being enlisted. A survey of 2,268 registered voters released this week by the American Gaming Association found that 82 percent believe prediction markets should be regulated as gambling rather than as financial products, and that 74 percent favor limiting trading to adults 21 and older.

What to watch

Three developments will shape the next phase. The first is how quickly the federal court in New Orleans acts on the CFTC's request to block the Louisiana order, and whether the state court's restraining order survives in the meantime. The second is the pending petition asking the U.S. Supreme Court to resolve the split among federal appeals courts over whether sports-event contracts are subject to state gaming law; three circuits have sided with states or tribes and one with Kalshi. The third is the expiration of the CFTC's 30-day window on geographic restrictions, which will test whether exchanges comply with the agency or with existing court orders protecting tribal lands.

For tribal governments, the immediate lesson of the Louisiana and Arkansas filings is that the federal agency is prepared to sue any jurisdiction that tries to enforce gaming law against registered exchanges. Whether that posture ultimately reaches tribal regulators directly is a question the courts have not yet been asked to answer.
