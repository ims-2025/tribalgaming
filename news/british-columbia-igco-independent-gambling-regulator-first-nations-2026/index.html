B.C.'s New Independent Gambling Regulator and First Nations Gaming

An arm's-length regulator takes over B.C. gaming oversight — and the province's First Nations have a stake in how it writes the rules.

By Linda CardinalPublished August 08, 20264 min read

British Columbia has handed oversight of its gambling industry to a new, independent watchdog — a structural change that carries real consequences for the province's First Nations, who share in gaming revenue and increasingly operate casinos of their own. The Independent Gambling Control Office (IGCO) took over regulation of all gaming in the province this spring, replacing the enforcement arm that previously sat inside government and promising what officials call a standards-based model for both land-based and online play.

The reform, enacted through a new Gaming Control Act, arrives after years of scrutiny over how the province policed its casinos. Investigations culminating in the Cullen Commission's inquiry into money laundering exposed serious gaps in oversight, and the rapid growth of online gambling created regulatory blind spots that the old structure struggled to close. The IGCO is designed to answer those criticisms by operating at arm's length from government, with authority to set standards and enforce them without waiting on political direction.

What the IGCO actually regulates

The new office's remit is broad. It covers the British Columbia Lottery Corporation's physical casinos and its digital platform, charitable gaming, and horse racing — effectively every legal form of wagering in the province. That single-regulator approach mirrors a wider trend in Canadian gambling, where provinces are professionalizing oversight as online markets mature. For First Nations, the question is less about the machinery than about the money and the relationship: how the regulator treats Indigenous-owned venues and how consistently it applies its standards.

British Columbia's First Nations receive a defined share of provincial gaming income through a long-term revenue-sharing arrangement, a framework that channels hundreds of millions of dollars to communities over the life of the agreement. That structure sits alongside a growing wave of First Nations ownership in the province's gaming sector, including the recent casino acquisitions that have put Indigenous groups in control of commercial properties. A regulator that changes how compliance is measured touches all of it.

The shift to an independent regulator is as much about credibility as control. After the money-laundering scandals, the province is signaling that oversight will no longer bend to political convenience.

How B.C. fits the national picture

The B.C. reform lands amid a broader reordering of Canadian gaming, where each province is charting its own course on revenue sharing and online expansion. Ontario, whose regulated internet market is the country's largest, saw its payments to the Ontario First Nations Limited Partnership climb sharply last year. Alberta, which opened a competitive online market this summer, carved out a percentage of gross gaming revenue for First Nations from the outset. Our comparison of Canadian First Nations gaming revenue frameworks lays out how those models differ province by province.

Those differences matter because they shape incentives. A revenue share pegged to provincial gaming income rises and falls with the province's own operations; a fixed percentage of a competitive online market grows as that market grows. British Columbia's independent regulator does not itself rewrite the province's revenue-sharing agreement, but a more rigorous compliance regime can affect operating costs, licensing timelines, and the pace at which new Indigenous-owned venues come online — all of which feed the revenue that ultimately reaches communities.

What to watch

The early test for the IGCO will be whether independence translates into clearer, faster, and more consistent decisions. Operators of every kind — Crown, commercial, and Indigenous — will be watching how the office handles licensing, anti-money-laundering compliance, and the supervision of online play. First Nations leaders have voiced a recurring concern across Canada that expanded online gambling can erode the foot traffic that sustains land-based casinos; a regulator with a firm hand on the digital market is, from that vantage, a mixed blessing.

There is also a governance subtext that resonates well beyond British Columbia. Placing a regulator at arm's length from the government it once answered to is a deliberate statement that oversight should not be vulnerable to the political incentives that shape a province's own gaming revenue. Because British Columbia's Crown corporation both runs casinos and depends on their proceeds, an independent supervisor removes an obvious conflict — the referee no longer reports to a party with money on the game. For Indigenous operators weighing new investments, a predictable and credible regulator can be as valuable as a favourable revenue formula, since financing and expansion decisions hinge on regulatory certainty.

For now, the practical effect is continuity with a new name and a new posture. Games continue, revenue continues to flow, and the province's First Nations remain both beneficiaries of the gaming economy and, increasingly, owners within it. The larger significance is institutional: British Columbia has decided that the credibility of its gambling oversight is worth insulating from politics. Whether that decision strengthens the position of Indigenous operators or simply adds a layer of scrutiny will become clearer as the IGCO writes its first rules.
