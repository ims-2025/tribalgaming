The Quiet Retreat From Revenue Sharing in Tribal Compacts

From Rincon to Fort Mojave, the meaningful-concession test is rewriting what states can demand for a Class III compact.

By James KingfisherPublished July 25, 20264 min read

When California Governor Gavin Newsom signed a 25-year compact with the Fort Mojave Indian Tribe in July 2026, the most notable feature was what it left out: a requirement to share casino revenue with the state's general fund. Instead of broad revenue-sharing payments, the tribe agreed mainly to reimburse California for regulatory costs, with a small contribution to a local impact fund if it operates more than 350 gaming devices. The deal is part of a broader retreat from general-fund revenue sharing in new tribal-state compacts — a shift with deep roots in federal law and significant consequences for how states and tribes divide the spoils of gaming.

For two decades, revenue sharing was treated as the price of exclusivity. That bargain is now unraveling, and the reason traces back to a single influential court decision.

What IGRA actually allows states to demand

The Indian Gaming Regulatory Act permits states to negotiate compacts covering Class III gaming, but it deliberately limits what states may extract. States cannot simply tax tribal gaming; IGRA bars the imposition of state taxes, fees or assessments on tribes as a condition of a compact. Revenue sharing survived that prohibition only through a narrow rationale: a state may negotiate for a share of gaming revenue if it offers the tribe a "meaningful concession" of real economic value in return — most commonly, a guarantee of exclusivity that protects the tribe from commercial competition.

The logic is that the tribe is paying not a tax but a fair price for something valuable. When the concession is genuine, revenue sharing can be lawful. When it is not, it looks like an illegal demand for state revenue dressed up as a negotiated term.

The Rincon decision and its long shadow

That distinction was sharpened by the Ninth Circuit's 2010 ruling in Rincon Band of Luiseno Mission Indians v. Schwarzenegger. The court held that California had negotiated in bad faith by demanding revenue-sharing payments into the state general fund without offering a sufficient new concession in return. General-fund revenue sharing, the court reasoned, functioned as a tax that IGRA forbids. The decision did not outlaw revenue sharing, but it drew a bright line: states must give tribes something of real value, and payments that flow to general state coffers without such a concession cross into impermissible taxation.

Revenue sharing was never meant to be a state entitlement. Under IGRA it is a price a tribe pays for a benefit — and when the benefit shrinks, so does the state's leverage to demand payment.

In the years since, tribes have negotiated from a stronger position. As exclusivity has eroded in some markets and as courts have policed the meaningful-concession requirement, states have found it harder to justify large general-fund cuts. The Fort Mojave compact — regulatory-cost reimbursement plus a targeted local mitigation contribution, but no general-fund revenue share — reflects exactly this recalibration. Winners, losers and the money in the middle

The retreat from revenue sharing is a clear win for tribal sovereignty and for tribal balance sheets, keeping more gaming revenue in tribal governments where IGRA intends it to fund services. But it complicates the politics of gaming expansion. States have historically used revenue-sharing dollars to justify exclusivity to legislators and to fund programs, including backstops for non-gaming tribes. In California, general-fund and special-fund payments have helped support the mechanisms that redistribute gaming wealth to tribes without casinos — a system explained in our overview of the California Revenue Sharing Trust Fund.

The dynamic is not confined to California. Every state that authorizes Class III tribal gaming negotiates within the same IGRA guardrails, and the meaningful-concession principle travels with them. In states where commercial competition is expanding — through sports betting, historical horse racing or new casino licenses — the exclusivity that once justified revenue sharing becomes harder to guarantee, and tribes are increasingly unwilling to keep paying full price for a benefit the state is diluting. In states where tribes still enjoy genuine exclusivity, by contrast, revenue sharing remains easier to defend because the concession is real.

That makes revenue sharing a barometer of tribal bargaining power. Where tribes can credibly walk away, invoke bad-faith negotiation, or point to eroding exclusivity, the payments shrink; where the state can still offer something valuable and unique, they persist. The Fort Mojave compact reads as a case of the former — a long-term deal that trades regulatory reimbursement and targeted local mitigation for the state's cooperation, without treating the tribe's revenue as a source of general funds.

As new compacts trend away from general-fund contributions, states will have to decide whether exclusivity alone is a sufficient trade, and tribes will keep pressing the legal principle that they cannot be taxed for the privilege of exercising their own sovereignty. The direction is set: the era in which revenue sharing was treated as an automatic feature of tribal gaming is ending, and Rincon's meaningful-concession test is the reason why. What replaces it — narrower payments, targeted local mitigation, or exclusivity with no cash attached — will define the next generation of tribal-state deals.
