The Wire Act, UIGEA and Tribal Online Gaming: How Federal Law Applies

Three federal statutes from three decades explain why tribal online wagering has stayed inside state lines.

By Daniel YazziePublished November 12, 20265 min read

Tribal online gaming sits at the intersection of three federal statutes written for different purposes in different decades. The Indian Gaming Regulatory Act of 1988 governs gaming on Indian lands. The Wire Act of 1961 targets the use of wire communications for certain interstate betting. The Unlawful Internet Gambling Enforcement Act of 2006, known as UIGEA, regulates the payments that fund internet wagers. Understanding how the Wire Act, UIGEA and IGRA fit together explains why tribal online sports betting and iGaming have developed as they have: mostly within a single state, and usually through compacts or state laws rather than any federal authorization of online play.

The Wire Act: a sports betting statute

The Wire Act, codified at 18 U.S.C. § 1084, makes it a crime for a person engaged in the business of betting or wagering to knowingly use a wire communication facility to transmit, in interstate or foreign commerce, bets or wagers, or information assisting in the placing of bets or wagers, on any sporting event or contest. For years the central question was whether that language reached only sports betting or also casino-style games and lotteries.

The Justice Department has taken both positions. A 2011 opinion from its Office of Legal Counsel concluded that the Wire Act applied only to sports betting, which helped open the door to state-authorized online casino and lottery sales. A 2018 opinion reversed course and read the statute more broadly. The New Hampshire Lottery Commission challenged that reversal, and in 2021 the U.S. Court of Appeals for the First Circuit held that the Wire Act applies only to bets or wagers on sporting events or contests. The government did not seek Supreme Court review. The practical result is that the Wire Act is widely treated as a sports betting statute, although the First Circuit's ruling is binding only within that circuit.

For tribes, the statute's significance lies in its interstate element. A sports wager placed and accepted within a single state does not involve the interstate transmission the law is aimed at, which is one reason tribal mobile sports betting has been built around geolocation that keeps bettors inside state lines.

UIGEA: the payments law with a tribal carve-out

UIGEA, codified at 31 U.S.C. §§ 5361-5367, does not itself make any form of gambling illegal. It prohibits businesses engaged in betting from knowingly accepting payments in connection with "unlawful internet gambling," a term defined by reference to bets that are unlawful under applicable federal, state or tribal law where the bet is initiated, received or otherwise made. Banks and payment processors must maintain policies to identify and block restricted transactions.

The statute contains two exceptions that matter for tribal operators. An intrastate exception covers bets initiated and received exclusively within a single state when state law expressly authorizes them and includes age and location verification and data security requirements. An intratribal exception covers bets initiated and received exclusively within the Indian lands of a single tribe, or between the Indian lands of two or more tribes as authorized by federal law, provided the wagering is conducted in accordance with IGRA and the applicable tribal ordinance and, for Class III gaming, the tribal-state compact, with age and location verification in place. UIGEA also provides that the intermediate routing of electronic data does not determine where a bet is initiated or received.

Just as important, UIGEA states that none of its provisions should be construed as altering, limiting or extending any federal or state law or tribal-state compact that prohibits, permits or regulates gambling. In other words, UIGEA polices payments; it does not decide what is legal. That question is answered by other laws, including the tribal ordinances and compacts described in our explainer on Class II and Class III gaming.

IGRA and the Indian lands question online

IGRA authorizes and regulates gaming conducted on Indian lands. It was enacted before consumer internet gambling existed and does not expressly address wagers placed by a customer located off Indian lands. That gap is the central legal question in tribal online gaming: when a bettor is at home miles from a reservation and the server sits on tribal land, where does the gaming occur? The statutory definition of Indian lands, discussed in our explainer on what counts as Indian lands under IGRA, does much of the work.

The most consequential answer so far came from the Seminole Tribe of Florida's 2021 compact, which deems wagers placed anywhere in Florida to be placed at the tribe's facilities where the servers are located. In West Flagler Associates v. Haaland, decided in 2023, the U.S. Court of Appeals for the D.C. Circuit upheld the Interior Department's approval of that compact. The court reasoned that IGRA does not bar a compact from addressing activity off Indian lands, while the lawfulness of that off-reservation activity is a matter of state law, which Florida's Legislature had enacted. This hub-and-spoke approach is examined in our hub-and-spoke explainer and our Seminole compact analysis.

The three statutes divide the work: IGRA governs gaming on Indian lands, the Wire Act polices interstate sports wagers, and UIGEA follows the money while deferring to other laws on what is lawful.

What it means in practice

Taken together, the three statutes produce a recognizable pattern. Tribal online gaming today is overwhelmingly intrastate. It is conducted either under a compact that, together with state legislation, treats statewide wagers as occurring on tribal servers, as in Florida, or under state online gaming laws in which tribes participate as licensees or exclusive operators, as in Michigan, Connecticut and Maine. Geolocation, age verification and data security are built into these systems because they are conditions of UIGEA's exceptions as well as requirements of state and tribal regulation. Interstate tribal online gaming, such as pooling players across state lines, would raise Wire Act questions for sports wagers and would need careful structuring under UIGEA's exception for wagers between tribes' Indian lands "as authorized by Federal law."

None of this is static. Justice Department interpretations have changed before, and courts continue to test the boundaries of IGRA's Indian lands concept. Tribal operators considering online products typically weigh all three laws alongside their compacts and ordinances.
