Kalshi Ruling Explained: What a Ninth Circuit Preliminary Injunction Means

The appeals court found tribes likely to succeed on their IGRA claims. That is a significant step, but it is not the end of the case.

By James KingfisherPublished October 03, 20264 min read

The Ninth Circuit's September 16, 2026 decision in Blue Lake Rancheria v. Kalshi has been widely discussed as a major win for tribal gaming. The ruling reversed a lower court's denial of a preliminary injunction and found that the tribes were likely to succeed on their claims under the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act. The result is important. But a preliminary injunction ruling has a specific legal meaning, and understanding it helps separate what the decision settles from what remains open.

What the court decided

According to published reporting on the decision, the Ninth Circuit determined that the tribes were likely to succeed on their IGRA claims and reversed the denial of preliminary relief. Reporting also describes the panel's reasoning as treating prediction market contracts as gambling that occurs on Indian lands when accessed from tribal territory, and therefore as subject to the tribal gaming framework Congress established. We have covered the substance of the decision in our report on the Ninth Circuit Blue Lake Rancheria ruling; this analysis focuses on the procedural posture.

What a preliminary injunction is

A preliminary injunction is temporary court-ordered relief issued while a case is still being litigated. Federal courts generally evaluate four factors, drawn from the Supreme Court's decision in Winter v. Natural Resources Defense Council: whether the party seeking relief is likely to succeed on the merits, whether it is likely to suffer irreparable harm without relief, whether the balance of equities favors it, and whether an injunction is in the public interest. The first factor is the one most often described in headlines, but all four must be addressed.

The key phrase in the Ninth Circuit's ruling, "likely to succeed," is a prediction, not a final determination. Courts reach it on an expedited record and without the full development of evidence that a trial or summary judgment would provide. A party that wins at this stage may still lose at final judgment, and the court can revisit its conclusions as the record grows.

A ruling that tribes are likely to prevail is a strong signal about how the circuit views the legal question. It is not a final judgment on the merits.

Why the stage still matters

Even so, appellate rulings at this stage carry weight. A published opinion from a federal court of appeals states the law for the circuit, and district courts within it are expected to follow the reasoning when similar questions arise. For tribes, the ruling provides a favorable articulation of how IGRA applies to wagering accessed from Indian lands, which is the central question in much of the litigation described in our mid-2026 litigation scorecard.

The decision also shapes the settlement and strategy calculus. Defendants that face a circuit-level signal against them have different incentives than those who have won in lower courts. Other tribes considering suits can point to the reasoning when deciding whether to file, a dynamic relevant to the cases covered in our reports on New Mexico tribes' IGRA suit and on the Ho-Chunk Nation ruling in Wisconsin.

What remains open

Several questions remain. First, further proceedings in the lower court will determine the scope and terms of any relief, and the case will continue toward a final resolution. Second, the losing party may seek further review, including rehearing by the full court; our article on the en banc petition addresses that avenue. Third, courts in other circuits have not all reached the same conclusions, and our analysis of the circuit split explains why a disagreement among circuits can eventually draw the attention of the Supreme Court.

There is also the broader regulatory context. Prediction market operators operate under federal commodities oversight, and the interplay between that regime and IGRA is a distinct legal question from the one the Ninth Circuit addressed at this stage. Federal agencies and Congress remain active in the area, as reflected in our coverage of the CFTC rule debate.

What tribes and operators can reasonably expect

In practical terms, the most likely near-term effect is on litigation posture rather than on the day-to-day market. Tribes with pending claims gain a persuasive precedent within the Ninth Circuit, and tribal associations gain support for requests to Congress and federal agencies. Prediction market operators, for their part, may seek further review, adjust their offerings in affected areas, or press their own legal theories in other forums. Which of those paths proves decisive will depend on how the district court applies the ruling and how other circuits continue to rule. Observers should expect a measured, multi-year process rather than a single decisive event.

How to read the headlines

Tribal leaders and associations have used the ruling to reinforce arguments for legislative and regulatory action, and coverage on October 1 noted that tribal nations were stepping up their fight on several fronts. That is a reasonable response to a favorable ruling. Readers should still distinguish three layers: what the court has held, what it has predicted, and what remains to be decided. The ruling holds that the tribes met the standard for a favorable preliminary determination on a specific record. It predicts the likely outcome. It does not resolve every question about prediction markets and tribal gaming nationwide.
