Tribal Lottery Systems in Washington: How Class III Terminals Work

Washington compacts use their own term for electronic gaming. Here is where the rules for those terminals actually live.

By Sarah BegayPublished October 16, 20264 min read

In most states, the electronic gaming machines found in tribal casinos are called slot machines. In Washington, the compacts that govern Class III gaming use a different term: the tribal lottery system, usually shortened to TLS. For visitors, investors and researchers, the phrase can be confusing. This explainer describes what a tribal lottery system is in the Washington context, how it fits within the federal framework for Class III gaming, and where its rules are set.

The legal frame: IGRA and the compact

The Indian Gaming Regulatory Act divides gaming into three classes. Class III, the category that includes casino-style games, may be conducted on Indian lands only under a tribal ordinance, in a state that permits such gaming for any purpose, and in conformity with a compact between the tribe and the state. Our explainer on Class II versus Class III gaming gives the full breakdown.

Washington's compacts are negotiated between each tribe and the Washington State Gambling Commission, then forwarded through the Governor and submitted to the Secretary of the Interior. After review, or after the 45-day period runs without action, the compact takes effect once notice is published in the Federal Register. The compact, not federal statute, defines the specific games and systems a tribe may operate. In Washington, the electronic player terminals authorized by compacts are referred to as tribal lottery system terminals, and the compact appendices set the operating rules for them.

What the compacts specify

Recent filings show the kinds of rules compacts contain for these terminals. In the Puyallup Tribe's proposed seventh amendment, announced as a tentative agreement in July 2025, the parties addressed wagers of $30 on tribal lottery system player terminals and an option to add 1,000 player terminals to the tribe's allocation through a stepped approach. Those two items illustrate the principal categories of terminal rules: how much a player may wager, and how many terminals a tribe may operate. Our explainer on device caps describes the second category in more detail.

Other provisions in the same proposal indicate the wider set of subjects: licensing and background provisions for people and vendors, community support commitments, a non-smoking room requirement if smoking is allowed, and responsible gaming rules covering training, self-exclusion, signage and self-imposed limits. These sit alongside the terminal rules because the compact is the instrument through which the state and the tribe agree on how Class III gaming is conducted.

Appendices as the working layer

Washington compacts keep much operating detail in appendices. That is why small changes can be filed without reopening a whole agreement. In October 2026, for example, the Stillaguamish Tribe and the Washington State Gambling Commission used a memorandum of incorporation to revise Appendix E of the compact. The Federal Register notice, published October 14, described the revision as increasing the purchase price of a single scratch ticket and setting online wager limits at $50, and it stated that the compact took effect that day after the Secretary took no action within 45 days. Our report on that filing provides the details.

For readers new to the topic, the practical takeaway is that the rules for a particular tribe's terminals are tribe-specific. Two Washington casinos may have different allocations, wager limits and conditions, because each tribe has its own compact and its own history of amendments. Comparing them means reading each compact and its amendments, not relying on a statewide template. The most-favored-nation concept, discussed in our explainer on most-favored-nation clauses, is one way tribes sometimes gain access to terms another tribe has negotiated.

Regulation and oversight

Day-to-day regulation of a tribal casino is carried out first by the tribe's own gaming regulatory authority, with the state's role defined by the compact, and with the National Indian Gaming Commission responsible for federal oversight. If you want to see how those layers fit together, read our guide to who regulates tribal casinos and the explainer on the tribal gaming regulatory authority.

The compact's responsible gaming terms also matter to players. Self-exclusion programs, for example, are discussed in our piece on self-exclusion in tribal casinos. And because Class III games differ from Class II bingo-based machines in legal basis, the distinction explains why some states with no Class III compact still have electronic gaming; see how Class II gaming machines work.

Where to find the rules

The authoritative sources for any given tribe's terminal rules are the compact and its amendments, which are published by the Washington State Gambling Commission, and the Federal Register notices announcing approval. Tribal casino directories, including our Washington directory, can point you to the operator and tribe, and the Legal Guide covers the underlying law. The terminology may be unfamiliar, but the structure is consistent: a federal framework, a negotiated compact, and appendices that carry the operating detail.
