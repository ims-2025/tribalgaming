Tribal Gaming Revenue Concentration: Why 9% of Casinos Earn 56%

A record year for the industry and a lopsided distribution of that record are the same fact seen from different distances, and the policy debates now under way depend on both.

By James KingfisherPublished October 17, 20264 min read

Headline numbers for tribal gaming revenue concentration tell two stories at once. Industry reporting puts tribal gaming's gross revenue at a record $46.2 billion in 2025, a figure that speaks to the sector's scale and its place in the American economy. The same reporting notes that about 56 percent of that revenue came from the top 9 percent of facilities. Both facts are true, and together they explain why debates over new revenue models, from mobile wagering to prediction-market partnerships, land so differently across Indian country.

This analysis looks at what concentration means in practice, why it persists, and what it implies for tribes with small or remote operations. It does not argue that concentration is a failure of the industry. It is largely a function of geography and law, and understanding it is a prerequisite for evaluating almost every policy proposal now under discussion.

Why a few facilities earn so much

Casino revenue follows population. A property within a short drive of a large metropolitan area competes for millions of potential customers, while one in a sparsely populated region draws on a fraction of that. This is the dynamic explored in our explainer on why location decides tribal gaming revenue, and it helps explain the performance gap documented in our urban-rural analysis.

The Indian Gaming Regulatory Act reinforces the pattern. Gaming must take place on Indian lands, and a tribe's lands are determined by history, treaties, and federal land-into-trust decisions rather than by market opportunity. A nation whose reservation sits far from population centers cannot simply relocate. Some tribes have no gaming facility at all, a gap we examined in our report on tribes without gaming and the IGRA access gap. The statute's off-reservation provisions exist but are narrow and slow, with governor concurrence and federal determinations as hurdles.

What concentration does to the policy debate

When revenue is concentrated, proposals are judged by who stands to gain. Sports betting under a compact, for example, tends to favor tribes with large existing customer bases, because a mobile or on-property sportsbook builds on loyalty programs and foot traffic. Our analysis of the sportsbook as a retention engine makes that point from the operator's side.

Prediction-market partnerships look different through the same lens. Tribal leaders who have signed them describe the revenue as a way to reach beyond a limited geography. Opponents, including the Indian Gaming Association, argue the legal risk is borne by exactly the smaller tribes least able to absorb it. Both positions respond to the same underlying fact: a tribe with a marginal casino has fewer options than one with a destination resort. Our coverage of the loss scenarios for tribal gaming from prediction markets models how the downside would fall unevenly.

Reading the numbers carefully

Two cautions apply. First, "facilities" and "tribes" are different units. A single nation may operate several properties, and the top 9 percent of facilities may be owned by a smaller share of tribes. Concentration by facility therefore does not translate directly into concentration by nation or by member. Second, gross revenue is not the same as what reaches members and governments. Operating expenses, debt service, regulatory costs, and reinvestment come first, and the use of net revenue is governed by IGRA's permitted purposes and each tribe's revenue allocation plan. For the mechanics, see our explainer on the five permitted uses of net gaming revenue.

Smaller operations also deliver value that revenue totals understate. A modest casino in a rural community may be the largest private employer in the county and a primary funder of tribal health, education and water services. The national figure is a poor guide to the importance of any single property to its community.

Implications

For policymakers, concentration argues for caution about proposals that assume a uniform tribal interest. For tribes, it underscores the value of diversification: non-gaming enterprises, hospitality investments, and shared-services arrangements can stabilize smaller economies. For regulators, it suggests that compliance burdens, including licensing and vendor requirements, weigh more heavily on operations with thin margins; our piece on vendor licensing burdens explores that. The broader numbers are collected in our by-the-numbers explainer, and the tribal gaming directory lets readers see the range of operations across states.

A record year for the industry and a skewed distribution of that record are not in tension. They are the same fact viewed from different distances, and any serious discussion of the sector's future has to hold both.

One further consideration is time. Concentration is not static: new casinos, expansions, and compact changes shift the distribution gradually, and a property that is marginal today can become a regional draw if a nearby market grows or a competitor closes. Longitudinal data, rather than a single-year snapshot, would give a clearer view of whether the gap is widening or narrowing, and readers should look for that kind of data as more industry reports are released.
