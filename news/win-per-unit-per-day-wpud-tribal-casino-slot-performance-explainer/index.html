Win Per Unit Per Day (WPUD): Reading Tribal Casino Slot Performance

The most common slot yardstick is simple to calculate and easy to misread; here is how operators, vendors and lenders use it.

By James KingfisherPublished October 30, 20264 min read

Win per unit per day, usually abbreviated WPUD, is one of the most widely used yardsticks for slot performance in casinos, and it appears regularly in tribal gaming coverage, vendor presentations and lender discussions. For readers trying to interpret claims about a floor "outperforming" or "underperforming," understanding how WPUD is calculated, and where it can mislead, is essential. This explainer sets out what the metric measures, how tribal operators and regulators use it, and why it should never be read in isolation.

How WPUD is calculated

The formula is simple. Take the net win from slot machines over a period, divide it by the number of machines in operation, and divide again by the number of days in the period. If a casino records a given amount of slot win across a month, spread over a known number of machines, the result is the average daily win each machine produced. The same calculation can be applied to table games, where the unit is a table rather than a machine, and sometimes to a whole floor.

The result is an average, which hides variation. A handful of popular machines can generate many times the win of the weakest ones, so a floor-wide WPUD says little about how any individual game performed. Operators therefore often compute WPUD by denomination, by manufacturer, by game theme and by location on the floor. For the underlying definitions of handle, coin-in and hold, see our explainer on casino handle, coin-in and hold.

What WPUD is used for

Inside a tribal gaming enterprise, WPUD is a management tool. It helps decide which machines to keep, move or replace, and it feeds the case for capital spending on new games. Our piece on slot floor mix and game merchandising describes how operators use performance data to balance titles and denominations.

Outside the enterprise, WPUD shows up in vendor negotiations. Manufacturers often present average performance for their titles, and operators comparing leased machines with purchased ones use the metric to judge whether a participation fee is justified. Our analysis of slot participation versus purchase explains why the economics of leased games depend heavily on the win they produce. Lenders and investors also look at it when evaluating expansions, since a new floor is typically underwritten on an assumed WPUD for the added machines.

WPUD answers the question of how hard a machine works, not whether the casino as a whole is profitable.

Why comparisons can mislead

The most common error is to compare WPUD across properties without adjusting for context. Several factors distort the comparison. Floor size matters, because a casino with fewer machines in a strong market may show a higher figure than a larger casino with excess capacity. Market maturity and competition matter as well. Promotional policy matters, because free play affects reported win: an operator that reinvests heavily in promotional credits may report a lower net win figure than one that does not, even if the underlying play is similar. Our explainer on how comps and theoretical loss work covers that accounting.

Game classification can also matter. In states where tribes operate Class II electronic bingo machines rather than Class III slot machines, performance may differ, and revenue definitions may too. The differences between the two categories are described in our Class II versus Class III explainer. Counting rules also vary: whether a machine out of service for a week counts for the full period, for example, changes the denominator.

Public data and its limits

Unlike some commercial jurisdictions that publish detailed monthly reports by property, most tribal gaming data is reported only in aggregate. The National Indian Gaming Commission publishes annual revenue totals drawn from tribal submissions, but it does not publish WPUD for individual casinos. Individual tribal enterprises generally treat operating metrics as confidential, a pattern we discuss in our analysis of the tribal gaming financial disclosure gap. As a result, WPUD figures that appear in public discussion usually come from state-reported jurisdictions, trade surveys or company presentations, and their definitions are not always consistent.

That limited visibility is one reason published tribal data should be read carefully. A figure labeled WPUD may reflect net win, gross win or an average that excludes new machines; reading the footnotes matters. Where a state regulator reports tribal-casino slot data, as some states do for revenue-sharing purposes, the numbers are more comparable but still reflect that state's definitions.

How to read the number responsibly

A sound approach is to treat WPUD as one input among several. Pair it with occupancy of the floor, hotel and food performance, labor cost and capital reinvestment, and look at trends within a single property rather than comparisons across properties. A rising WPUD after an expansion can signal healthy demand, but if the expansion added machines faster than demand grew, WPUD may fall even while total revenue increases. That is not necessarily a failure: it can reflect a deliberate decision to capture market share.

For tribal governments, whose casino revenue funds services, the more consequential question is usually total net revenue and its allocation, not the per-machine average.
