Tribal Gaming by the Numbers: 250 Tribes, 545 Facilities, 29 States

What the headline statistics mean, how IGRA organizes the industry, and why a small share of facilities generates most revenue.

By Daniel YazziePublished October 15, 20264 min read

Tribal gaming is one of the largest segments of the American casino industry, yet its scale and geography are often poorly understood. Recent national reporting, drawing on National Indian Gaming Commission (NIGC) data, puts the numbers plainly: roughly 250 tribal governments operate about 545 gaming facilities in 29 states, and tribal gaming generated record gross gaming revenue of $46.2 billion in 2025. This explainer walks through what those figures mean, how the industry is organized under federal law, and why a handful of regions and operators account for so much of the total.

The legal framework in brief

Modern tribal gaming rests on the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act (IGRA), which Congress passed in 1988 after the Supreme Court's 1987 decision in California v. Cabazon Band of Mission Indians confirmed that tribes could conduct gaming on their lands free of state regulation where the state permitted similar activity. Our explainers on Cabazon and the history of IGRA trace that background.

IGRA divides gaming into three classes. Class I covers traditional ceremonial and social games and is regulated solely by tribes. Class II covers bingo and similar games, including electronic bingo-style machines, and is regulated by tribes under NIGC oversight. Class III covers casino-style games such as slot machines, blackjack, roulette and, increasingly, sports betting, and generally requires a tribal-state compact. The distinctions are described in full in our guide to Class II versus Class III gaming.

Regulation is layered. Tribal gaming commissions license and inspect operations day to day, the NIGC oversees compliance with federal law and minimum standards, and states participate through compacts for Class III games. See who regulates tribal casinos for the details.

Reading the numbers

The 29-state figure is a reminder that tribal gaming is not confined to a few well-known destinations. Facilities exist from the Pacific Northwest to Florida and from Alaska to the Northeast, though intensity varies widely. Our state hubs for California, Oklahoma, Florida, Connecticut, Michigan, Arizona, Washington and Minnesota provide property-level listings for several of the most active markets, and the full directory covers more.

The $46.2 billion figure refers to gross gaming revenue, meaning wagers minus winnings paid to players, before operating expenses. It is not profit, and it is not the amount available to tribal governments. Net revenue, after expenses, is what tribes must allocate to the uses IGRA permits. For a precise definition, see our explainer on gross gaming revenue. The NIGC's annual numbers, discussed in our coverage of the fiscal 2025 report, are compiled from audited statements submitted by tribes.

Another figure from recent reporting is that about 56 percent of tribal gaming revenue came from the top 9 percent of facilities. In other words, a relatively small number of large properties generate most of the revenue, while the majority of facilities are smaller and often serve local or regional customers. Concentration reflects market access more than anything else. Our explainer on why location decides tribal gaming revenue notes that properties near large population centers or along major travel corridors consistently outperform remote ones.

Why geography shapes outcomes

Two features of the legal system amplify the role of location. First, IGRA generally limits gaming to Indian lands, and land acquired after 1988 is subject to restrictions, so a tribe's reservation or trust land largely determines its market. Second, Class III gaming depends on compacts negotiated with state governments, which set the scope of permitted games, any revenue sharing and exclusivity protections. States that are more permissive, or that have large populations, can support larger tribal enterprises, while others may constrain tribes to Class II gaming or limited game offerings. Our overview of regional divergence between mature and emerging markets examines how this plays out.

Compacts also affect how much revenue tribes share with states. Some states receive substantial payments in return for exclusivity, while others receive little or none. These arrangements are explained in our guide to revenue sharing in tribal-state compacts.

Where the money goes

Because tribal governments, not private shareholders, own and benefit from the enterprises, gaming revenue supports services that other governments fund through taxes. IGRA limits net revenue to specified purposes: tribal government operations and programs, the general welfare of the tribe and its members, tribal economic development, charitable donations and support for local government agencies. Per capita payments to members are allowed only under an approved revenue allocation plan. Details are in our explainers on permitted uses and per capita payments.

Canada compared

Readers may wonder how this compares with Canada. First Nations gaming there operates under provincial authority within the Criminal Code framework rather than through a federal statute that recognizes tribal regulatory power, producing models such as the Saskatchewan Indian Gaming Authority, covered in our SIGA coverage. The differences are summarized in our comparison of US and Canadian legal models.

A note on data

Statistics about tribal gaming can vary by source and year, depending on whether they cover gross revenue, net revenue or a particular fiscal period, and on how facilities are counted. The figures cited here come from recent national reporting and NIGC data; readers who need exact numbers should consult the NIGC's published reports and the Legal Guide for definitions. Understanding the scale and structure of the industry is a prerequisite for evaluating nearly every policy debate that follows, from compact negotiations to federal action on emerging forms of wagering.
