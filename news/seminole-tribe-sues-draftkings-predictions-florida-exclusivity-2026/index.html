Seminole Tribe DraftKings Lawsuit Targets Predictions Product in Florida

The tribe says a federally licensed prediction market is a sportsbook by another name, and it is asking a Florida state court to say so.

By Marcus WhitehorsePublished October 13, 20265 min read

The Seminole Tribe of Florida filed suit on Oct. 8 against DraftKings, Inc. and its chief executive, Jason Robins, in Florida's 17th Judicial Circuit Court in Fort Lauderdale. The Seminole Tribe DraftKings lawsuit asks the court to declare that DraftKings Predictions, the company's prediction-market product, is an illegal sports-betting operation under Florida law and a violation of the tribe's exclusive gaming compact. It is the most direct challenge yet to a commercial sportsbook operator by a tribe that holds statewide online sports-betting exclusivity.

The filing arrives in a month when the prediction-market dispute is moving on several fronts at once. Tribal organizations are pressing the question in federal appeals courts and before the Commodity Futures Trading Commission, and Florida's governor has said publicly that the question needs a fast federal answer. What distinguishes this case is the forum and the defendant: a state trial court, and a company that is itself a licensed sportsbook in many states.

What the complaint alleges

According to reporting on the filing, the tribe contends that DraftKings is running an unlicensed sports-betting business in Florida. The complaint leans on public statements by Robins acknowledging that Predictions is nearly identical to the company's traditional sportsbook, and it argues that the product operates "under the guise" of a federally regulated prediction market. The tribe seeks a declaratory judgment, an injunction barring the platform from operating in Florida, restitution and disgorgement of profits, nominal damages, and attorneys' and court costs.

The complaint also reaches back into the state's political history. It asserts that DraftKings spent many millions of dollars on unsuccessful efforts to legalize commercial sports betting in Florida, and alleges that the company withholds certain sports offerings in states where it holds a sports-betting license because of concern about upsetting regulators there. Those are the tribe's allegations; DraftKings has not conceded them.

DraftKings has responded that its prediction-market offering operates under the federal framework of the Commodity Exchange Act and that it holds a CFTC license for the product. A company spokesperson said DraftKings has deep respect for the tribe's sovereignty and remains confident in its legal position. That exchange frames the core question: whether federal commodities law displaces state gaming law, and the compacts built on it, when a contract is tied to the outcome of a sporting event.

The compact behind the claim

The tribe's theory of injury rests on the structure of its 2021 compact. That year, Gov. Ron DeSantis and the Seminoles amended the Class III compact under the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act, adding house-banked table games and exclusive rights to online sports betting. The tribe's sportsbook is Hard Rock Bet, and the compact uses a hub-and-spoke design in which the servers that accept wagers sit on Seminole land. That model survived legal challenge, with the state and the tribe prevailing in defending it. Our explainer on the hub-and-spoke model walks through why the location of the server matters.

In exchange for exclusivity, the tribe agreed to a guaranteed minimum payment to the state of $2.5 billion over the compact's first five years. That bargain is the reason exclusivity is more than a marketing advantage in Florida. The tribe paid for it, and the state is a counterparty with its own fiscal interest in whether unlicensed competitors can operate. For background on how those promises are written and enforced, see our piece on how tribal gaming exclusivity works and the broader Seminole compact analysis.

The complaint's emphasis on Florida law, rather than only on federal preemption, is notable. A state court can decide what counts as sports betting under the state's own statutes and the compact, and the tribe is betting that a product marketed as a financial contract still falls inside that definition. Prior reporting and our own coverage of state-court challenges to tribal exclusivity show why venue has become a strategic variable in these disputes.

Why the governor's comment matters

On Oct. 8, DeSantis said that federal courts and lawmakers need to resolve quickly whether prediction markets can offer trading on sports events. The statement does not take a side on the merits of the lawsuit, but it signals that the state's chief executive sees the issue as unsettled and consequential. Because the state is a party to the compact and receives revenue under it, the governor's position will be watched closely as the case develops.

The wider legal backdrop remains divided. Federal appeals courts have not spoken with one voice on whether federal commodities law displaces state gambling law for sports-event contracts, and a coalition of 145 tribes and tribal organizations has asked the U.S. Supreme Court to take up the question in Flaherty v. KalshiEX. Our coverage of the amicus effort explains the conflict between circuits. A Florida state-court ruling would not settle the federal question, but it could shape how other states and tribes frame their own claims.

What to watch

The first procedural questions are likely to be about forum and timing. DraftKings may seek to move the case to federal court or argue that federal law forecloses the state-law claims, and the court will have to decide whether the tribe can proceed on its compact and state-law theories. Any request for interim relief would raise the stakes quickly, since the football season is under way and the revenue at issue is concentrated in the fall and winter months.

For other tribes with exclusivity, the case is a test of whether enforcement can run through state law and state courts rather than depending only on federal litigation.
