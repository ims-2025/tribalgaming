San Manuel Campaign Puts a $26.5 Billion Figure on Tribal Gaming

A single-tribe public-awareness campaign is putting new numbers behind an old argument: tribal gaming underwrites a large share of California's economy.

By Elena RuizPublished September 28, 20264 min read

The Yuhaaviatam of San Manuel Nation launched a statewide public-awareness campaign this month called "Committed to California," and in doing so became, by its own description, the first tribe to run a solo, statewide messaging initiative of this kind rather than working through an industry association. The campaign's headline numbers are large: $26.5 billion in economic output, 151,210 jobs supported statewide, $10.2 billion in annual wages and worker compensation, and $5.1 billion in tax revenue attributed to tribal government gaming's footprint across California for 2026.

Why One Tribe Is Running a Statewide Campaign

California already has, by a wide margin, the largest tribal gaming economy in the country, and the industry has never been shy about publicizing its economic weight. What's new here is the format: rather than a joint association effort or a one-time economic-impact study, San Manuel — operator of the Yaamava' Resort & Casino near San Bernardino — is running outdoor advertising, digital placements, and print media under its own name, tying the campaign explicitly back to California's Proposition 5, the 1998 ballot measure that first authorized tribal gaming in the state. Chairwoman Lynn Valbuena framed the effort as filling an awareness gap rather than making a new argument.

"Many people know that tribal communities have grown stronger," Valbuena said, "but fewer realize how deeply tribal gaming contributes to the economic success of our state."

The timing is not incidental. California tribes have spent much of 2026 playing defense on multiple fronts at once: litigation over cardroom blackjack-style games that tribes argue erode their constitutional gaming exclusivity, a decision by the state's gaming tribes to defer another sports-betting ballot fight until 2028 after the costly failure of earlier measures, and ongoing scrutiny of unregulated sweepstakes-style gaming platforms operating in a legal gray area. A campaign that puts a concrete dollar figure on tribal gaming's public benefit gives tribes a ready answer — measured in jobs and tax revenue rather than legal argument — the next time any of those fights reaches the legislature, the ballot, or a courtroom.

San Manuel is also one of the few tribes in the state with the scale to fund a campaign like this on its own. Yaamava' Resort & Casino has grown into one of the largest gaming operations in California, and the tribe has increasingly used that scale to fund initiatives well beyond gaming itself, from regional infrastructure to education partnerships. Running a solo statewide media campaign — rather than pooling resources with other gaming tribes through an association — is a natural extension of that same capacity, and it lets San Manuel control the message and timing in a way a joint campaign with dozens of other sovereign nations, each with its own priorities, typically cannot.

How the New Numbers Compare

Economic-impact figures like these are typically built using input-output modeling that traces not just direct casino employment and spending, but the indirect and induced spending that ripples outward — a casino's vendors, the households its employees support, the tax revenue generated at each step. That methodology tends to produce larger headline totals than a narrower measure of gross gaming revenue alone would, and it's why campaigns like this one often report a figure well above what any single tribe's win totals would suggest on their own. The $26.5 billion figure sits meaningfully above earlier statewide estimates in the $20 billion range, reflecting both real growth in the underlying industry and the natural tendency of updated economic-impact studies to capture a wider circle of indirect activity than the studies that preceded them.

That gap is worth reading with some caution rather than treating as a simple measure of year-over-year growth. Two economic-impact studies commissioned at different times, by different sponsors, using different modeling assumptions, will rarely produce numbers that are directly comparable on an apples-to-apples basis — a wider multiplier, a broader definition of "supported" jobs, or a different treatment of construction spending can each move the headline total by billions of dollars without reflecting any underlying change in the industry itself. None of that makes the $26.5 billion figure wrong; it simply means the number is best understood as this campaign's specific framing of tribal gaming's footprint, not a precise, directly comparable successor to earlier estimates.

A Model Other Tribes May Follow

Whether other California tribes — or tribes in other states — follow San Manuel's lead with their own individual campaigns is worth watching. Collective advocacy through statewide and national associations remains the industry's default, in part because it distributes cost and keeps messaging consistent across dozens of sovereign nations with different individual interests. A single tribe funding its own statewide campaign is a different model: faster to launch, easier to tailor to one nation's specific political priorities, but also one that puts a single tribe's name and resources behind a message meant to benefit the entire industry. Given the size of San Manuel's operation and the stakes California tribes face heading into the next round of cardroom and sports-betting fights, "Committed to California" may prove to be a template other well-resourced gaming tribes borrow rather than a one-off.
