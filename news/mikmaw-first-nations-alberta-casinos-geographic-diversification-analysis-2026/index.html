Why Mi'kmaw Nations Bought Alberta Casinos: A Diversification Analysis

Five Nova Scotia communities went west to buy commercial casinos, testing whether direct ownership beats revenue sharing.

By Linda CardinalPublished September 30, 20264 min read

When five Mi'kmaw communities from Nova Scotia set out to buy casinos, they did not look to the Maritimes. They looked more than 3,000 kilometres west, to Alberta. That decision, and the way it has been followed by a further acquisition in 2026, makes Indigenous Gaming Partners one of the clearest examples of geographic diversification in First Nations gaming in Canada. Rather than expanding within a home market of small gaming operations, the partnership bought commercial casinos in a different province, spreading risk and opening a new source of community revenue.

This analysis looks at what the structure reveals about First Nations gaming strategy: why geography matters, how ownership differs from earlier revenue-sharing arrangements, and what questions the model still has to answer.

How the partnership was built

Indigenous Gaming Partners, known as IGP, was formed by five Mi'kmaw communities: Millbrook, Paqtnkek, We'koqoma'q, Glooscap, and Annapolis Valley. In December 2024, the group's acquisition of Pure Canadian Gaming was reported as its first major entry into commercial casino gaming, covering four casinos in Edmonton, Calgary, and Lethbridge. Michael Peters, IGP's chair and a Glooscap First Nation member, described the logic in terms of both industry and place, saying the communities were trying not only to expand by diversifying into new industries but also to diversify geographically.

The acquisition matters because of where these communities started. Before the purchase, their gaming activity was limited to smaller initiatives such as video lottery terminals. Moving from that base to ownership of a portfolio of commercial casinos represents a significant change in scale and in operating responsibility. The partnership has since grown further: according to Canadian Gaming Business, IGP purchased Gamehost Inc. in late March 2026, and its holdings now include seven Alberta properties, among them PURE Casino Edmonton, PURE Casino Calgary, and Rivers Casino Fort McMurray. A separate news report at the time of the deals characterized the Mi'kmaw nations as set to own about a third of Alberta's gaming market, a framing that reflects the reporting rather than an independently verified market share. For further detail on the operators involved, see our profile of Indigenous-owned casino operators PURE and Gamehost and our report on the Gamehost closing.

Why geography is the strategy

Geographic diversification does several things at once. It reduces exposure to a single provincial regulatory regime, since a change in one province's rules will not affect properties in another. It provides access to larger urban markets than most Maritime communities can reach. And it allows communities without a local casino market of their own to participate in gaming revenue at all.

The alternative models illustrate the contrast. Some First Nations gaming in Canada operates through provincial frameworks in which communities receive a share of revenue from casinos they do not own. In Saskatchewan, the Saskatchewan Indian Gaming Authority operates First Nations casinos and returns revenue through a defined structure, a model we examined in our coverage of the SIGA expansion. In contrast, the IGP model is direct ownership of commercial assets, purchased in the open market and operated for the benefit of the owning communities. Our analysis of revenue sharing versus ownership in First Nations gaming explores that distinction in depth.

Ownership changes the conversation, as one industry executive put it in commentary on recent British Columbia casino sales.

That comment, from Ian Simpson of Petroglyph Development Group, was made in the context of the Snuneymuxw First Nation's purchase of five British Columbia casinos, but it captures a theme running through the sector. Great Canadian Entertainment sold eight of its nine British Columbia casinos to Indigenous groups, retaining only Chances Dawson Creek, according to the same reporting. Across British Columbia and Alberta, control of large commercial casinos has been moving into First Nations hands.

Early returns and open questions

Early financial evidence is limited but encouraging. Canadian Gaming Business reported that Glooscap First Nation generated more than $600,000 in six months from the initial PURE acquisitions. That is a small figure in the context of a multi-property operator, but for a single community it represents a new stream of own-source revenue, and it arrived within the first year of ownership. The purchasing communities cite economic self-determination, reduced reliance on federal funding, and the reaffirmation of sovereignty as their motivations.

Several questions remain. Acquisition financing carries obligations, and the terms have not been detailed in the public reporting cited here, so it is not possible to assess how quickly the debt burden will be repaid or how distributions to the five communities will change over time. Performance will also depend on the Alberta market, including competition, regulatory changes, and the province's approach to online gaming. Readers following the provincial policy environment can review our overview of the Canadian iGaming map beyond Ontario and Alberta.

There is also a governance question. A partnership of five communities must make decisions collectively, and the interests of each may not always align. The model's durability will depend on how well the partnership manages reinvestment, distribution, and expansion over time.

What is already clear is that the strategy has widened what First Nations gaming in Canada can look like. It is no longer limited to on-reserve casinos or provincial revenue shares. It now includes national-scale commercial ownership, built by communities that started with far smaller operations.
