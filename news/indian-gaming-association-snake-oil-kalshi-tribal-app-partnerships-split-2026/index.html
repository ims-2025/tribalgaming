IGA Calls Kalshi Tribal App Deals 'Snake Oil' as Indian Country Splits

Four more tribes have signed on, and the Indian Gaming Association says smaller nations are being sold the wrong product. The disagreement turns on how IGRA applies to event contracts.

By Marcus WhitehorsePublished October 17, 20264 min read

The arrival of tribal-branded prediction market apps has opened a visible rift in Indian country. In early October 2026, Kalshi announced partnerships with four more tribes, following a similar September agreement with the Tunica-Biloxi Tribe of Louisiana, and the Indian Gaming Association responded with sharp criticism. IGA Executive Director Jason Giles said Kalshi is "selling them snake oil," arguing that the company is targeting smaller tribes with limited options.

The disagreement is more than rhetorical. It rests on two different readings of what these products are, who regulates them, and whether a tribe that lends its name to one is strengthening or undermining the framework that made tribal gaming possible.

What the new partnerships look like

The four tribes announced in early October are the Kletsel Dehe Wintun Nation, Greenville Rancheria and Alturas Indian Rancheria in California, and Alabama-Quassarte Tribal Town in Oklahoma. Under the structure described publicly, each tribe owns its app and runs the customer-facing business, while Kalshi operates the exchange and handles trade execution and clearing. Of the four, only Alturas Indian Rancheria operates a casino. We covered the initial details in our report on the four-tribe Kalshi app announcements.

The tribes involved describe the deals in terms of economic development. A representative of the Kletsel Dehe Wintun Nation said the arrangement could help fund water infrastructure and framed it as sovereignty and self-determination. Alabama-Quassarte Chief Wilson Yargee said geography has kept many tribes from sharing in the prosperity gaming created. Kalshi's chief executive said prediction markets and tribal economic development do not have to be at odds.

The case against

The Indian Gaming Association and other tribal gaming organizations argue that sports event contracts are, in substance, unauthorized sports wagering. Under the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act, Class III gaming such as sports betting is conducted under a tribal-state compact, and many tribes have negotiated exclusivity and revenue-sharing terms around it. From that perspective, a federally regulated exchange offering sports contracts nationwide sidesteps the compact structure that tribes spent decades building. The Washington Indian Gaming Association has also criticized the company's ownership economics, and tribal representatives have criticized the Commodity Futures Trading Commission's approach as bypassing established tribal gaming regulation.

The argument about smaller tribes is the sharpest. Giles's concern is that tribes with few gaming options may be offered an apparent opportunity while the larger legal question remains unresolved. Reporting on the dispute notes that sports-related contracts make up more than 80 percent of prediction market trading volume industry-wide, which is why tribal opponents view the product as sports betting in all but name.

The case for, and the courts

Kalshi describes its products as event contracts and swaps overseen by the CFTC rather than gambling. Tribes that partner with it make a different point: gaming income has been unevenly distributed. Industry reporting cites record tribal gaming gross revenue of $46.2 billion in 2025, with about 56 percent of it coming from the top 9 percent of facilities. Smaller and more remote nations see an alternative revenue path where a conventional casino is not feasible. Our analysis of how the tribal consensus is fracturing examines that tension in more detail.

The courts have not settled it. Two California tribes recently won a federal appeals ruling involving Kalshi and Robinhood over sports contracts offered on tribal land, which we examined in our coverage of the Ninth Circuit Blue Lake Rancheria ruling. In Wisconsin, by contrast, a federal judge declined to shut Kalshi down on Ho-Chunk Nation land, finding the tribe had shown little evidence that its casinos were losing customers. Results therefore differ by circuit and by the evidence presented.

Why it matters for the industry

The split changes the politics of the issue. Until recently, opposition to prediction markets was presented as a near-unified tribal position. A tribe that signs a partnership complicates that message in litigation, in Congress and with state regulators, even if the tribe's own legal position is carefully worded. The next developments to watch are whether more tribes sign, whether courts distinguish between tribes that partner and those that sue, and whether Congress or the CFTC clarifies jurisdiction. Readers who want the statutory background can consult the Legal Guide, and our three-paths overview maps likely outcomes.

Both camps claim to be defending tribal sovereignty. The disagreement is over whether sovereignty is best exercised by protecting the compact framework or by entering a new market on tribal terms. That question will not be resolved by one announcement, and it deserves careful, evenhanded reporting as it develops.

For tribal leaders weighing similar offers, the practical questions are familiar from any gaming venture: who holds the licenses, who bears legal and regulatory risk, how revenue is shared, and what happens if a court rules against the product. Counsel with experience in IGRA and federal commodities law will likely be central to any decision, and the answers may differ by tribe, state and federal circuit. That variability is itself a reason the industry's public debate is likely to continue well beyond this week's announcements.
