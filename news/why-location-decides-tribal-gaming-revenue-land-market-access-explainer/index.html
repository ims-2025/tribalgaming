Why Location Decides Tribal Gaming Revenue: A Plain-Language Explainer

Two tribes with the same legal rights can earn very different amounts. The difference usually starts with a map.

By James KingfisherPublished October 13, 20265 min read

Federal law gives eligible tribes the same basic right to conduct gaming on their own lands. In practice, the revenue that right produces varies enormously, and the single most reliable predictor is location. This explainer lays out why tribal gaming revenue follows geography so closely, what the law allows tribes to do about it, and why the question has become central to debates about newer forms of gaming.

The National Indian Gaming Commission reported record gaming revenue for fiscal year 2025, a figure we summarize in our report on the $46.2 billion total. That national figure is an aggregate. Underneath it, a relatively small number of large operators near major population centers account for a disproportionate share, a pattern we analyze in our piece on mega-operator concentration.

The legal starting point: Indian lands

The Indian Gaming Regulatory Act of 1988 permits tribes to conduct gaming on "Indian lands," and that phrase does a great deal of work. Generally, it covers reservation land and land held in trust for a tribe, subject to exceptions for lands acquired after 1988. A tribe cannot open a casino wherever it chooses; it must have eligible land, and the land has to be in a place where customers can reach it. Our explainers on the definition of Indian lands and on land-into-trust and gaming eligibility detail the rules.

History matters here. Many reservations were established in remote locations, and some tribes were restored to federal recognition with small land bases or none at all. A tribe whose land lies far from a metropolitan area can have every legal right to operate a casino and still face a customer base that is too small to support a large facility. That is the core of what is sometimes called the land-poor problem.

How location shapes revenue

Three variables dominate. The first is the size of the population within a reasonable drive, because most tribal casinos draw heavily from a regional customer base. The second is competition, including from other tribes, commercial casinos and, increasingly, online gaming. The third is the legal environment, meaning what games a tribe is permitted to offer under its compact or under Class II rules.

The distinction between game classes is central. Class II gaming, which includes bingo and certain electronic games that resemble it, does not require a tribal-state compact. Class III gaming, including slot machines and house-banked table games in most settings, does. Tribes that can negotiate a compact gain access to a broader product set, while tribes in states that will not agree to terms may be limited to Class II. Our Class II versus Class III explainer covers the difference, and the operation-of-law explainer describes one federal mechanism when negotiations stall.

Compacts can also set limits on the number of machines a facility may operate and on where tribes can operate, and they sometimes grant exclusivity in a defined territory in exchange for payments to the state. See our explainers on device caps and exclusivity. These terms are negotiated, and tribes with more leverage, often those near large markets, tend to secure more favorable ones.

Can a tribe change its location?

Sometimes, but the process is demanding. A tribe seeking to game on newly acquired land generally needs the land taken into trust and a determination that the land is eligible. Off-reservation proposals can require a two-part determination that includes consultation and, in many cases, the concurrence of the state's governor. The governor concurrence explainer and our overview of the federal approval pathway describe the steps. Local and intertribal opposition can add time, as our coverage of intertribal opposition shows.

Because of these hurdles, many tribes look for other ways to diversify. Some invest in hospitality, real estate or other enterprises, a trend we track in the non-gaming revenue analysis. Others form partnerships, including management arrangements with experienced operators, subject to the oversight rules in IGRA's management-contract provisions.

The practical lesson for readers comparing tribes is to look past the legal right and ask three questions: how many people live within a reasonable drive, what games the compact or Class II rules allow, and what competition the property faces. Two operators with identical legal authority can reach very different outcomes once those answers are in hand, which is why simple rankings by revenue say more about geography than about management quality.

Why this matters for digital gaming

Location also helps explain the current disagreement over digital products. Online and mobile gaming can reach customers beyond a casino's drive-time radius, which is attractive to tribes whose land limits physical revenue. Some small nations have cited exactly that reasoning in deciding to partner with prediction-market operators, as in the recent announcements we covered in our report on four more Kalshi partnerships. Larger gaming nations, whose physical casinos and compact exclusivity already generate substantial revenue, often view the same products as a threat to the bargains they negotiated. Both positions follow from where each tribe sits on the map.
