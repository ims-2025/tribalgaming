Wyoming's Felony Sweepstakes Ban Bill and What It Means for Wind River

Wyoming's tribes do not need new authority from the Legislature. They need state law to keep drawing a clear line around where casino-style gaming can occur.

By Sarah BegayPublished November 17, 20265 min read

Wyoming lawmakers will take up in their 2027 general session a proposal to make operating online sweepstakes casinos a felony, a measure that would put the state among a growing number moving to shut down a product tribal gaming leaders have long described as unlicensed gambling. The Wyoming sweepstakes ban bill, sponsored without dissent by the Legislature's Select Committee on Gaming in October, is aimed at a gap in state law. Its significance for the Wind River Reservation's tribal casinos lies less in what it would add than in what it would clarify about where gaming in Wyoming is permitted.

The draft bill would ban online sweepstakes games, which the committee described as drawings in which prizes are awarded purely by chance, and would classify operating them as a felony. The committee, created in 2025, also advanced legislation updating fingerprinting requirements for certain individuals regulated by the Wyoming Gaming Commission. Both drafts now move to the full Legislature.

A gap in Wyoming gaming law

Wyoming's current statutes bar online casinos but permit online sports wagering and fantasy sports contests. They are unclear on sweepstakes, which use a dual-currency model in which players buy one virtual currency for entertainment play and receive a second, redeemable currency as a promotional bonus. Operators argue that model keeps them outside the definition of gambling because no purchase is required to play for prizes.

The lack of clarity has produced a regulatory problem as well as a legal one. Regulators have been unsure whether complaints about online sweepstakes should go to the state's Consumer Protection and Antitrust Unit or to the Gaming Commission. A felony prohibition would resolve that question by placing the activity squarely outside the law rather than inside a regulatory category.

Wyoming is not alone. Several states have moved against sweepstakes casinos over the past two years, and tribal governments have been among the most active supporters of those bans. California's 2025 prohibition passed with strong backing from tribal gaming organizations, which argued that sweepstakes platforms offer casino-style games statewide without a compact, a license or consumer protections. What Wind River has at stake

All of Wyoming's tribal casinos are on the Wind River Reservation in Fremont County, which is shared by the Northern Arapaho Tribe and the Eastern Shoshone Tribe. The Northern Arapaho operate Wind River Hotel & Casino in Riverton, the state's largest gaming property, along with two smaller facilities. The Eastern Shoshone operate Shoshone Rose Casino & Hotel near Lander.

The two tribes reached Class III gaming by different routes. The Northern Arapaho pressed the state for years to negotiate a compact, and after the Tenth Circuit ruled for the tribe in 2004, the Interior Department issued secretarial procedures in 2005 under which the tribe conducts Class III gaming. The Eastern Shoshone operate under a tribal-state compact first approved in 2006 and later revised. Our Wyoming tribal gaming market deep dive covers that history and the properties in detail.

Wyoming has no commercial casinos, but the Wind River tribes do not hold a monopoly in practice. The state has authorized historical horse racing terminals, which operate in bars and off-track betting locations across Wyoming, as well as online sports wagering. Online sweepstakes casinos add a third competitor that reaches every phone in the state, offers slot-style games that resemble what tribal properties provide, and operates with no Wyoming license.

The question for Wind River is not whether sweepstakes casinos are a large share of the market today, but whether Wyoming law will treat casino-style games offered outside Indian country as permitted, regulated or prohibited.

Why a felony standard matters

For tribal operators, the form of the state's response matters as much as its existence. A regulatory framework that licensed sweepstakes would effectively recognize a new category of statewide, online casino-style gaming. In most states with tribal gaming, that would raise compact questions, because tribes negotiate their positions on the premise that Class III gaming is limited and that the state will not authorize broad competition outside the compact.

A prohibition takes the opposite approach. By classifying sweepstakes operation as a crime, Wyoming would align online casino-style play with the state's existing bar on online casinos, leaving lawful slot-style gaming limited to tribal properties and to the historical horse racing machines the Legislature has specifically authorized. That outcome would not expand tribal rights, but it would stop the erosion of the market the tribes serve.

The felony standard also matters for enforcement. Sweepstakes operators frequently argue that civil penalties are a cost of doing business and that their legal status is unsettled. A criminal statute gives prosecutors, payment processors and app platforms a clearer basis to act, which is why tribal organizations elsewhere have generally favored criminal bans over civil fines.

The prediction-market parallel

The Wyoming debate runs alongside the larger fight over sports-event contracts, where tribal governments argue that federally registered exchanges are offering sports wagering on Indian lands without a compact. The two issues are legally distinct. Sweepstakes operators claim to fall outside gambling law altogether, while prediction markets claim federal preemption under the Commodity Exchange Act. But the policy concern is the same: products that look and function like regulated gaming reaching customers through a phone, without the licensing, tax and responsible-gaming obligations that tribal and commercial casinos carry.

Indian Gaming Association leaders have framed the sweepstakes campaign as preparation for the prediction market fight, and the coalitions overlap. State attorneys general, tribal leaders and commercial casino operators have found common ground on sweepstakes in a way they have not always found on other issues. What comes next

The committee's sponsorship gives the bill a meaningful head start, but passage is not assured. Sweepstakes operators have lobbied against similar proposals in other states, often arguing for regulation and taxation rather than prohibition, and some Wyoming lawmakers have previously considered regulatory approaches to casual and online gaming. The bill's final form, including whether the felony classification survives and whether it reaches suppliers and promoters as well as operators, will determine how much practical effect it has.

For the Northern Arapaho and Eastern Shoshone, the session offers a low-cost opportunity to shape the outcome. Neither tribe needs new authority from the Legislature. What they need is for state law to keep drawing a clear line around where casino-style gaming can lawfully occur, and the sweepstakes bill is the most direct test of that line Wyoming lawmakers have taken up.
