Tobique First Nation Enacts Prediction Markets Act in New Brunswick

A small New Brunswick nation has built a regulatory structure for prediction markets, raising new questions about First Nations jurisdiction.

By James KingfisherPublished November 10, 20266 min read

Tobique First Nation in New Brunswick has enacted a Prediction Markets Act and created a body called the Tobique Prediction Market Authority, according to trade reporting published November 2. The authority operates separately from the Tobique Gaming Commission, which was established under the Tobique Gaming Act of 2023 and, by the nation's account, rests on its inherent right of self-government. The move makes Tobique, a community of fewer than 3,000 people according to the 2025 census figures cited in coverage, one of the first Indigenous governments in Canada to publish a framework aimed specifically at prediction markets.

Public detail about the framework is limited at this stage. The reporting available describes the structure, not the operating rules, and we have not seen the text of the act. This article therefore sets out what is known, explains the legal context, and identifies the questions that will determine whether the model has practical reach.

What Tobique has created

As reported, the Prediction Markets Act establishes the Tobique Prediction Market Authority as its own regulator. The Tobique Gaming Commission continues to oversee only the permit holders it licenses. Separating the two bodies is a design choice with a clear logic: prediction market contracts are structured differently from slot machines, table games or sportsbooks, and a dedicated authority can develop rules around contract design, settlement and market integrity without reworking the existing gaming permit system.

Coverage notes that the step follows a pattern already seen outside Canada, citing Gibraltar as a jurisdiction that has moved to set up its own prediction market regulation. That reference is the publication's framing, not an official statement from Tobique, and we treat it as context rather than as evidence of how the nation's rules are built.

The jurisdictional question

In Canada, gambling is regulated through a patchwork. The Criminal Code prohibits gambling except where it is conducted and managed by provincial governments or licensed under provincial authority, an arrangement we outline in our explainer on Section 207 and provincial authority over First Nations gaming. First Nations that operate casinos typically do so under agreements with the province, as in Saskatchewan and Manitoba, rather than under an independent legal regime.

Tobique's framing is different. By describing its gaming commission as a sovereign authority operating outside provincial and federal gambling law, the nation asserts a jurisdictional claim that Canadian governments have not accepted in the gaming context. The most prominent comparison is Kahnawake in Quebec, whose gaming commission has licensed online operators for years while remaining in tension with provincial and federal authorities. Our analysis of the Kahnawake Gaming Commission and Canadian iGaming sovereignty traces how that model works and where it is contested.

A prediction markets regime adds a further layer, because the underlying legal status of these products is unsettled even in the United States. Courts and regulators there are still debating whether event contracts are derivatives, gambling products, or both, and how far federal commodities law displaces state gambling law. Tribal governments have taken sharply divergent positions, as our analysis of the fracturing tribal consensus on prediction markets describes. An Indigenous government in Canada entering the field does so against that unsettled backdrop and without the federal framework that the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act provides south of the border.

Why a small nation would do this

The economics of First Nations gaming in Canada have been shifting. Some nations are acquiring existing casinos, others are seeking a share of provincial online revenue, and a few are asserting independent regulatory authority. A regulatory model that can be licensed and exported, rather than tied to a physical casino, is attractive to a community without a large local market. Tobique is not operating from a position of scale; its leverage lies in jurisdictional claims and in the regulatory services it may offer to operators.

Regional context matters too. New Brunswick has seen First Nations activity in gaming development, including the Sitansisk casino project near Fredericton described in our report on the Sitansisk Fredericton casino resort. Tobique's step is a separate initiative, but both illustrate the range of approaches Atlantic First Nations are exploring.

Open questions

Several points will determine whether this framework has real-world effect. The first is operator uptake: whether any platform seeks authorization from the new authority and, if so, which markets it would offer and to which customers. The second is the response from the Province of New Brunswick and from federal authorities, who have not, to our knowledge, commented publicly on the act. The third is the reaction of other First Nations and of intergovernmental bodies that have sought negotiated frameworks rather than independent ones; our comparison of Canadian First Nations gaming revenue frameworks shows how varied those positions are.

Finally, there is the practical matter of consumer protection and enforcement. A regulator with limited resources will need to demonstrate that it can police market integrity, handle disputes and prevent underage or problem participation. Those capacities, more than the text of a statute, are what lenders, partners and eventual regulators elsewhere will examine.
