Where First Nations Gaming Revenue Goes: Evidence From the S-241 Report

Tsuut'ina, Rama, Whitecap Dakota and the FSIN show how casino revenue becomes community infrastructure, and what changes if retention rises.

By Linda CardinalPublished October 16, 20264 min read

Debates over First Nations gaming revenue in Canada tend to focus on percentages: how much a province keeps, how much a host community receives, how much is pooled for others. A less discussed question is what First Nations actually do with the money once it arrives. The Standing Senate Committee on Indigenous Peoples' Fifth Report on Bill S-241, tabled on May 27, 2026, gathers several concrete examples, and when read alongside Saskatchewan's most recent payment figures they show a pattern: gaming revenue is functioning less as a discretionary windfall than as a core source of community infrastructure funding.

The structural question: retain or pool

Under existing provincial agreements, provinces largely set how much revenue First Nations keep, according to the committee's report. In Alberta, the report states, host First Nations give 30 percent of slot revenue to the province and 10 percent to non-host First Nations. The first share funds provincial priorities; the second is a deliberate redistribution to communities that have no casino of their own. Our analysis of Alberta's First Nations Development Fund describes how that pooled money flows to grants.

The committee acknowledged the trade-off directly. Full retention by casino-operating First Nations could reduce resources for other First Nations in the same province, which could be difficult for rural or remote communities. That is not an argument against retention so much as a reminder that any revenue model distributes risk and reward among communities with very different geography and market access. The same tension appears in the comparison of revenue sharing and ownership models that we have published separately.

Four allocation examples from the report

The report offers several illustrations of how revenue is used. Tsuut'ina Nation allocates its remaining revenue to education (41 percent), fire and policing (27 percent), housing (18 percent) and health and social development (14 percent). The breakdown is notable because it assigns roughly two-thirds of the total to education and public safety, categories that in many municipalities are funded by property taxes. For a First Nation without a comparable tax base, casino revenue effectively substitutes for it.

Rama First Nation, by the report's account, runs a revolving loan fund for off-reserve home mortgages. That design is distinctive: rather than spending revenue once, the fund recycles it, and it addresses a long-standing barrier to homeownership for First Nations members who live off reserve and cannot use band-held land as collateral. Casino Rama, according to the report, has directed more than $2 billion in capital to businesses in Orillia and Barrie over 26 years, a figure that illustrates the regional economic role of a First Nation-hosted casino. For history on that property, see our feature on Casino Rama at 30 years.

Whitecap Dakota First Nation's casino, the report says, employs 300 people, with another 125 in operations and governance. Employment on that scale in a single community is an outcome the committee treated as a central benefit. In Saskatchewan, the Federation of Sovereign Indigenous Nations allocates about $22.5 million annually from gaming revenue to gambling-related problems and addictions, which shows revenue being reinvested into mitigating the harms of the activity that generates it. Our coverage of workforce development and the responsible gaming landscape adds context on both themes.

Saskatchewan's numbers show scale

Saskatchewan offers a recent measure of scale. According to the provincial government, as reported in March 2026, the province issued an additional $46.2 million in gaming-related payments to First Nation and Métis organizations, bringing the total for the 2025-26 fiscal year to $211 million. The government attributed the increase to stronger-than-expected results from the Saskatchewan Indian Gaming Authority casinos, SaskGaming, video lottery terminals and online gaming through the province's First Nations-led partnership model. The funds support economic development, cultural programming, education and social services. We analyzed the framework in Saskatchewan's record gaming payments and the operating model in our SIGA expansion report.

What the pattern suggests

Taken together, the examples point to three observations. First, allocation decisions are highly local: Tsuut'ina's percentages, Rama's loan fund and the FSIN's addiction spending reflect different priorities, which is the argument made by supporters of greater First Nation authority under S-241. Second, the revenue is concentrated in essential services, which raises the stakes of any change in revenue share, because a shift of a few percentage points can alter the funding of schools, policing or housing. Third, the geographic divide remains: host communities benefit from employment and capital spending, while non-host communities depend on pooled mechanisms that a more retention-oriented regime might shrink.

None of this resolves the policy debate, and the figures above come from witness testimony and government statements rather than audited, standardized reporting across provinces. That is itself a finding: Canada lacks a uniform public data set comparable to the annual revenue reports published by the National Indian Gaming Commission in the United States.
