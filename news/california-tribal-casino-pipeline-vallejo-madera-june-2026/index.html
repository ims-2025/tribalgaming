Two California Tribal Casino Projects Clear Hurdles in Late June

A municipal agreement near the Bay Area and a 1,500-job hiring drive in the Central Valley signal a buildout that runs well beyond the courtroom fights making headlines.

By Marcus WhitehorsePublished June 30, 20265 min read

Two California tribal casino projects advanced in the closing weeks of June, a reminder that the state's gaming buildout is proceeding on the ground even as higher-profile disputes over cardrooms and off-reservation land move through the courts. In Vallejo, the city council approved a memorandum of understanding with the Scotts Valley Band of Pomo Indians on June 21, advancing a temporary Class II casino toward opening. In the Central Valley, the North Fork Rancheria launched a hiring push for more than 1,500 jobs ahead of a planned fall opening near Madera.

Neither development is a finished casino, and both sit inside contested histories. Taken together, though, they show how California's tribes are pursuing parallel tracks to market entry: a fast, modest first phase that can open while a permanent resort is built, and a fully financed flagship project that aims to anchor a region. For the wider context, our analysis of California's roughly $20 billion tribal gaming economy lays out why the state remains the single largest market in Indian Country.

Vallejo agreement advances a temporary path

The Vallejo memorandum is a municipal milestone rather than a federal one. An MOU between a city and a tribe typically addresses local concerns — traffic, public safety, infrastructure, and services — and signals cooperation, but it does not by itself confer gaming eligibility, which turns on federal land status and the type of gaming proposed. By focusing initially on Class II gaming, which covers bingo-based electronic machines and similar games rather than house-banked table games, a tribe can open sooner because Class II does not require a Class III state compact.

That sequencing has become a recognized strategy. A temporary or phase-one facility lets a tribe begin generating revenue and employment while a larger Class III resort works through compacting and construction. We examined the trade-offs of that approach — speed and cash flow against scale and amenities — in our look at the temporary phase-one casino playbook.

North Fork staffs up for a fall opening

Farther inland, the North Fork Rancheria's project near Madera moved into a visible operational phase with a drive to fill more than 1,500 positions. Hiring at that scale is one of the clearest signals that an opening is near, because casinos must recruit and train dealers, cage staff, food-and-beverage workers, security, and surveillance personnel months before doors open. The project has been one of the most closely watched in the state, in part because of its institutional financing structure, which we covered in our report on North Fork's $725 million Madera financing.

A 1,500-job hiring push is not a press release. It is payroll — the most concrete sign that a casino has moved from approval to opening.

For the surrounding community, a project of that size functions as a regional employer well beyond the gaming floor. Construction trades, local vendors, and service businesses tend to feel the effect first, followed by the longer-term payroll of a full-time workforce. Those ripple effects are a central reason tribal governments pursue gaming in the first place, funding services that range from health care and housing to education and public safety.

A buildout running alongside the courtroom fights

California's gaming headlines in 2026 have been dominated by conflict: litigation over cardroom player-dealer games, intertribal opposition to certain off-reservation projects, and a federal setback that stalled at least one high-profile proposal. Those disputes are real and consequential. But the Vallejo and Madera developments underscore that much of the state's expansion is advancing through ordinary channels — municipal agreements, financing closings, and hiring — rather than through landmark rulings.

That distinction matters for anyone tracking the market. The legal fights set the outer boundaries of where and how tribes can build, but the pace of actual openings is set by financing, construction timelines, and labor. California's tribes have shown they can move on all three fronts at once, and the latest steps in Vallejo and near Madera are evidence of a pipeline that continues to fill. What comes next

It is worth keeping the two projects in proportion. A temporary Class II facility and a fully financed Class III resort are different animals, with different revenue ceilings, amenity profiles, and competitive footprints. The Vallejo project is about getting a foot in the door quickly; the Madera project is about planting a flagship. Lumping them together as a single "expansion" obscures the strategic logic behind each. What they share is momentum at a moment when much of the commentary about California gaming has fixated on conflict rather than construction.

For the Scotts Valley project, the next questions are federal and procedural: confirmation of land status, the scope of gaming permitted, and a firm opening timeline for the temporary facility. For North Fork, the countdown is operational — finishing construction, completing licensing and background checks for new hires, and setting an opening date. Both will be watched not only for their own sakes but as test cases for how quickly California's next wave of tribal casinos can move from paper to payroll.
