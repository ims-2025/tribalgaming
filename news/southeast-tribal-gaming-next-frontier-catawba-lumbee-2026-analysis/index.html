The Southeast is becoming tribal gaming's next frontier

Long a thin market for Indian gaming, the Southeast is drawing new tribal projects, referenda and political fights over who gets to expand.

By James KingfisherPublished July 21, 20266 min read

For most of the modern era of Indian gaming, the American Southeast has been a thin market. Outside Florida, where the Seminole Tribe built one of the country's most powerful gaming operations, and western North Carolina, where the Eastern Band of Cherokee Indians ran the region's marquee mountain casinos, tribal gaming in the Southeast was the exception rather than the rule. That is changing. A cluster of developments in 2026 — a major new opening, fresh expansion ambitions, a tribal referendum and renewed legislative debate — suggests the Southeast is becoming tribal gaming's next frontier.

The clearest signal came from the Catawba Nation, which opened the introductory phase of its $1.25 billion Two Kings Casino near Charlotte, North Carolina, in July and immediately declared it was not finished. At the ribbon-cutting, tribal leadership said the nation intends to build two additional casinos in the state at locations not yet disclosed, with the full Two Kings resort slated for completion in 2027. The details of that flagship project are covered in our report on the Two Kings permanent resort.

Why the Southeast, and why now

Three forces are converging. The first is demographics and demand: the Southeast's population has grown rapidly, and large metro areas such as Charlotte sit far from established gaming destinations, leaving underserved catchment areas that a well-placed casino can capture. The second is precedent — the success of the Seminole operation in Florida, profiled in our Seminole Tribe of Florida profile, demonstrated that Southeastern markets can support gaming at scale and gave other tribes a template to point to. The third is a live policy debate over gambling expansion in states that have historically resisted it, which creates openings for tribal operators positioned to move first.

North Carolina illustrates the pattern. Beyond Catawba's ambitions, the Lumbee Tribe advanced a gaming question to its own citizens, moving a proposed constitutional-style amendment on gaming toward a tribal vote — a procedural step that reflects how tribes are building the internal legal architecture needed before any casino can open. Whether or not any single project materializes, the volume of activity marks a decisive shift from a region where tribal gaming was once an afterthought.

The frictions ahead

Expansion into new territory rarely comes without conflict, and the Southeast will be no different. The most predictable flashpoint is intertribal competition. When one tribe moves to open a casino outside its historic territory — particularly on newly acquired land — neighboring tribes often object that the project encroaches on their markets or stretches the land-into-trust process beyond its intent. That tension is a recurring feature of American gaming expansion, examined in our analysis of intertribal opposition to off-reservation casinos, and the Southeast's newer tribal footprints make it especially prone to these disputes.

The Southeast's opportunity is real, but so is its complexity: every promising new market also invites competition from neighboring tribes and scrutiny of how the underlying land was placed into trust.

The second friction is legal. Building a casino in a new location almost always requires putting land into trust and clearing the eligibility hurdles that govern gaming on after-acquired land. Those processes are slow, litigation-prone and sensitive to shifts in federal policy — the mechanics are laid out in our legal guide to land-into-trust and gaming eligibility. A tribe can announce a project, secure financing and even break ground, only to spend years defending the land's status in court. The Catawba experience, which involved its own protracted federal and legal path before Two Kings could open, is a case study in how long the road can be.

What it means for the map

If even a fraction of the announced Southeastern projects reach completion, the geographic center of gravity in tribal gaming will shift. For decades the industry's growth story was concentrated in California, Oklahoma, the upper Midwest and the Northeast corridor. A durable Southeastern expansion would add a fourth pillar — one anchored by fast-growing metros and, in several states, comparatively limited existing competition.

For existing Southeastern operators, the shift is double-edged. New entrants promise to grow the region's overall gaming footprint and normalize expansion in states that once resisted it, but they also introduce competition into catchment areas that established tribes have long treated as their own. A metro like Charlotte can support a large casino precisely because it has been underserved; the moment two or three projects target the same corridor, the economics change for everyone. That is why the announcements matter even before ground is broken — they reset the strategic calculus of tribes already operating in or near the market, prompting decisions about defensive expansion, marketing and their own compact positioning.

The caveats are significant. Announcements outrun openings, land-into-trust decisions can stall for years, and political winds in conservative Southeastern legislatures can turn against expansion as quickly as they turn for it. But the direction is unmistakable. Between Catawba's multi-casino ambitions, the Lumbee referendum and renewed debate in neighboring states, the Southeast has moved from the periphery of tribal gaming to one of its most closely watched growth stories — a frontier where the next decade of Indian gaming will be partly written.
