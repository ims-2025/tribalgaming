North Fork Casino Opens October 28 as Management Contract Ruling Looms

A $725 million Madera County casino is days from opening, and a pending federal ruling could still reach the contract that governs how it is run.

By Elena RuizPublished October 22, 20264 min read

The North Fork Rancheria of Mono Indians and its management partner, Station Casinos, are preparing to open a $725 million casino in Madera County, California, on October 28, 2026. A federal lawsuit filed by a neighboring tribe has not yet been resolved, and a ruling is expected at any time. The North Fork casino opening is therefore arriving with an unusual question attached: what happens if a court rules on the management contract after the doors are already open?

The project is large by any measure. Reporting describes a 100,000-square-foot gaming floor with more than 2,000 slot and video poker machines and 40 table games, inside a facility of roughly 264,000 square feet. Planning began in 2003, construction started in 2024, and the developers project more than 1,500 jobs. Readers following the property's earlier milestones can find them in our earlier coverage of the Madera opening.

What the Picayune lawsuit actually challenges

The case, filed on June 5, is captioned Picayune Rancheria of the Chukchansi Indians v. the Chair of the National Indian Gaming Commission. It is pending before U.S. District Judge Kirk E. Sherriff. Defendants named in the action include North Fork, Red Rock Resorts, Station Casinos and SC Madera Management.

The target of the suit is narrower than the casino itself. Picayune challenges the National Indian Gaming Commission's approval of a 2024 management contract between North Fork and Station Casinos, and it asks the court to annul that approval. Its legal theory is that gaming on land acquired after 1988 for an off-reservation site requires a two-part determination, one that depends on approval by both the federal government and the state. Picayune's position is that this step was required here. The defendants' position is the subject of the litigation; this article does not attempt to predict how the court will weigh the competing readings of the statute.

That framing matters because management contracts are a distinct regulatory object. Under the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act, a contract that gives an outside party responsibility for managing a tribal gaming operation needs federal approval, and the Commission's approval rests on findings about the contract and the land. Our explainer on IGRA management contracts describes what that review covers, and our earlier report on the Picayune suit traces how the dispute reached this point.

Why timing makes this case unusual

Press accounts of the dispute note that a ruling in Picayune's favor could block gambling operations even after the building has opened. That possibility is what separates this situation from a typical pre-opening dispute. A casino that has hired thousands of workers, installed gaming equipment and committed to a debt schedule is far harder to pause than one still under construction, and every party understands that.

It also explains why the calendar is being watched so closely. An opening date set weeks in advance cannot easily adjust to a ruling whose date nobody controls. If the court rules before October 28, the question will be whether any relief it grants affects the opening itself or only the contract approval. If the court rules afterward, the question becomes whether and how operations could be altered while the facility is running. Neither scenario is certain, and the court has not signaled which it prefers.

For California's tribal gaming market the stakes extend beyond one property. The state's compact framework, discussed in our California state hub, concentrates revenue in a relatively small number of large operators, and a new casino of this scale in the Central Valley changes competitive dynamics for existing facilities in the region. The litigation is, in part, a window into how rival tribes use federal court to contest those dynamics.

What to watch next

Three developments will clarify the picture. The first is the district court's ruling on the claims against the Commission's approval, which could arrive before or after the opening. The second is any request for interim relief, since a motion seeking to halt operations would be decided on a different standard than the underlying merits; our explainer on the preliminary injunction standard outlines that test. The third is whether any party seeks appellate review quickly, which would move the question to the Ninth Circuit.

Until a ruling issues, the facts on the ground are straightforward. A major new tribal casino is scheduled to open, a competitor tribe has asked a federal court to unwind the federal approval of its management contract, and the court has not yet decided. Those facts can change within hours, and this article reflects reporting available on October 22, 2026. Operators, lenders and regulators across the state will be reading the docket as closely as the opening schedule.
