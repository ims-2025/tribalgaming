Little Creek Casino Resort Adds SCORE! Arcade and Eats & Sweets Nov. 27

The Squaxin Island Tribe's Shelton resort is betting that an arcade and a casual eatery will bring in guests the casino floor cannot.

By Linda CardinalPublished November 20, 20264 min read

Little Creek Casino Resort, the Squaxin Island Tribe's resort near Shelton, Washington, will open two new non-gaming venues on Friday, November 27, the day after Thanksgiving. SCORE! Arcade is an interactive gaming and prize-redemption space set apart from the casino floor. Eats & Sweets is a casual food-and-beverage outlet built to serve the new entertainment area. The resort announced the additions in mid-November, describing the pair as a destination for families, social groups and multi-generational visitors.

The project is modest by the standards of the billion-dollar resort builds that have dominated tribal gaming news this year. It is also a clear example of where many regional tribal properties are putting their capital: into amenities that widen the customer base beyond adult gamblers, at a time when slot growth in mature markets has slowed.

What is opening at Little Creek Casino Resort

According to the resort's announcement, SCORE! Arcade will offer interactive games and prize-based entertainment in a dedicated space outside the gaming floor. Eats & Sweets is a casual concept meant to feed arcade guests and anyone looking for a quicker, lower-cost option than the resort's existing restaurants.

The resort has not published square footage, an investment figure, hours, capacity or staffing numbers for either venue, and TribalGaming.com has not seen those figures elsewhere. The timing is deliberate. Opening on Black Friday puts both venues in front of the Thanksgiving travel crowd and the holiday weeks that follow, when families are more likely to be looking for something to do together.

The additions join a resort that already includes a hotel, several restaurants, an event program and golf at the tribe's Salish Cliffs Golf Club. Little Creek sits off U.S. Highway 101 in Mason County, within driving distance of Olympia and the southern Puget Sound. That location makes day-trippers and regional overnight guests its main market, so things to do beyond the casino floor count for more than they would at a destination resort.

Why non-gaming space matters for a regional tribal casino

For a property of Little Creek's size, the reasoning behind an arcade is not complicated. The casino floor is limited to adults, and in a family group the adults who want to gamble often bring people who cannot. A space where younger guests and non-gamblers can spend an afternoon makes the whole trip more likely, and it can stretch a visit from a few hours into a meal and an evening.

That reasoning has run through tribal resort planning across the country this year. Our analysis of non-gaming amenities as a diversification strategy found tribes putting money into hotels, entertainment venues, golf and family attractions. The goal is income that holds up when gaming revenue flattens. The larger version of that trend shows up in arenas and showrooms, which we covered in our look at the tribal casino entertainment venue buildout.

An arcade is a smaller bet. It needs less space than a showroom, has no booking risk, and can draw repeat local visits instead of relying on one-off events. Food and beverage adds to the per-visit spend. For a resort that already has lodging and golf, the arcade fills a gap for families with children that the existing amenities do not cover.

For regional properties, the question is increasingly not how to add more machines, but how to give every member of a visiting group a reason to come.

Prize-redemption arcades also fall into a different regulatory category from the casino floor. Casino gaming at Little Creek is conducted under the tribe's Class III compact with the State of Washington and regulated by the tribe's gaming agency. An arcade's amusement and redemption games are generally treated as entertainment, not gambling, though the exact classification depends on game design and on how the tribe's own ordinances and agreements define them. Operators usually keep such spaces physically separate from gaming areas, as Little Creek has described doing here.

The Squaxin Island Tribe's position in Washington

The opening comes in a busy year for the Squaxin Island Tribe on the regulatory side. Its seventh compact amendment, a restatement of its Washington Class III compact that folds the 1993 original and six later amendments into one document, took effect on September 29 by operation of law after the Interior Department's 45-day review period passed without action, as we reported in our coverage of the Federal Register notice. Those changes are part of a broader wave of Washington compact amendments moving through the state this year.

Washington's tribal gaming market is one of the most competitive in the country, with more than two dozen tribes running casinos. The large Puget Sound resorts compete on scale. Properties like Little Creek have to make their case through location, convenience and the full experience of a visit. The Washington state hub in our directory lists the state's tribal operators and properties.

The new venues are unlikely to show up as a line item in any public revenue figure. Like most tribal enterprises, the Squaxin Island Tribe does not publish property-level financials. How the venues perform will show mainly in things the tribe tracks privately: hotel occupancy on holiday weekends, food-and-beverage covers, and repeat visits from families who might otherwise have gone elsewhere.

What to watch

The first test comes over the Thanksgiving weekend and the December holidays. Beyond that, two questions matter for the wider market. The first is whether Little Creek treats the arcade as a standalone addition or as the first step in a larger family-entertainment area. The second is whether similar regional properties in Washington and Oregon follow with comparable investments. Tribes in smaller markets have increasingly treated non-gaming space as part of how they compete, not an extra on the side.
