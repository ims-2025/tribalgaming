Tribal Casino Intergovernmental Agreements: How Host Communities Are Paid

Cooperative aid agreements, mitigation funds and compact provisions are the tools tribes and local governments use to settle who pays for a casino's public costs.

By Daniel YazziePublished October 19, 20265 min read

When a tribe proposes a casino, the neighbors usually ask the same question: who pays for the extra police calls, road wear, water demand and emergency services? Because land held in trust by the United States is generally exempt from state and local property taxes and zoning, local governments cannot answer that question through the tools they use for other developments. Tribal casino intergovernmental agreements fill the gap. This explainer describes what they are, where they come from and how they work in practice.

Why the issue arises

Tribal governments are sovereign, and land taken into trust for a tribe is removed from the local tax rolls and from most local land-use authority. A county or city that hosts a casino still provides services to the people who visit it: sheriff and fire response, road maintenance, sewer and water connections, and sometimes schools and social services. If nothing is agreed in advance, the tribe may fund its own police, fire and utility services, but neighboring jurisdictions can still bear spillover costs.

The law does not require a tribe to sign a particular agreement with a county or city, but several federal and state processes create strong incentives to do so. Understanding those incentives is the key to understanding why such agreements are so common.

Where agreements come from

The first source is the federal land process. A tribe that wants land placed in trust must apply to the Bureau of Indian Affairs, which notifies state and local governments and invites comment on the effects on tax revenues and on regulatory jurisdiction and special assessments. For gaming on land acquired after October 17, 1988, the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act generally prohibits gaming unless an exception applies. One exception, the two-part determination, requires the Secretary of the Interior to consult with state and local officials, including officials of nearby tribes, and to find that gaming would not be detrimental to the surrounding community. The governor of the state must then concur. Our explainer on governor concurrence covers that sequence. An environmental review under the National Environmental Policy Act also analyzes effects on traffic, public services and neighboring communities, as described in our guide to NEPA review for tribal casinos.

Because the Secretary and the governor must weigh local concerns, a signed agreement with the host county or city is persuasive evidence that the project is not detrimental. Tribes therefore often negotiate before they file.

The second source is the tribal-state compact. For Class III gaming, such as slot machines and table games, IGRA allows compacts to address the application of state and tribal laws, the allocation of criminal and civil jurisdiction, and assessments by the state to defray regulatory costs. Many compacts also contain provisions that direct money or services to local governments, either through a fund or through a requirement that the tribe enter agreements with affected jurisdictions. The Class III compact process is outlined in our Legal Guide.

Common types of agreements

Intergovernmental agreements vary, but most fall into a handful of categories. Law enforcement agreements specify which agency responds to incidents at the casino and how costs are allocated, often through cross-deputization or mutual aid. Fire and emergency medical agreements provide for response by a neighboring department, sometimes in exchange for a service fee or equipment. Infrastructure agreements cover road improvements, traffic signals, water and sewer connections and utility service, frequently with the tribe paying for construction that its project makes necessary. Payments in lieu of taxes, sometimes called service payments, compensate local governments for lost property tax revenue or for services the tribe does not provide itself. Finally, mitigation funds pool contributions that local governments can use for impacts identified in an environmental review.

California offers the most developed example of the last category. Its modern compacts require tribes to contribute to mitigation for off-reservation impacts, and our explainer on California local impact mitigation funds details how those funds work. Other states take different approaches; some direct a share of compact payments to local revenue-sharing arrangements. For a state-level overview, see the California state hub.

Cooperative aid agreements in practice

A recent example comes from Minnesota. When the White Earth Nation briefed Clay County commissioners on its planned fee-to-trust application for a site near Moorhead, it proposed a cooperative aid agreement with the county and the city covering public safety, roads and utilities. That is the same menu of services described above, and it was offered before any application was filed, a sequence designed to answer local objections early. Details of that briefing are in our report on the White Earth trust application. No agreement has been announced.

Strengths and limits

These agreements offer clear benefits. They convert general concerns into specific, enforceable commitments; they give the host community a stake in the project's success; and they reduce the risk of litigation that can delay a casino for years. For tribes, they can accelerate federal and state approvals and build the working relationships that a long-term operation requires.

They also have limits. Because tribes are sovereign, enforcement depends on the terms the parties negotiate, including whether the tribe waives sovereign immunity for the specific purpose of enforcing the agreement. A limited waiver is common, but it is a negotiated provision rather than a default; our analysis of limited sovereign immunity waivers explains the concept. Agreements can also be criticized for being negotiated under pressure, with local governments agreeing to terms before they understand a project's full effects. Finally, an agreement does not bind other neighbors, such as nearby tribes or competing jurisdictions, who may still object during federal review.

How to read an announcement

When a tribe announces an agreement with a county or city, three questions help assess it. What services and payments does it cover, and for how long? Does it include an enforceable dispute mechanism? And does it precede or follow the federal decision? An agreement signed before a filing is a sign of strategy; one signed after a denial or lawsuit is closer to a settlement. Either way, intergovernmental agreements have become a standard part of the casino development toolkit, and understanding them is essential to following how tribal projects move from proposal to opening.
