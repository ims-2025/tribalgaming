What Is a TERO? Tribal Employment Rights and Casino Hiring Explained

The ordinance that decides who gets hired — and who builds the next expansion — at tribal casinos.

By Linda CardinalPublished July 19, 20266 min read

Behind every tribal casino's workforce sits a legal instrument that rarely makes headlines but profoundly shapes who gets hired and who gets the contract to build the next expansion: the Tribal Employment Rights Ordinance, universally known by its acronym, TERO. For tribes, TERO is an exercise of governmental authority over economic activity on their lands. For outside contractors and job seekers, it is the rulebook that governs Indian preference in hiring, contracting, and workforce development at tribal enterprises. Anyone trying to understand tribal casino employment needs to understand TERO first.

A Tribal Employment Rights Ordinance is a law enacted by a tribal government requiring that employers operating on the tribe's lands give preference to qualified Native American workers — and, in many cases, to tribally owned businesses when awarding contracts. TERO ordinances are typically administered by a dedicated TERO office or commission that issues rules, collects fees, monitors compliance, and resolves disputes. The framework grew out of the broader push for tribal self-determination and rests on the same foundation of sovereignty that supports gaming itself, a foundation explained in our Legal Guide.

How TERO works in practice

At a tribal casino, TERO touches employment at multiple levels. In hiring, it establishes a preference hierarchy — commonly favoring members of the host tribe first, then members of other federally recognized tribes, then the general labor pool — applied to qualified candidates. The word qualified matters: TERO preference operates among applicants who meet the position's legitimate requirements, rather than mandating hiring regardless of ability. In contracting, TERO often requires that construction and service contracts for casino projects give preference to Native-owned firms, and that non-Native contractors working on tribal land meet Indian-hiring benchmarks for their crews.

TERO is not a diversity program a tribe chooses to adopt; it is an exercise of governmental authority over economic activity on tribal land, grounded in the same sovereignty that authorizes gaming.

Enforcement gives the ordinance teeth. A TERO commission can require employers to submit workforce plans, report their hiring by tribal status, and demonstrate good-faith recruitment through the tribe's own job-referral system before turning to outside labor. Where an employer falls short, remedies can include compliance orders, fines, or in serious cases suspension of the right to work on tribal land. Because these powers flow from tribal law rather than state authority, they apply to contractors precisely because those contractors have chosen to do business on the reservation.

Most TERO offices fund themselves through a fee, commonly a small percentage of the value of contracts performed on tribal land. Those fees support workforce training, job placement, skills programs, and the administrative machinery of compliance monitoring. In this way TERO does more than allocate existing jobs; it builds the tribal labor pool over time, developing the trained workforce that a casino and its construction projects require. For a large resort expansion, TERO can direct millions of dollars in contracting value and hundreds of jobs toward tribal members and Native-owned businesses.

Where TERO meets federal and state law

TERO sits at a complex intersection of legal authorities. Indian preference in employment is expressly permitted under federal civil-rights law, which contains carve-outs recognizing the political — not merely racial — status of tribal membership. That distinction, rooted in the government-to-government relationship between tribes and the United States, is what allows tribal preference to coexist with anti-discrimination statutes that would otherwise prohibit it. But the boundaries are actively contested, particularly where tribal labor authority brushes against federal agencies asserting jurisdiction over tribal enterprises, a tension examined in our analysis of the Tribal Labor Sovereignty Act and NLRB jurisdiction.

TERO also operates alongside, but separately from, the licensing regime that governs casino employees. Every worker in a key gaming position must clear the background-check and licensing process required under gaming regulation, a distinct requirement covered in our employee licensing explainer. A candidate benefiting from TERO preference in hiring must still satisfy those regulatory standards; the two systems answer different questions — one about preference, the other about suitability.

Why TERO matters to the gaming economy

TERO is one of the primary mechanisms by which gaming revenue translates into tribal economic self-sufficiency beyond the per-capita check. By channeling jobs and contracts toward tribal members and Native-owned enterprises, TERO ensures that the labor and construction spending generated by a casino circulates within the tribal community rather than flowing entirely to outside firms and workers. That multiplier effect — gaming dollars funding training, employment, and Native business capacity — is central to the economic-development case for tribal gaming, and it helps explain why labor questions loom large in an industry facing the staffing pressures now common across Indian Country.

For contractors, understanding TERO is a practical necessity: bidding on a tribal casino project without accounting for TERO fees and hiring requirements is a recipe for a noncompliant bid. For workers, TERO can be the difference between watching a casino rise on tribal land and building a career in it. And for anyone studying tribal gaming as an engine of self-determination, TERO is where the abstract principle of sovereignty becomes a paycheck, a contract, and a trained workforce — the concrete machinery through which a tribe governs its own economy.
